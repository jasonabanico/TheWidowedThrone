## Chapter 13: The Spring Champion

*Let not him that girdeth on his harness boast himself as he that putteth it off.*

*The First Book of Kings*

---

They passed the City on the way east, and did not stop.

The *Theotokos* came up the Marmara on a grey morning in the first week of the new year with her hold full of Egyptian grain, and Stephanos stood at the rail and watched the Land Walls rise out of the mist: the long grey line of them marching from the sea to the Golden Horn, the towers, the gates, the raw new stone at Charisios where he had been laying courses the morning Niketas nearly died. He could see the Fool's Tower. He could see the red tile roof on the top storey, where the Abba sat by his window.

Fotios put in at the Harbour gate long enough to unload the grain and take on water. The Eparch's harbourmaster came down to the quay and wept when he saw the ship, and asked for the Hornbearer, and was told the Hornbearer was not aboard. Stephanos stayed below. He wrote a letter by the light of a hanging lamp, with Theophanes's pen, and gave it to Fotios to carry to the Tower.

He wrote about the Waters and the Gardens. He wrote about the Goldhand in his altar cloth and the Lady Eudokia in the rot. He copied out the two Words of Symeon. He wrote about Petros diving into the flood, and carrying the children up the golden stair. He wrote that he was going east to the Heights, because there would be a third word there, and because Anak son of Gathos would be calling for a champion in the spring.

At the end he wrote: *Tell the Domestikos I will answer for my oath when I come home. Tell him I am sorry. I am not sorry I went.*

He did not write about the veil. He did not write about what Amma Thekla had said. He folded the letter and sealed it and gave it to Fotios, and the *Theotokos* sailed on across the strait to Nikomedia with the band aboard, and the City sank into the mist behind them.

"You could have gone ashore," said Kale, at the rail.

"I know."

"You didn't want to face the Domestikos."

"I didn't want him to stop me," said Stephanos. That was true. It was not all of the truth. He did not tell her the rest of it, which was that he had wanted to go ashore more than he had wanted anything in months, to climb the stair to the top room of the Tower and kneel by the Abba's stool and tell him everything; and that he had been afraid the Abba would listen to all of it, and then ask him the Question, and he would not know what to say.

---

They rode east across Anatolia in the dead of winter.

It took them six weeks. Kale led. She knew the old military roads and the places where they had crumbled, and the villages where an Akrites could ask for bread and a barn to sleep in and not be refused. The Goldhand had given them gold before they left Paros, a great deal of it, and Stephanos had refused it and then accepted a tenth of it for horses and food when Amma Thekla pointed out, tartly, that the vow of poverty had not been meant to make old women walk to Cappadocia in the snow. They bought seven horses and a mule at Nikomedia. Sergios would not ride. He walked the whole way, barefoot in the snow, and his feet did not freeze, and nobody could explain it.

The land grew higher and colder and emptier as they went. The villages grew further apart. The churches in them were shut, or burned, or turned into barns. In the high country past Ankyra, they began to see the Blight of the Heights.

It came up out of the ground. That was the first thing. Iron. Veins of it, dark and rusty, splitting the rocks along the roadside the way roots split paving stones, spreading in branching lines across the hillsides like the black veins on the back of an old man's hand. Then the rain came, and it was red. It fell out of a low grey sky and stained the snow the colour of dried blood, and it smelled of metal, and where it pooled in the ruts of the road it left a crust of rust behind.

Then, in a village called Zamanti, they saw the swords.

The village was deserted. Its people had gone, Kale said, two winters ago, fled into the underground cities to the south. Its fields lay fallow under the red-stained snow. And in the fields, in rows, as if they had been planted, swords were growing.

They came up out of the frozen earth point-first, like green shoots. Some of them were no longer than a finger. Some were as long as a man's arm, and had hilts, and crossguards. The oldest, at the edges of the fields, stood waist-high, rusty and pitted, leaning at angles, like wheat after a storm.

Petros rode out into the field and pulled one up. It came out of the ground with a tearing sound, roots and all. He held it up. The blade was sharp. The hilt was wrapped with something that looked like leather and was not.

"Put it back," said Amma Thekla.

"It's a good sword," said Petros. He weighed it in his hand. "It's better than good. Look at the edge on it."

"It grew from the Prince of the Heights. Every blow you strike with it, you'll strike for him. Put it back."

Petros looked at the old woman. Since Thessalonike, he had not argued with her once. He had fasted every Friday since, without being told, and he had not touched wine. He looked at the sword a moment longer. Then he threw it down in the snow, hard, point-first, and it stuck there quivering.

He did not say anything. But he rode on ahead of the others for the rest of the day, alone, and at night by the fire he sat apart and sharpened his own knife on a stone for a long time, over and over, until the edge was like a razor.

---

They came to Göreme on the first day of Lent.

Stephanos had heard of the cave cities of Cappadocia all his life. His grandmother had come from there. She had told him stories of a valley where the rock grew up into towers, and the towers were hollow, and people lived inside them like bees in a comb. He had thought, as a child, that it was a fairy tale.

It was not. The valley of Göreme was the strangest place he had ever seen. The rock was soft and pale, the colour of bread crust, and the wind and the rain had carved it over ten thousand years into cones and spires and chimneys and pillars, hundreds of them, standing in the valley like a forest of stone. Some were small. Some were as tall as church towers. And every one of them was hollow. Doors and windows were cut into them at every level. Stairs wound up their sides. Smoke rose from holes in their tops. And in the flanks of the valley, where the soft rock rose in cliffs, there were more doors, hundreds of them, row above row, the dark mouths of the cave churches and cave houses and cave stables where the Christians of the Heights had lived for five hundred years.

"The underground cities go down from here," said Kale. "Eight levels. Ten. Nobody's sure how deep. When Anak's raiders come, the whole valley goes down, and rolls stones across the doors, and waits." She looked up at the cliffs. "They've been waiting a long time."

Above the valley, far off to the east, a mountain stood up against the sky.

It was vast and white and alone, a single great cone of snow rising out of the brown plain, so high that its peak was lost in cloud. Stephanos had seen mountains before. He had seen the Taurus and the Antitaurus and the Armenian ranges. He had never seen anything like this. It did not look like a mountain. It looked like something that had been placed there.

"Argaeus," said Kale. "Where Gathos is buried. Anak has his hall on the southern face, in the old barrow." She did not look at the mountain for long. "Nobody from the valley goes there. Not since the first champion."

---

Count Hagop met them at the door of Saint George of the Rock.

He was an Armenian, like Stephanos, a big grizzled man with a broken nose and a beard like a bear's pelt, and he had been a cataphract of Taron once, before he took the purple. He and Stephanos had ridden together under the red eagle for four years. When Hagop saw him coming up the path from the valley floor, the Count stood very still in the doorway of the cave monastery for a long moment. Then he came down the path at a run and threw his arms around him.

"Stephanos! Stephanos, you mad Taronite dog! They wrote from the City that you'd gone to Thrace and never come back! The Domestikos has named you oathbreaker in chapter! He's put it in the register!" Hagop held him at arm's length and looked at him, and his face was shining. "What are you doing in the Heights?"

"Looking for a champion," said Stephanos.

Hagop's face changed. The shine went out of it.

"Come inside," he said. "You'd better see the boy."

---

Saint George of the Rock was a monastery cut into the cliff above the valley floor: a chapel, a refectory, a stable, cells for twelve knights, all carved out of the living rock, with walls painted in faded red and ochre with pictures of the warrior saints. Only five knights lived there now. The rest had died on Argaeus, in the spring, one at a time.

"Eleven years ago, Anak came down from the mountain for the first time," Hagop said. They were sitting in the refectory, a long low cave with a table cut from the rock, and a fire burning at one end. "He burned half the valley. Then he stood in the middle of it, in the ashes, and he said: *I am the grandson of Gathos, whom the shepherd killed. Every spring, at the equinox, send me a champion to fight me in my grandfather's tomb. If your champion wins, I will leave you in peace for a year. If he loses, I will take your children.*" Hagop turned his cup in his big scarred hands. "We sent a champion the first spring. A Hornbearer. Brother Tigran, from this house. He didn't come back. Anak took forty children that year. We sent another champion the second spring. And the third. Every year." He drank. "Every year, the best man in the valley goes up the mountain, and doesn't come back. And every year, Anak takes the children anyway. Fewer, now. The families hide them underground. But he finds some."

"And this year?" said Stephanos.

"This year there's nobody left to send." Hagop put down the cup. "My knights are old men or boys. The valley's best fighters are dead. So the elders have chosen." He looked up. "They've chosen a shepherd boy."

He turned his head and called something in Armenian toward the door. A moment later, a boy came in.

He was about fourteen, small for his age, thin and brown and sharp-faced, with black hair cut short and the too-large hands of a boy who has not finished growing. He wore a shepherd's sheepskin coat and carried a sling looped around his wrist. He stood in the doorway and looked at the strangers with dark, wary eyes.

"This is Mikros," said Hagop. "The elders chose him by lot. His father went up the mountain four years ago. His name was drawn first." The Count's voice was rough. "He came to me a month ago and asked me to teach him the sling. I've been trying. My hands are no good for it anymore." He held up his big scarred right hand; two of the fingers were stiff and crooked. "I thought, if a shepherd killed Gathos with a sling, maybe..."

"Maybe a shepherd could kill Anak with one," said Stephanos.

"It's a foolish thought."

"It's the thought the elders had." Stephanos looked at the boy. The boy looked back at him without blinking. "It's not foolish."

"I'll do it."

They all turned. Petros had stood up from the table.

He was a head taller than anyone in the room. His dark curly hair almost brushed the rock of the ceiling. He looked down at the thin shepherd boy in the doorway, and then at Hagop, and his face was flushed and set.

"I'll be your champion," Petros said. "I'll go up the mountain. Let the boy stay home."

"Petros," said Dora quietly.

"No, Dora. Listen." Petros turned to the Count. "I'm eighteen. I'm the strongest man in any village I've ever lived in. I've fought a wolf with a stick, and I broke the Goldhand's chains with my bare hands, and I swam the flood in the Mint four times. I'm the great-grandson of the lame lord of Arkadiopolis. The Shepherd's blood is in me." He drew himself up. "The Shepherd killed Gathos. It's only right his heir should kill Gathos's grandson. Isn't it?"

Hagop stared at him. Then he looked at Stephanos.

"Is it true?" said the Count. "The Shepherd's blood?"

Stephanos opened his mouth.

He saw Amma Thekla at the end of the table, weaving. She did not look up. He saw Kale by the fire, watching him. He saw Dora, very still, with the veil of the Myrrh-bearer over her dark hair.

"It's true," he said.

"God be praised," said Hagop, and crossed himself. "God be praised. After eleven years."

The shepherd boy in the doorway said nothing. He looked at Petros for a long moment with his dark, wary eyes, and then he turned and went out.

---

The Concord came to Göreme in the second week of Lent.

They came up the valley road with an escort of twenty soldiers in good mail and red cloaks, marching in step, and a closed carriage drawn by four mules, and behind the carriage six wagons, empty. The soldiers halted in the square below the great rock chimney that served the valley as a church. A man got out of the carriage.

He was a Scribe of the Treasury, in grey silk with a silver chain of office. He was young and pale and pleasant, and he carried a leather folder, and he stood in the square in the thin spring sunshine and read from a parchment in a clear, courteous voice, while the people of Göreme came out of their caves and their chimneys and stood in silence on the rock around him.

"The Concord of the Tower," he read, "moved by the suffering of the Christians of Cappadocia under the tyranny of the giant Anak, offers the valley of Göreme its protection. The Iron Host will garrison the valley with two hundred men. Anak will be forbidden to come down from Argaeus. No more champions will be required of you. No more children will be taken." He looked up and smiled. "In return, the Concord asks only a modest tax, to pay for the garrison. We ask for your icons."

A murmur went through the crowd.

"Not all of them," said the Scribe. "Only the old ones. The painted boards, from the cave churches. They are of great interest to the Concord's scholars, who study the art of the ancient empire. They will be preserved in the libraries of Nebrodia, and cared for, and admired. You may paint new ones, of course. As many as you like." He closed his folder. "The elders have until the equinox to decide. After the equinox, I understand, you will have other things to think about."

He bowed, and got back into his carriage, and the soldiers made camp in the square.

That night, the elders of Göreme met in the rock church, and Stephanos went to listen.

There were eleven of them, old men and old women in sheepskin and black wool, sitting on the stone bench that ran around the wall of the church under the painted saints. Father Basil, the valley's priest, sat in the middle. He was ancient and nearly blind. Hagop sat beside him. Lazaros sat at the end of the bench, with his scarred hands clenched on his knees.

"They'll give the icons to the Bowed," Lazaros was saying. His voice was shaking. "They won't put them in any library. The Treasury sells them. I know. Theophanes has seen the price list. The Bowed buy them to defile in their rites. They'll burn them, or worse."

"We know," said one of the elders, an old woman with a face like a walnut. "We're not fools, painter."

"Then how can you even think of..."

"Because they'll stop Anak," said the old woman. "Two hundred men. Iron Host. They've beaten giants before; everyone knows it. They'll stop him, and we'll stop sending our sons up that mountain, and our grandchildren will sleep in their own beds for the first time in eleven years." Her voice cracked. "I sent my son. Four years ago. Mikros's father. My son. I want my grandson to live. Is that such a sin? For boards? For paint?"

Lazaros had no answer. He looked at his hands.

"We will decide after the equinox," said Father Basil. His voice was very soft. "If the champion comes back, we will not need the Concord. If he doesn't..." He spread his thin hands. "If he doesn't, God forgive us, I think we will give them the boards."

---

Petros trained every day.

He trained in the yard of Saint George of the Rock with Hagop and the five old knights, with sword and mace and axe, from dawn until the light went. He was very good. He was better than good. He had a shepherd's strength and a wrestler's balance and a speed that none of them could match, and within a week he could put Hagop on his back in the dust, and within two he could fight three of the old knights at once and win.

"He's a natural," Hagop said, watching. "I've never seen anything like it. Put him in armour and put a lance in his hand, and he'll be the best cataphract in the empire in five years."

"Teach him the sling," said Stephanos.

Hagop looked at him.

"The sling," said Stephanos. "Gathos died to a sling. Anak is his grandson. Giants fall to small things. Everyone knows it."

"Everyone knows it," Hagop agreed. "But I've fought giants. So have you. Did you ever kill one with a sling?"

Stephanos thought of the ford at Arkadiopolis, and the stone going into the lead giant's forehead, and the giant falling sideways into the river. He did not say anything.

Petros would not train with the sling. He tried it once, in the yard, and threw a stone that went wild and struck the chapel door, and the old knights laughed. Petros laughed too. Then he threw the sling down.

"It's a child's weapon," he said. "It's for boys minding goats. I'm not going up the mountain to throw pebbles at a giant. I'm going to cut his head off."

He said it loud, in the yard, in the hearing of the old knights and the novices and the people of the valley who came up the path every day to watch the champion train. They cheered him. By the third week of Lent, people were coming from all over the valley to watch. Old women brought him bread. Girls brought him flowers. The children followed him around the yard and pretended to be him, fighting each other with sticks.

"I'll bring back his head," Petros told them, laughing, with a child on each shoulder. "I'll bring it back on a pole, and we'll set it on top of the church chimney, and every giant in the Heights will see it and run."

The children cheered. Their mothers cheered. Hagop smiled.

Amma Thekla, sitting in the shade of the chapel door with her basket, closed her eyes.

"He's boasting," she said to Stephanos, who was sitting beside her. "Every word of it goes up the mountain. Anak hears it. Anak's master hears it. It feeds them."

"He's young, Amma. He's excited. He'll learn."

"He'll learn on the mountain," said the old woman. "That's the trouble with learning."

---

Dora taught Mikros the sling.

Stephanos found them at it one evening, down on the valley floor among the rock chimneys, where the old knights could not see. The shepherd boy was standing at one end of a stretch of flat ground with his sling in his hand, and Dora was standing beside him, and at the other end, forty paces off, a row of pebbles sat on top of a rock.

"Ring finger," Dora was saying. "Not the middle. Pinch the other end. Like a feather. Don't think about it."

Mikros whirled the sling and let fly. The stone went high and wide.

"You're thinking about it."

"I'm thinking about my father," said Mikros. It was the first time Stephanos had heard him speak. His voice was low and hoarse. "He went up the mountain. He was the best man in the valley. He had a sword and a shield and a coat of mail that Hagop gave him. He didn't come back."

"I know," said Dora.

"So how am I going to kill Anak with a stone?"

"You're not," said Dora. "Petros is going up the mountain, not you."

"Then why are you teaching me?"

Dora looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked up at Argaeus, huge and white and alone against the evening sky.

"I don't know," she said. "It seemed like something someone should do." She picked up a stone and put it in his hand. "Again. Ring finger."

He threw. The stone struck the rock six inches below the pebbles.

"Better," said Dora. "Again."

Stephanos went back up to the monastery without letting them see him. He did not know why. He lay awake that night in his cell cut into the rock, listening to the wind moan among the chimneys, and he thought about Petros in the yard with the children on his shoulders, and about the thin shepherd boy with his hoarse voice, and about his own sling, hanging on the peg by the door.

He got up in the dark and took it down. He went out to the edge of the terrace and stood there in the cold under the stars, and he practised. Ring finger. Pinch the end. Like a feather.

He practised until his arm ached and dawn came up grey behind Argaeus. Most of the stones went wild. A few did not.

---

At the equinox, a herald came down from the mountain.

It was a Gibbor, nine feet tall, in iron mail rusted red, with an iron collar around its neck and iron growing through its skin in dark veins along its arms and across its face. It came down the valley road at dawn with a horn in its hand, and the people of Göreme fled into their caves as it passed. It stopped in the square below the church chimney, where the Concord's soldiers had drawn up in a line with their spears levelled, and it looked at them with contempt, and blew its horn.

The sound was terrible. It went through the valley like a blade. The rock chimneys rang with it.

"*Göreme!*" the herald roared. "*The grandson of Gathos calls for his champion! Send him up the mountain before the sun sets, to the barrow of Gathos, or tomorrow the grandson comes down!*"

It turned and went back up the road. It did not wait for an answer.

Petros was ready. He had been ready since dawn. He stood in the yard of Saint George of the Rock in the best armour Hagop could find him, mail and lamellar and a helm with a nasal guard, and a sword at his hip and a steel mace in his hand and a shield with the purple of the Tagma painted on it, which Hagop had given him the night before. He looked magnificent. He looked like a warrior saint stepped down off the chapel walls. The old knights knelt to him when he came out. So did the novices. So did half the valley, gathered in the square below.

"I'll bring back his head," Petros told them.

They cheered.

He looked at Stephanos. His eyes were very bright, and there was something behind the brightness that Stephanos had not seen before, something tight and frightened.

"You'll come?" Petros said, low, so the crowd would not hear. "You and the others? You'll be there?"

"We'll be there," said Stephanos. "Kale knows another way in. Through the old graves on the east face. We'll go in the back while you go in the front. There'll be a lamp niche, and a word, like the others. And if it goes badly..."

"It won't go badly," said Petros. He said it too fast. "It won't. I'm the Shepherd's heir. You said so yourself."

"I said so."

"Then it won't go badly." Petros turned away and lifted the mace over his head, and the crowd below roared. He started down the path.

Stephanos watched him go. He watched the tall figure in borrowed armour walk down into the square, and through the cheering crowd, and out onto the road toward the mountain, alone, with the purple shield on his arm and the sun on his helm.

Somewhere behind him, he heard Amma Thekla begin to pray.

He turned to go and get his own armour. And he saw, at the edge of the yard, in the shadow of the chapel door, the shepherd boy Mikros standing alone with his sling in his hand. The boy was watching Petros go too. His thin face was very still.

When he saw Stephanos looking at him, he did not look away.
