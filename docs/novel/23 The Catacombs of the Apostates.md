## Chapter 23: The Catacombs of the Apostates

*The dead speak true to her.*

*Of the veil of the Myrrh-bearer*

---

The steps went down through the crypts first.

Stephanos knew them at once. The long low vault of brick, the porphyry tombs in their niches, the Shepherd's plain lid at the head of the hall with its crumbling wildflowers. They had come in from the side this time, through the crack, which opened in the roof of the crypt just behind the tomb of Andronikos the Proud. The empty tomb stood with its lid pushed aside. The bundles of forged letters were gone; the Eparch's men had taken them months ago.

The dead emperors slept. Amma Thekla's candles had long since burned out on the lids of the three that had been bound, but the dead in them did not stir as the band went past. Dora walked down the middle of the crypt with her brother's ugly lamp held up in front of her, and the little flame threw her shadow long and thin across the porphyry, and nothing moved.

At the Shepherd's tomb, she stopped.

She looked at the plain lid for a long time. She did not touch it. Then she bent her head, the way you bow to an old person in a village street, and went on.

The stair behind the tomb of Andronikos went down to the Drowned Cisterns, as it had before. But the cisterns had changed.

The water was higher. It had risen almost to the ledge at the foot of the stair, black and still, and the forest of columns stood up out of it with their capitals nearly touching the vault. The causeway was gone, drowned deep. There was no way across.

And the backward singing was louder. It came up out of the dark beyond the columns, from somewhere far on the other side, many voices, slow and heavy and wrong.

"We can't swim it," said Kale. "Not in armour. Not the Amma."

Sergios was already crouching at the edge of the ledge. He had the lamb inside his shirt, its small black face poking out at his collar. He leaned out over the black water and looked down into it for a long moment.

Then he whistled. Two notes, high and low, the way Captain Fotios had taught Dora to whistle the dolphins off Skiathos.

The water in front of the ledge stirred.

A long ripple came across the cistern from between the far columns. It came slowly, straight toward them. And then the water in front of the ledge bulged and broke, and the blind white head of the thing from the Cisterns came up out of it, streaming, as big as a cart, with its smooth eyeless face and its great mouth closed.

Kale had an arrow on the string before it was fully out of the water. Stephanos put his hand on her arm.

"Wait," he said.

The blind thing did not attack. It laid its great chin on the edge of the ledge, like a dog laying its head on its master's knee, and it snuffled at Sergios.

"Hello," said the fool softly. "Hello again. Did they feed you? After the Goldhand? After the scribes went away?" He reached out and stroked its white snout. "No. Nobody fed you. Nobody ever does."

He dug in his rags and brought out a heel of bread from the Tower's kitchen and held it out. The great mouth opened just enough, and the bread went in, and the mouth closed, and somewhere under the water the millstone sound of its chewing went on for a long time.

"There," said Sergios. "Now. Will you carry us?"

---

It carried them.

They rode across the Drowned Cisterns on the back of the blind white thing, all seven of them, sitting astride its long smooth neck behind its head like children on a log. It was cold and wet and slippery, and it swam with a slow rolling motion that made Theophanes turn green and hold on with both arms around the purple book. The columns went past on either side, huge and silent. Their reflections went past beneath. Dora sat in front, behind the fool, with the ugly lamp held up, and its small light went out ahead of them over the black water and lit the capitals of the columns one by one.

"Why is it helping us?" Lazaros whispered.

"Because it was hungry," said Amma Thekla behind him, "and someone fed it."

The blind thing carried them across the cisterns to a place on the far side where the columns gave way to a wall of rough rock, and in the rock there was a doorway, square, cut by men, with worn steps leading up out of the water to it. The thing laid its head against the steps and was still.

They climbed off. Sergios was last. He stood on the bottom step with the water up to his knees and put both his arms around the great white snout and held it for a moment.

"Thank you," he said. "Go and rest now. Nobody's going to sing at you anymore."

The blind thing slid back into the water. The ripple of it went away across the cistern between the columns and was gone.

Beyond the doorway, the backward singing was very loud.

---

The Catacombs of the Apostates had been a holy place once.

Stephanos saw that at once, as they came up the steps into the first hall. It was long and narrow and low, cut in the rock, with burial niches in the walls on both sides, row above row, from the floor to the vault. Every niche had held a body once, wrapped and laid on its shelf. The walls between the niches were painted. The paint was faded and peeling, but he could make out the figures: saints in rows with their hands raised, doves and fishes, vines and lambs, the Good Shepherd with a sheep across his shoulders. The oldest Christians of the City had buried their dead here, in the days before the first emperor, when the faith was new and hunted.

Then someone had changed it.

The paintings had been painted over. Not all of them; most of them. Over the saints with their hands raised, someone had painted other figures: men and women in rich clothing, bowing. Over the doves and fishes, serpents. Over the Good Shepherd, a crowned figure on a throne, with a sword in one hand and a bag of gold in the other, and the people of the City kneeling at its feet.

The bodies in the niches were different too. They were not wrapped in linen. They were dressed in silk and gold, in the robes of lords and bishops and courtiers, and they were laid out not on their backs but on their faces, in the attitude of worship, bowing toward the far end of the hall.

"The ones who bowed," said Amma Thekla quietly. "In the days of the seven heirs. When the palace minted coin for the Sea-Dragon and the armies marched under the Prince of the Heights. The ones who gave themselves to the archons, and were buried here, bowing, so they could go on bowing forever." She looked at the painted-over Shepherd. "They buried them on top of the saints. On purpose."

At the far end of the hall, the backward singing came through a second doorway. And through the doorway, there was light.

---

The false saints were in the second hall.

There were seven of them. They stood in a half-circle around an altar at the far end, facing it, with their backs to the doorway, and they were singing. They wore vestments: gold and white and scarlet, the vestments of bishops and priests, heavy with embroidery and pearls. They held censers, swinging, and the smoke that came out of the censers was black and smelled of burned hair. They were singing the liturgy. The same words the Patriarch sang every Sunday in the roofless Great Church, the ancient words, every syllable correct. Backward, from the end to the beginning.

Around their feet, on the floor of the hall, things were crawling.

Stephanos did not look at them closely. He did not want to. They were the dead of the catacombs, or what was left of them: pieces, bones held together with dried sinew and scraps of silk, crawling on the floor, dragging themselves toward the altar. As the false saints sang, the pieces moved faster. They drew themselves together. Bones found other bones. Stephanos saw a hand crawl across the floor and fasten onto the wrist of an arm that had no hand, and the fingers flexed.

"They're healing them," breathed Lazaros. "The singing. It's putting them back together."

"Putting them back together for what?" said Kale.

Nobody answered.

Then one of the false saints turned around.

It had a face. That was the worst of it. It had a face under the golden mitre, a dead man's face, grey and shrunken and dried like leather, with its lips drawn back from its teeth and its eye sockets empty. But the eye sockets were not dark. There was a light in them. A dim green light, like the light in the eyes of the bound emperor in the crypt, the first time they came down, a long time ago.

It held something in its hands. A cross, silver, small, with a relic set in the middle of it under a crystal. The cross was glowing, faintly. It was a holy thing, stolen and bent.

"*Blessed is he who comes,*" said the false saint, in a voice like dry leaves, "*in the name of the Lord.*"

It said it forward. It said it in exactly the tone the Patriarch used. It raised the stolen cross in blessing.

And all over the floor of the hall, the crawling pieces of the dead lifted their heads and turned toward the doorway.

---

They came in a wave.

Stephanos met them in the doorway with his sword. It was not like fighting men. It was not even like fighting the coin-wights in the Mint. The pieces came at him from the floor, from the walls, from the niches, scrabbling, clawing, bones and teeth and dry fingers, and he cut them, and they fell apart, and on the far side of the hall the false saints sang, and the pieces drew themselves back together, and came again. He cut a skeletal arm in two at the elbow and the forearm crawled away across the floor toward the altar and the false saint there sang a line of the backward liturgy and the forearm crawled back to its elbow, and joined, and came at him again.

Kale shot one of the false saints. The arrow went into its chest through the golden vestment and stuck there. It did not seem to notice. It went on singing.

"They heal each other!" Lazaros shouted. He had the icon of the Archistrategos up, blazing, and the pieces of the dead shrank back from its light, but only a little, and only for a moment. "The singing! As long as they sing, they can't be killed!"

"Theophanes!" Stephanos shouted. "The words! You read the seer's robe! Can you..."

"It's not written!" Theophanes was pressed against the wall of the doorway with the purple book clutched against him. "It's not written anywhere! It's the liturgy! It's the real liturgy, only backward! There's nothing to read!"

"Then name it," said Amma Thekla.

She had come forward into the doorway, between Stephanos and Lazaros. She was not weaving. She stood very small and bent in her black habit, with her empty hands at her sides, and she looked at the false saint with the stolen cross.

"Name it, Theophanes," she said. "The lie. You know what it is. You've known your whole life. You spent eleven years copying lies for the Tower. You know what a lie sounds like when it's dressed up in the right words. Name it."

Theophanes stared at her. Then he stared at the false saint. His lips moved. He was listening to the backward singing, Stephanos saw; listening hard, with his whole face, the way he had listened to the fog and the Words.

"It's the same words," he whispered. "It's all the same words. Every one of them. Only backward."

"And?"

"And backward, it's..." He stopped. His eyes went wide. "Backward, it ends with the beginning. It ends with *Blessed is the kingdom*. That's the first thing the priest says. *Blessed is the kingdom of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit.* It's the first line. And they're singing it last." He was speaking faster now. "They're singing the whole liturgy backward so they can end on the kingdom. Their kingdom. They're taking every word of the true liturgy and turning it around so that it points at a throne. *That's* the lie. It's the same lie as the paintings. They've painted over the Shepherd with a king. They've turned the whole thing around so that it bows to a crown instead of a cross."

"Then say it," said Thekla. "Out loud. To their faces."

Theophanes pushed himself off the wall. He stepped into the doorway beside the old woman. He was shaking. He raised his voice over the backward singing, and it cracked, and he raised it again.

"You're lying!" he shouted at the false saint with the stolen cross. "You're singing it backward to make it bow to a king! It doesn't bow to a king! It never did! The kingdom in the first line isn't yours! It's the Father's and the Son's and the Holy Spirit's! It's not a crown! It's a cross! You stole the cross in your hands from a saint and you're using it to bless a lie! I know what you are! I've been one! You're a forger!"

The false saint stopped singing.

It stood in front of the altar with the stolen cross raised, and its empty green eye sockets turned toward Theophanes, and it did not sing. Its dried lips moved. No sound came out.

The other six stopped too, one after another. The backward liturgy faltered and broke off in the middle of a line. And on the floor of the hall, the crawling pieces of the dead stopped moving. They lay where they were. They did not draw together again.

In the silence, Stephanos cut down the nearest false saint with one blow. It fell, and did not rise.

Kale shot the next. Lazaros walked forward with the blazing icon, and the third false saint turned away from it with its hands over its empty eyes, and crumbled, and fell in a heap of vestments on the floor. Sergios threw an onion at the fourth and it fell over backward into the altar. The last three stood still in front of the altar and did not fight. They stood there with their censers hanging from their hands, and the green light went out of their eyes one by one, and they folded down onto the floor of the catacomb and were still.

The stolen cross fell from the first one's hands and rang on the stone.

Dora picked it up. She looked at the relic under the crystal in the middle of it: a tiny fragment of bone, brown with age.

"It was a saint's," she said. "Somebody holy. They stole it."

"They'll give it back now," said Amma Thekla. "Put it on the altar. Face the right way."

Dora went to the altar and laid the silver cross on it, carefully, facing out toward the hall. The altar was old stone, far older than the false saints' vestments, and when she laid the cross on it, the painted wall behind the altar changed. The crowned figure on its throne with the sword and the bag of gold seemed to fade, and under it, faint and old and peeling, the Good Shepherd looked out, with a sheep across his shoulders.

---

The third hall was where the Hungry was.

Stephanos smelled it before they reached it: the cold rotten sweetness of the grave, stronger than anywhere else in the catacombs, so strong that his eyes watered. He heard it too. A wet sound, many sounds together, like a crowd of people eating.

It was not a hall. It was a pit. The catacomb opened out into a great round chamber, and the floor of it had been dug away, long ago, into a deep bowl, and in the bottom of the bowl, heaped, were bones. Thousands of them. The bones of every body the false saints had not been able to put together, and every body before that, all thrown into the pit, century after century.

And the bones were moving.

They were not crawling, the way the pieces in the second hall had crawled. They were writhing, all together, like a heap of worms, and out of the heap, as Stephanos watched from the edge of the pit, something was rising. It was grey and cold and shapeless, like the Rephaim on Argaeus, but it was not one shape. It was many. Dozens of grey shadow-shapes, pieces of shades, a hand here and a face there and a shoulder, all knotted together into one great shifting mass. And all over it, everywhere, there were mouths. Open mouths. Grey, hungry, gaping.

"The Hungry," said Thekla.

The cold came off it in waves. Stephanos felt it reach his face across the pit, and the old white scar on his forearm, where the Rephaim had touched him on the mountain, began to ache.

The Hungry rose up out of the bone pit and turned toward them, and all its mouths opened wider.

"Burial rites," said Thekla. She was already praying. "It's all the ones nobody buried. All of them. Every one who was thrown in the pit. Say the rite. All of you. Every one of you who says it strips it."

They said it. Stephanos said it, and Kale, and Lazaros, and Theophanes reading from the back of the purple book, and Sergios singing it to the tune of a drinking song. *With the spirits of the righteous made perfect, give rest, O Saviour, to the souls of your servants.* The Hungry reached for them across the pit with its grey hands, and where the words touched it, the hands faded and went thin and fell away. But it was so big. There were so many. For every hand that faded, three more came up out of the heap of bones, and the cold was so bad now that Stephanos could not feel his fingers on his sword.

Dora had not said the rite.

She was standing at the edge of the pit with her brother's lamp in her hands and the veil of the Myrrh-bearer over her face, and she was listening.

"Dora!" said Kale. "The rite! Say it!"

"They're talking," said Dora.

Kale stared at her.

"The dead," said Dora. "In the pit. They're talking to me. All of them. I can hear them." Her voice was strange and far away. "The veil. The Amma said. *The dead speak true to her.* They're telling me their names."

She took a step forward, to the very edge of the pit, and held the lamp up. The grey hands of the Hungry reached for her, and stopped a yard from her face.

"Anna," said Dora. "Daughter of Kyros. You were a weaver. You died in the plague in the reign of the fourth heir. They threw you in the pit because your husband bowed to the Sea-Dragon and they buried him in a niche and you in the pit." She turned her head slightly, listening. "Basil. You were a soldier. You didn't bow. They killed you for it and threw you here to shame you." Another turn. "Maria. Maria. You were seven. Nobody knows why you're here. Nobody remembers." She was weeping now, under the veil, but her voice did not stop. "Ioannes. Theodoros. Eudokia. Makarios. Zoe. Petros. Another Petros. Another Maria."

She went on. She stood at the edge of the pit and said their names, one after another, as they told them to her, and as she said each one, one of the grey hands of the Hungry drew back, and faded, and was gone. She said forty names. A hundred. Stephanos lost count. The Hungry was shrinking. It sank down into the bone pit as she spoke, the mass of it growing smaller and thinner, its mouths closing one by one.

"Kyrie," whispered Theophanes beside Stephanos. "Dear God. She's burying them. She's burying them by name."

At the end, there was one shape left.

It rose up out of the bone pit alone, grey and thin, and it was not a hand or a face or a piece. It was a whole shade, a tall man, a big man, and on its head it wore a crown, and on its shoulders it wore a cloak that had been purple once. Its face was grey and blurred like smoke. But Stephanos knew it. He had seen it carved on a tomb lid in the crypt above, calm and noble and a little sad.

Andronikos the Proud.

Dora looked up at him.

"You," she said.

The grey shade of the Emperor looked down at her. For a long moment it did not speak. Then its voice came, and it was not the dry hiss of the bound dead or the contempt of the seer. It was a man's voice, old and tired and very quiet.

"*Child of the Shepherd's house,*" it said. "*I know you. I have waited a hundred years for one of you to come down.*"

"Why are you here?" said Dora. "Your tomb's up there. In the crypt. It's empty."

"*They never found my body,*" said Andronikos. "*I died in the smoke on the night the Hunter broke the walls. I ran down the crypt stair to hide, and the seers came down after me, and I ran further, and fell into the pit. And the pit kept me.*" The grey shape was very still. "*I have had a hundred years to think, child. In the pit. With the ones who bowed and the ones who wouldn't. I have had a hundred years to think about the night of the statue.*"

"Tell me," said Dora.

"*You're wearing the veil,*" said the shade. "*I have to. The dead speak true to her who wears it.*" The blurred face turned, very slightly, toward Stephanos, and then back. "*I set up my likeness in the house of God. Under the dome. Where the people stood to hear the Gospel. I thought I was teaching them who kept them. I was opening a door.*"

"The Gate of Sheol," said Theophanes, very low.

"*Pride is a door,*" said Andronikos. "*The oldest one. The Lord of the Tower opened it at Babel, and the Flood shut it, and I opened it again with a bronze statue and a speech. On that night the Gate of Sheol stood open under the Great Church, and the grave was coming up into the City.*" The grey shape seemed to bow its head. "*And the Horn went down. It went down with the lamps, to the Gate, and lay across the threshold, and shut it. Like a stone rolled across a tomb.*"

"And it's been there ever since," said Stephanos.

"*A hundred years. Holding the door. With the seven lamps burning around it.*" The shade looked at Dora. "*That is why it has never come up, child. It cannot. If it lifts from the threshold, the door opens. It has been waiting for someone to come down and take its place.*"

"The least of the Shepherd's house," said Dora.

"*No,*" said Andronikos.

Everyone went still.

"*The least goes down,*" said the shade, "*so the Horn will come up. The least carries it. The least is who it comes up for. But someone must stay at the door.*" The blurred face turned toward Stephanos again, and this time it stayed there. "*Someone must stand in the gap. Someone must keep the lamps burning at the threshold through the first night, when the Horn is lifted and the grave comes up to see who has taken its place. If the lamps burn till morning, they will hold the door forever, as the Horn held it. That is what the lamps are for. That is why the Horn took them down.*"

Stephanos felt the words go into him like cold water. He did not look at Theophanes. He did not need to. He had known since the refectory. He had known, if he was honest, since the lion lay down across the crack.

*When you were young, you dressed yourself and went where you wanted. When you are old, someone else will lead you where you do not want to go.* He did not know where the words came from. He knew they were not his.

"*One of the strong,*" said the shade of Andronikos softly. "*It must be one of the strong. The strong will seek the Horn, and it will not rise for them. But the strong can stand at the door while it rises for someone else.*" The grey shape was fading now, growing thinner, like smoke thinning in the wind. "*I was strong once, Hornbearer. I was the strongest man in the empire. I used it to set up my own face in the house of God. Use yours better.*"

"Emperor," said Dora.

The fading shade looked at her.

"Do you want to rest?" she said. "Like the others?"

The shade of Andronikos the Proud was silent a long time.

"*Yes,*" it said at last, very faintly. "*God help me. Yes.*"

Dora said the rite. She said it alone, in her clear small voice, at the edge of the pit, with her brother's lamp held up in both hands and the veil of the Myrrh-bearer over her face. *Give rest, O Saviour, to the soul of your servant Andronikos.* She did not stumble over the name.

The grey shape bowed its head. And then there was nothing in the pit but bones, still and quiet, and the lamplight lying on them.

---

The vigil lamp of the Catacombs was on the far side of the bone pit, in a niche in the wall above a narrow stair that went down.

Amma Thekla sat down beside it.

She sat down on the top step of the stair with her back against the wall, under the niche, and she took out her basket, and her hands began to move on it. She did not say anything. She did not need to.

"Amma," said Kale. "No."

"I can't go further," said the old woman. "I've known it since the cisterns. My legs are finished. My heart is finished. I'm sixty-two years old and I've been in the desert forty-two of them, and I rode a mule to Cappadocia in the snow and walked down a salt well and sat on a sea-monster." She did not stop weaving. "And someone has to keep this lamp lit. For the way back. The Blight will come for it the moment you're gone. If it goes out, you won't be able to come back up this way, and the Hunter's seers will come down it after you." She looked up at Kale. "Light it, child. And then go."

Kale stood very still. Her face was working.

"I'll stay with you," she said.

"No. You'll go down with the girl, and shoot whatever needs shooting." The old woman's voice softened. "And then you'll come back up, and marry the painter, and have a great many children, and teach them all to shoot and to paint. That's what you'll do."

Kale's face went scarlet under its brown. Behind her, Lazaros made a small choked sound.

"How did you...?"

"I'm an Amma," said Thekla. "I see into hearts. Also, I have eyes." She went back to her weaving. "Light the lamp."

Kale lit it. She filled Lazaros's lamp with the last of their oil and hung it in the niche, and lit it from Dora's ugly lamp, and the small flame caught and stood up straight. On the plaster at the back of the niche, no word appeared. There were no more words. There had only ever been four.

But the light of the lamp went out across the bone pit and lay on the bones, and on the painted walls where the old saints still showed faintly through, and for a moment the whole terrible chamber looked like what it had been before the apostates came: a place where Christians had laid their dead to sleep in hope.

Dora knelt in front of the old woman on the step.

"Amma," she said. "Will you be here? When we come back up?"

"If God wills," said Thekla. "If not, I'll be somewhere better, and you'll be sorry you didn't bring me more figs." She put down her basket and took the girl's face in both her brown, wrinkled, palm-scarred hands, and looked at her for a long moment. "Go down, child. Go down and bring it up. It's been waiting a long time."

She kissed Dora on the forehead, through the veil. Then she let go and picked up her basket again.

"*Lord Jesus Christ,*" she said. Over, under. "*Son of God.*"

They went down the stair past her, one by one. Stephanos was last. He stopped on the step below her and knelt, and kissed her hand, the way he kissed the Abba's.

"Amma," he said. "You told me in Thessalonike that I was protecting the wrong one."

"I did."

"You were right."

"I know," said Amma Thekla. She did not look up from her basket. "Go and protect the right one. And Stephanos."

"Amma?"

"Whatever you do at the bottom," she said, "do it with your hands. And say the prayer while you do it. It helps."

He went down the stair into the dark. Behind him, above him, he heard her voice going on, steady and soft, *have mercy on me*, over and under, until the turn of the stair took it away.

Below, the stair went down and down, and the air grew warm, and dry, and still, and the backward singing had stopped. There was no singing anymore. There was only silence, and, very far below, a light: not green, not the sick marsh-light of the seers. Gold. A small, steady, golden light, like a lamp burning in a window a long way off, at the bottom of the world.
