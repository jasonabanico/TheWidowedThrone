## Chapter 20: The Lion

*Jesus said to Simon Peter, "Simon son of John, do you love me more than these?"*

*The Gospel according to John*

---

The Hunter's host came up to the Land Walls at sunset, and the City watched it come.

Stephanos did not watch. He was lying on a bench in the hospice of Kosmas the Anargyros, near the Blachernae gate, with his lamellar cut off him and his face in a pillow, while Kosmas worked the Gibbor's arrowhead out of the muscle beside his spine. He did not cry out. He had not said anything since the Tower. Kosmas talked to him the whole time, quietly, the way he talked to children and dying men, about nothing: the weather, the price of bread, a cat that had had kittens in the hospice laundry. Stephanos did not listen.

When the arrowhead was out, Kosmas set his wrist. It was broken in two places now, and the bones had begun to knit crooked since the barrow. Kosmas broke them again. Stephanos felt it, and did not cry out then either.

"You won't hold a sword with this hand for a while," Kosmas said, binding it. "A long while. Maybe not ever. I'll pray. God's been kind to broken bones before." He tied off the linen. "The knee I can't do anything about. You've wrecked it. You'll limp."

Stephanos said nothing.

The physician sat back on his stool and looked at him. Kosmas was a small, plain, balding man with ink-stained fingers and a patient face, and he had healed the sick of the City for twenty years without taking a copper for it. He had seen a great deal.

"The Abba's in the chapel of the Tower," he said at last. "The Amma's with him. And Kale. The brothers who lived are keeping vigil. There are eleven of them." He paused. "Gregoras didn't come back from the bridge."

"I know. I found him."

It was the first thing Stephanos had said. His voice was a croak.

"The Domestikos did," said Kosmas. "Come back. The levies brought him in at dusk, on a cart. He's lost his arm. The left one, above the elbow. I took it off an hour ago. He'll live, I think." He hesitated. "He asked for you."

Stephanos turned his face to the wall.

After a while, Kosmas got up and went away. Stephanos heard him in the next room, talking quietly to someone else, and then in the room after that. The hospice was full. It had been full since the afternoon, when the levies came back from Melantias.

Through the small window high in the wall, he could hear the Hunter's drums. They had not stopped since sunset. They were closer now. They were very close.

---

He got up in the middle of the night and went out.

Nobody stopped him. Kosmas was asleep on a pallet by the door with his head on his arms. The streets were dark and empty; the Eparch had ordered every lamp in the City put out, so the Hunter's seers could not see where the people were. The only light came from the west, from the walls, where the sky over Thrace was red with the fires of the Hunter's camp. It lay on the domes and the roofs and the empty squares like the light of a forge.

He limped through the dark city with his right arm bound across his chest and his left hand on the walls of the houses to steady himself. He did not know where he was going. His feet knew. They took him down the long straight street of the Mese, past the Forum of Theodosius and the Forum of Constantine, past the Milion where the stair went down to the Undercroft, and into the great empty square before the Great Church.

The doors stood open. They always stood open. There was nothing inside to steal.

He went in.

---

The Great Church was dark. The stars were out, and they shone down through the great round hole where the dome had been, and made a pale circle of light on the marble floor in the middle of the nave. The rest of the church was black. The columns went up into the dark on either side like the trunks of trees in a forest at night. At the far end, under its canopy of oiled canvas, the altar was a darker shape against the dark, and behind it, the icon screen, and behind that, the sanctuary.

He walked up the nave into the circle of starlight, and stopped in the middle of it.

This was where the statue had stood. He had never thought about that before, in nine years of coming to the Great Church. He knew the story; everyone did. But he had never stood on the spot and thought: here. Here, under the dome, where the people stood to hear the Gospel. Here Andronikos the Proud set up his bronze likeness with its calm face and its hand raised in blessing, and walked around it three times, and said nothing.

*Where the proud one stood, the Horn lay down.*

He looked down at the marble under his feet. It was cracked and worn and stained with a hundred years of rain. There was nothing to show that a statue had ever stood there.

He took off the belt of Truth.

He did it with his left hand, clumsily. The buckle was stiff. The bronze plates were so cold they burned his fingers. He held it for a moment, looking at the word cut into the buckle in the old square letters. Then he bent down and laid it on the marble, in the middle of the circle of starlight, where the statue had stood.

He did not know why. It seemed like the only thing left to do.

He sat down on the floor beside it.

---

He did not pray. He tried. He began the Jesus Prayer, the way the Amma had taught him, and got as far as the first two words and could not go on. He began the Question, the Abba's question, and got as far as *Whom* and stopped.

He sat on the cold floor with his knees drawn up and his bad arm across his chest and his good arm around his knees, and looked at the belt on the marble in front of him, and thought.

He thought about the fog off Naxos, and the hand on his wrist, and the voice counting his debts. Everyone else had said it. *I forgive you.* Dora had said it, and Sergios, and Kale, and even Zenon the captain, who had paid the Goldhand's tithe for eleven years. He had not said it. He had stood on the deck with his sword in his hand, and not said it.

He thought about the inn at Tarsus, and the white wall, and the lamps burning along it. The stranger sitting on the rail beside Petros in the full light of the lamp, with nothing behind him. He had seen it. He had looked at the wall and seen it with his own eyes. And Dora had said, *He's pretending,* and he had said, *It was dark. The lamps were flickering. You're thirteen.*

He thought about the armoury, last night, a hundred years ago. Dora in the doorway with the veil over her hair and the sling around her wrist. *Somebody has to go down. Come with me. Please.* And he had said, *No. You're a child.*

Three times.

Three times she had stood in front of him with the truth in her hands, and three times he had turned away from it. Because it was not the truth he wanted. Because he had wanted a tall man, a strong man, a man who could stand in the Hippodrome in purple and make the lords kneel. Because he had wanted to be the one who found him. Because he had wanted, God help him, to ride out under the red eagle of Taron one more time, in front of the whole City, and have the whole City see.

And the red eagle was in the mud at Melantias, torn in half, with a giant's blood on it. And Gregoras was dead, and the Emperor was dead, and Dove was dead, and the Abba was dead, and Dora was in the Hunter's camp, and it was his fault. All of it. He had done it. Not the stranger, not the Hunter, not Theophanes. Him.

*Whom will the Horn anoint?*

He did not know. He had never known. He had only wanted to.

He put his face down on his knees, and sat there in the dark, in the middle of the Great Church, on the spot where the proud man had stood, and he did not weep. He was past weeping. He sat there, empty, and waited for nothing.

---

He smelled rain.

It came into the Great Church all at once, the way a smell does when a door opens. It was the smell of rain falling on dry ground: the first rain after a long summer, when the dust drinks it and gives up a scent that is like nothing else in the world, green and clean and old. It filled the nave. It was so strong that he lifted his head.

There was no rain. The stars were shining down through the open dome, bright and cold and clear. The marble floor around him was dry.

The smell grew stronger.

And he felt warmth. It came from behind him, from the east end of the church, from the direction of the altar. It was like the warmth of a hearth when you come in from the snow: it touched his back first, and then his shoulders, and then his whole body, and went into him, into his cold hands and his cold feet and his broken wrist, and the ache in his back where the arrowhead had been. It was so gentle that he did not notice at first how much it hurt. It was the hurt of frozen hands held to a fire.

He heard breathing.

It was deep and slow and very large. It sounded like wind in a chimney. It came from behind him, close, closer than the altar. It came from the dark just outside the circle of starlight.

He could not turn round. He found that he could not move at all. He sat on the floor with his knees drawn up and his arm around them, and he could feel his heart beating in his chest so hard it shook him, and he could not turn his head.

Something walked into the circle of starlight.

It was a lion.

He saw it first as a great golden shape moving past him on his left side, so close that he could have touched it: a flank, huge, covered in fur the colour of wheat at harvest, the colour of the Emperor's hair, the colour of honey, glowing faintly in the starlight as if lit from inside. Then a shoulder, and a leg, and a paw. The paw came down on the marble floor beside the belt of Truth, and it was the size of a dinner platter, and where it touched the marble, grass grew.

He saw it happen. The grass came up out of the cracks in the marble around the paw, green and soft and wet, as if it had been waiting there under the stone for a hundred years. Small white flowers opened in it. The same flowers he had seen in the tracks in the frost at Arkadiopolis.

The lion walked past him into the middle of the circle of starlight, and turned, and faced him.

Stephanos had seen lions before. There were lions in the Syrian desert, and in the hills of Armenia when he was a boy. He had seen them in cages in the Goldhand's gardens, and on the walls of churches, painted, with the evangelist Mark. He had never seen a lion like this.

It was bigger than a horse. Its mane was a great mass of gold, darker than its body, falling around its shoulders and its chest like a king's cloak. Its face was broad and calm and very old and very young at the same time, and its eyes were golden, and they looked at him.

They looked at him the way no one had ever looked at him in his life. They saw everything. They saw the courtyard at Kaisareia, and the ford, and the fog, and the Goldhand's hall, and the inn at Tarsus, and the armoury, and the bridge. They saw every angry word he had ever said and every stone he had ever thrown in two directions. They saw all of it, all at once, and they did not look away.

He saw the paws.

The front paws, resting on the grass that had grown on the marble. Across the top of each one, through the golden fur, there was a scar. A round scar, puckered and white and old, as if a nail had been driven through.

He saw the side. Under the great ribs on the left, through the gold, a long scar, like the scar a spear leaves.

He fell on his face.

He did not decide to. His body did it. He was on his knees and then his face was on the marble, on the new grass, and he was shaking so hard his teeth rattled, and he could smell the rain and the grass and the lion, a smell like hot stone and summer fields, and the warmth was all around him, pressing down on him like a great weight, and he could not breathe.

He thought: *I'm going to die.*

He thought: *Good.*

"Stephanos."

The voice did not come from the lion's mouth. He did not hear it with his ears. He felt it in his chest, in his ribs, in the marble under his face, deep and low, like the sound of the sea in a cave, like the sound a bell makes long after it has stopped ringing.

"Stephanos, son of Tigran. Get up."

He could not. He tried. His arms would not hold him.

Something touched the back of his head. It was the lion's nose. It was warm and soft and enormous, and it pushed him, gently, the way a ewe pushes a lamb that has fallen, and he found that he could move. He got up onto his knees. He knelt on the grass in the circle of starlight with his bound arm across his chest, and he looked up into the lion's face.

"Lord," he said.

The golden eyes looked down at him.

"Do you love me?"

He opened his mouth. Nothing came out. He shut it again.

He thought of the fog. He thought of the hand on his wrist, and the words he had not said.

"Lord," he said, "you know I didn't forgive them. In the fog. On the ship. Everyone else forgave, and I didn't. I couldn't. I held my sword." He was shaking. "I didn't forgive my count. I never have. I carried it for nine years, like a stone, and I threw it at everyone. You know that. You saw."

The lion was silent. It waited.

"I love you," said Stephanos. His voice broke. "Lord, you know I love you."

"Feed my lambs," said the lion.

He did not understand. He knelt there with the words in his chest like a warm coal, and did not understand them.

The lion lowered its great head until its face was close to his. He could feel its breath on his face, warm, smelling of grass.

"Stephanos, son of Tigran," it said again. "Do you love me?"

He thought of Tarsus. The white wall. The empty space where the shadow should have been.

"I saw it," he whispered. "At the inn. I saw the wall. She told me what it meant and I saw it with my own eyes and I said she was a child. I said the lamps were flickering. I let Petros go. I let him go with the liar. I let him go because I wanted him to be the one, and if the stranger was a liar, then maybe he wasn't. I chose my own wanting over what I saw. Over her." He was weeping now. He had not thought he could weep again. "Lord, you know I love you. You know."

"Tend my sheep," said the lion.

And a third time. The lion did not move. It stood over him in the starlight on the new grass, huge and golden and still, with the scars on its paws and its side, and its eyes on his face.

"Stephanos, son of Tigran. Do you love me?"

He was grieved. He did not know why. It was the third time, and something in him broke at the third time, the way something breaks in a man who has been asked a thing once too often and has run out of ways to say it.

He thought of the armoury. Dora in the doorway. *Come with me. Please. Not to the bridge. Down.*

"She asked me to go down," he said. "With her. Under the church. To the Gate. She said somebody had to. She said she'd go, and she asked me to come. And I said no. I said she was a child. I said the Horn could wait one more day. I rode out to the bridge under my old banner, because I wanted everyone to see me carry it. Because I wanted to be strong. And they took her while I was gone. They took her because I left her. The Abba died in the door trying to keep them out, and I wasn't there, because I was at the bridge, being strong." He could hardly speak. "Lord. You know all things. You know everything I've done. You know I love you. You know it. I don't know anything else anymore. I don't know whom the Horn will anoint. I don't know if I ever did. I only know I love you. That's all I have left."

The lion looked at him for a long time.

Then it lowered its head and breathed on him.

It was like standing in the doorway of a bakehouse when the ovens are opened. The warmth went into him. It went into every part of him, into his chest and his belly and his bones. It went into his broken wrist, and he felt the bones in it shift and settle, and the swelling go down, and the pain go out like water running from a jar. It went into his back, where the arrowhead had been, and the torn muscle knit. It went into his knee.

It went into the cold places. The courtyard at Kaisareia. The deck of the *Saint Phokas* in the fog. The gallery at Tarsus. The armoury. It went into all of them, and they did not go away, but they were warm now. They were not stones anymore. They were only things that had happened, that he had done, that were done.

"Feed my sheep," said the lion.

---

Stephanos knelt on the grass and looked at his right hand.

It was whole. He moved the fingers. He closed it into a fist, and opened it. There was no pain.

He looked at his knee. He shifted his weight onto it. It held. But when he moved it, it did not move the way it used to. There was a stiffness in it, a hitch, deep in the joint, like a door that has been hung a little crooked. He would limp. He understood that. The lion had healed his hand and left him the limp, the way the angel at the ford of the Jabbok had left Jacob limping after the night of wrestling.

He looked at the belt of Truth, lying on the marble beside the lion's paw, in the new grass.

It was not cold. He did not have to touch it to know. The bronze plates were catching the starlight, and they looked warm.

He looked up at the lion.

"Lord," he said. "What about her?"

The words came out before he could stop them.

"Dora. Theodora. The shepherd girl. Is she the one? Is she the least of the Shepherd's house? Is she the one the Horn will anoint?" He heard himself, and could not stop. "And Petros? What about Petros? Is he alive? Is he..."

The lion looked at him. And for the first time, something moved in the great golden face that might have been, in a man, the beginning of a smile.

"What is that to you?" said the lion. "You must follow me."

Stephanos was silent.

The lion turned. It walked away from him across the circle of starlight, toward the east end of the church, toward the altar under its canvas canopy. Where it walked, the grass came up out of the marble. It walked past the altar, and through the Beautiful Gate of the icon screen, which stood open, and into the sanctuary. Stephanos got up and followed it. He limped. He did not notice.

The sanctuary of the Great Church was small and dark. The seven silver lamps hung above the altar on their short chains, dark and cold, as they had hung for a hundred years. To the left of the altar was a shelf of green marble, empty. And in the floor between the altar and the shelf was the crack: a long jagged split in the marble, wide enough for a man to climb down through, black, going down into the dark. Over it lay the icon of the Archistrategos Michael, the one Lazaros had renewed, face up, with seven candles burning around it on the floor.

Lazaros was asleep beside it, curled on his side on the marble with his scarred hands folded under his cheek.

The lion walked past the sleeping painter without waking him. It stopped beside the crack in the floor. It looked down into it for a long moment. Then it lay down.

It lay down across the crack, the way a dog lies across the door of its master's house, with its great head on its paws and the icon of the Archistrategos under its chest. It looked back at Stephanos with its golden eyes.

He understood.

He stood in the Beautiful Gate and looked at the lion lying across the door to the Undercroft, and he understood. He understood the way you understand a thing you have known all along and refused to look at: all at once, with no argument, and no way back.

*It went down with the lamps, and it keeps the door.*

*The strong will seek it, and it will not rise for them.*

*When the least of the Shepherd's house goes down, the Horn will come up.*

It was not his to choose. It had never been his to choose. It was not Petros's and it was not Bardas's and it was not the Domestikos's. It was not even Dora's. The Horn would come to whom it came, and it would not be the one anybody picked, and that was the whole point, as Dora's grandmother had said, as the Abba had said every night for thirty years without ever saying it.

He could not choose the heir. He could not make the Horn come up. He could not win it at a bridge or buy it on a slave block or find it in a book.

But somebody had to go down. And the least of the Shepherd's house was in the Hunter's camp tonight, in chains, because he had left her.

He could go and get her. And he could go down with her. That was all. That was all he could do, and it was enough, and it was his.

*You must follow me.*

He looked at the lion, and the lion looked at him. And then, between one breath and the next, the lion was not there.

He did not see it go. It was there, lying across the crack with its head on its paws, and then there was only the crack, and the icon, and the seven candles burning around it, and the smell of rain fading slowly out of the sanctuary like the last note of a hymn.

In the marble floor, all the way from the circle of starlight under the dome to the lip of the crack, there was a line of green grass, the width of a lion's stride, dotted with small white flowers.

---

Lazaros woke at dawn to find a Hornbearer kneeling in the sanctuary beside him, with the belt of Truth buckled around his waist and his right hand resting on the icon of the Archistrategos.

The painter sat up. He looked at Stephanos. He looked at the grass on the floor. He looked at it for a long time, and then he put out one scarred hand and touched it, very gently, and it was real, and wet with dew.

"Stephanos," he whispered. "Stephanos, what..."

"He was here," said Stephanos.

Lazaros did not ask who. He looked at the grass, and his face crumpled, and he began to weep.

---

Sergios was sitting on the steps of the Great Church when they came out into the grey morning.

He was eating a raw onion. He looked up at them as they came down the steps, and he looked at Stephanos's right hand, which was no longer bound, and at his knee, and at his face. His mad bright eyes went very clear.

"Oh," said the fool softly. "Oh. He came, didn't he. He came to you."

"Yes."

Sergios put down his onion. He got up from the step and came and stood in front of Stephanos and looked up at him, the way Dora looked up at him, with his head tilted back.

"I know where she is," the fool said.

Stephanos went still.

"In the Hunter's camp. Outside the Golden Gate, where his tower stands now. In a tent at the foot of it, with her brother, and the scribe, and the Master Scribe, and a lot of seers. They're keeping her there till the walls fall. Then they're bringing her here. To the church. To make her go down." Sergios's voice was calm. "I was there last night. I walked around. Nobody stopped me. Nobody ever stops a fool."

"Then take me there."

"No," said Sergios.

"Sergios..."

"You can't come," said the fool. "They'd see you in a heartbeat. You're a Hornbearer. You walk like a Hornbearer. You smell like a Hornbearer. You're too big and too sane." He grinned suddenly, a wide mad grin full of broken teeth. "It has to be me. I'm the only one who can walk into the Hunter's camp and walk out again. Because nobody ever thinks a fool can do anything. That's what fools are for."

He turned and began to walk away, down the steps, across the empty square, toward the west. He was barefoot. His rags flapped around him in the dawn wind.

"Sergios!" Stephanos called after him. "What do I do? While you're gone? What do I do?"

The fool turned round, walking backward, and threw his arms wide.

"Get the others!" he shouted. "Get the lamps! Get ready to go down! I'll bring her to the church!" He laughed, high and cracked and joyful, the laugh that had made a giant crack his head on his grandfather's lintel. "Come in the morning, Hornbearer, and see!"

He turned again and ran, skipping, across the square and into the dark mouth of the Mese, toward the Land Walls and the Hunter's drums, and was gone.
