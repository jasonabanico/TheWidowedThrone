## Chapter 2: Letters in a Borrowed Hand

*The Adversary has no army. He has a pen, and he knows how to forge your brother's hand.*

*Sayings of the Desert Mothers*

---

The council of the City met in the Magnaura, which had been a palace hall when there were emperors to hold court in it. Now half its roof was sailcloth, and pigeons nested in the capitals of the columns, and the throne on its porphyry dais at the far end stood under a sheet of grey linen that nobody ever took off.

Stephanos stood behind the Domestikos with his helm under his arm and his knee throbbing, and he tried to keep his face still.

"This," said the Eparch, "is a letter in my hand."

Irene Doukaina was a small woman in her fifties, with iron-grey hair pinned close under a plain black veil and the sharp, tired face of someone who had not slept a full night in a decade. She had run the City's grain and watch for twelve years. People said she knew the weight of every sack in every granary between the Golden Gate and the Horn, and people said she had once had a baker flogged for adding chalk to his flour, and both things were true. She held the letter up now between two fingers, the way a woman holds a dead mouse by the tail.

"It is addressed to Leon Chrysocheir, whom the islanders call the Goldhand. In it I offer to hand him the customs of the Harbour gate for ten years, in exchange for a loan of fifty thousand nomismata. It is sealed with my seal. It is written in my hand. It was found yesterday on the body of a drowned courier at the Neorion docks." She lowered it. "I did not write it."

The council hall murmured. There were forty of them on the benches along the walls: guild masters, deacons, captains of the watch, the heads of the great families who had come home from the exile. They sat by faction, the way they always sat, and Stephanos could have drawn the lines between them on the floor with chalk. The families who had lost the most in Nebrodia sat together near the door and trusted no one. The guild masters sat together near the Eparch and trusted only money. The clergy sat together near the Patriarch and argued with each other in low voices. And along the east wall, in their good wool and their fur collars, sat the lords who owned land outside the walls, and who wanted a crown on someone's head before the Tower came.

"Forgive me, Lady Eparch," said a guild master from the benches. It was Ioannes the silk merchant, a fat, careful man with a gold ring on each finger. "How can we know that? It is your seal."

"Because I have been Eparch for twelve years, Master Ioannes, and I have never borrowed a copper from the Goldhand, and I will cut off my own hand before I do."

"So you say."

"So I say." The Eparch's voice did not rise. "And the Patriarch also has a letter."

The Patriarch Esdras the Younger stood up from his bench. He was a tall man, stooped, with a long grey beard and the large, soft hands of a scholar. He had been born in the exile and had learned his letters in the brick fields, and people said he could recite the whole Psalter from memory in Greek and Hebrew and Syriac. He held a letter too. His hand was shaking.

"This is addressed to Lord Tobias of Selymbria," he said. "In it I promise him the lands of the monastery of Saint Mamas in the Thracian hills if he will send his men to guard the Great Church against the Eparch's watch. In it I call the Eparch a woman of the Goldhand's party. I say that she means to sell the City to the islands." He lowered the letter. "It is in my hand. It is sealed with my seal. I did not write it."

"Then who did?" someone shouted from the east wall.

"If we knew that, my lord, we would not be standing here."

The hall broke into noise. Men were on their feet on every bench, shouting across the floor. Ioannes the silk merchant was pointing at the Eparch. A deacon was pointing at Ioannes. Someone near the door was shouting that the Patriarch had always been soft on the monks and the monks had always been soft on the Tower. Stephanos felt his hand move toward the hilt of his sword, and he closed it on his helm instead.

The Domestikos stood up.

Nikephoros Skleros did not shout. He did not need to. He was a big man, broad as a door, with close-cropped grey hair and a face cut with old scars like a map of the eastern campaigns. He had commanded the Tagma of the Horn in the field for fifteen years under the Abba, and before that he had been a strategos of the old Anatolikon theme, when there had still been an Anatolikon theme. When he stood, the hall went quiet around him, one bench at a time.

"My lords," he said. "Lady Eparch. Holy Father. We have been here before."

He walked out onto the floor of the hall, between the benches, and turned slowly to look at each faction in turn.

"Two years ago, it was a letter from the Captain of the Watch to the Gibborim of the Strymon, offering to leave the Golden Gate unbarred. Before that it was a letter from the Patriarch's predecessor to Nebrodes himself. Before that, a letter from my own hand to the Goldhand, selling him my Hornbearers as mercenaries for his fleet." He smiled, without warmth. "I remember that one. It cost me three good knights, who believed it and left the order in disgust. We never found out who wrote any of them. We only found out that they were false, after they had done their work."

"So what do you propose, Domestikos?" said the Eparch.

"I propose that we stop looking for the pen, Lady, and look at the hand that holds it." Nikephoros spread his arms. "Why do these letters work? Because this City has no head. The Eparch runs the grain, the Patriarch runs the church, the guilds run the markets, the lords run their lands, and every one of them suspects every other of reaching for more. We are forty men and women in a room with no roof, arguing about who will cut the bread, while the Tower sharpens its knives outside the walls. A forged letter works on a body with no head. It cannot work on a body that has one."

"You mean a crown," said the Patriarch.

"I mean an emperor, Holy Father. Anointed in the Great Church, before God and the people. I mean a man the lords would kneel to and the guilds would pay taxes to and the Hornbearers would ride behind. I mean an end to this." He gestured at the forged letters, at the shouting benches, at the sailcloth roof. "And you all know the man I mean."

Every head in the hall turned toward the east wall.

Lord Bardas Argyros rose from his bench.

Stephanos had seen him before, at a distance, in processions and at the races in the half-ruined Hippodrome. He had never stood this close. Bardas was not quite thirty. He was tall, taller than the Domestikos, and he moved with the easy balance of a man who had ridden and fought since boyhood. His hair was the colour of ripe wheat, and he wore it long in the old style, and his face was open and handsome and a little sunburned. He wore no jewels. His cloak was plain blue wool, and his sword belt was plain leather, and around his neck on a cord hung a small wooden cross of the kind the country priests gave to children.

"My lords," he said. "You know I did not ask for this."

His voice was warm and low. Stephanos found himself leaning forward to hear it.

"I am a soldier. I have fought Anak's raiders on the Sangarios and the Strymon, and I have buried too many of my own men, and I have come home to this City to find her quarrelling over letters while her walls fall into the ditch." Bardas looked around the hall. "If God has put the Shepherd's blood in my veins, I did not put it there. I would give it back if I could. But the Grammatikoi have read the old registers, and they say it is there, and the Domestikos says the Tagma will stand behind me if I stand up. So I will stand up. I will not seize anything. I will not buy anything. I will ask the Patriarch to anoint me, if he judges it right, and I will serve this City until God takes the crown back and gives it to someone better."

For a moment the hall was entirely silent. Then someone near the door began to clap, and someone else took it up, and in a few breaths half the benches were on their feet, clapping and calling Bardas's name.

The Patriarch did not clap. Neither did the Eparch.

"My lord," said the Patriarch, when the noise had died a little. "The Horn is not in the Great Church. It has not been in the Great Church for a hundred years. With what oil would you have me anoint you?"

"With the oil of the Great Church, Holy Father," said Bardas. "The same oil that anoints the sick and the dying and the newly baptised. Is it not holy?"

"It is holy," said the Patriarch. "It is not the Horn."

"Then we will pray that God blesses it as if it were."

"God is not a merchant, my lord, to take one coin when another was promised."

"Then what would you have us do?" Bardas's voice was still gentle, but there was something harder under it now. "Wait another hundred years? The Tower will not wait, Holy Father. My scouts in the east say Nebrodes has sent to the giant kings of Armenia and the Caucasus for tribute and troops. My scouts say the Concord is buying grain in Egypt, more than it could need unless it means to feed an army on the march. We have a year. Perhaps less. Do we sit in the roofless church and ask the Question while the Gibborim climb the walls?"

He looked, as he said it, directly at Stephanos. His eyes were blue and very steady.

Stephanos felt his face grow hot. He did not look away.

"The Tagma will do as the Abba commands," he heard himself say.

"The Abba commands nothing," said the Domestikos over his shoulder, not unkindly. "The Abba asks a question and waits. That is his gift. It is not a policy." He turned back to the hall. "I ask the council to consider Lord Bardas's anointing at the Feast of the Cross. We need not decide today. But we must decide."

---

Afterward, in the portico of the Magnaura, with the council breaking up into knots of angry men behind them and the pigeons circling overhead, Bardas found Stephanos by the column where he had gone to rest his knee.

"You're the Hornbearer who killed the giant at Charisios," he said. "They were talking about it at the Blachernae gate this morning. Stephanos, isn't it? Of Taron?"

"Of the Horn, my lord. I was of Taron once."

"I served with a man from Taron on the Sangarios. Vahan, son of Aram. Do you know him?"

"I knew him. He was my cousin. He died at Dorylaion."

"I'm sorry." Bardas said it without ornament, and Stephanos believed him. The tall lord leaned against the next column and looked out over the ruined palace gardens, where the remnant grew cabbages in the old fountains. "Your Abba doesn't like me."

"The Abba likes everyone, my lord."

"Then he doesn't trust me." Bardas smiled. "It's all right. I wouldn't trust me either, in his place. A man stands up in council and says he'll take a crown if people insist on it; that's what every usurper in the chronicles has said, from Phokas on. But I mean it, Stephanos. I don't want the throne. I want the walls to stand."

"So do I, my lord."

"I know. I watched your face when Tobias's name came up." Bardas glanced at him. "You'd like to ride out to Selymbria and burn his hall over his head."

Stephanos said nothing.

"So would I. And if I were crowned, I could. Not burn it. Summon him. Put him on trial before the council for treason and hang him from his own gate if the council finds him guilty. That's the difference a crown makes. It makes justice possible again." He pushed off the column. "Think about it. That's all I ask. The Domestikos says you're the best lance in the Tower. I'd be glad of you."

He walked away down the portico. People turned to watch him as he went. A woman with a basket of eggs stopped and curtsied to him, and he stopped and asked her name, and she went pink to the ears.

Stephanos watched him go, and felt something in his chest that he did not have a name for. It was not envy. It was nearer to hunger.

---

He was in the armoury of the Fool's Tower that night, oiling the rings of his mail by lamplight, when the knocking began at the postern gate below.

It was well past the midnight office. The Tower was dark. The brothers were asleep in their cells, all but the two on watch at the top of the wall and Kale, who had taken the guest cell by the armoury and who did not seem to sleep at all. She was sitting on the armoury bench with her bow across her knees, restringing it, and she lifted her head at the knocking the way a hound lifts its head at a sound in the woods.

"Someone's in trouble," she said.

"How do you know?"

She shrugged. "I know."

The knocking came again, frantic, a fist beating on oak. Then a voice, high and cracked: "Please! In Christ's name! Please!"

Stephanos took up his sword and a lamp and went down the stair with Kale behind him. The postern opened onto a narrow lane that ran along the inside of the wall between the Tower and the old cisterns of Aetios. He drew back the bolt and opened the door a hand's breadth.

A young man fell through it and landed on his hands and knees on the flagstones.

He was thin, pale, perhaps twenty-eight, with ink stains on his fingers and a satchel clutched against his chest. His grey scholar's robe was torn at the shoulder and wet with blood. He looked up at Stephanos with wild eyes, and then past him at the dark stair, and then back over his own shoulder at the lane.

"Shut the door," he gasped. "Shut it. Please."

Stephanos looked into the lane. Three figures stood at the far end of it in the moonlight, in dark cloaks, with the dull gleam of knives in their hands. They had stopped when the door opened. As he watched, a fourth figure came around the corner behind them and stopped too.

"Hornbearer," one of them called. Its voice was pleasant, educated, with the smooth vowels of the Concord schools. "That man is a thief. He has stolen documents from our Chamber. We have a writ for his arrest from the Eparch's office."

"Show me," said Stephanos.

The figure hesitated. "It's in my lodgings."

"Then fetch it, and come back in the morning, and knock at the front gate like a Christian."

He shut the door and shot the bolt. Something struck it from the other side, hard, and then again. Then nothing.

The young man was still on his knees on the floor, shaking. Kale crouched beside him and pulled his torn robe back from his shoulder. The cut was long and shallow, a knife slash that had skidded off the bone.

"He'll live," she said. "He'll need stitching."

"Who are you?" Stephanos asked.

The young man looked up at him. His face was very white under the ink smudges, and his mouth kept trying to smile, as though that were a habit it could not break.

"My name is Theophanes," he said. "Until tonight I was a reader in the Chamber of Scribes of the Concord of the Tower. I studied under Master Kallinikos for eleven years. I copied the chronicles. I kept the registers. And for the last two years, Hornbearer, I have been forging letters for the council of this City." He held up his inky hands. "I wrote the Patriarch's letter to Lord Tobias. With these. I wrote it at the Master's dictation, and I sealed it with a copy of the Patriarch's seal that I cut myself from a wax impression. I'm very good at it. I'm the best they have."

Stephanos stared at him. He felt the heat come up in his chest like a tide.

"Then why," he said, "are you on my floor instead of in the Tower's bed?"

"Because of this." Theophanes fumbled open the satchel with shaking fingers and drew out a single sheet of parchment, folded in three. "The Master gave me a new one to write this morning. In the Eparch's hand, to the captain of the Golden Gate. Telling him to admit a party of the Patriarch's men after dark on the Feast of the Cross, and to ask no questions about their weapons." He held it out. "They mean to kill the Patriarch on the feast day, Hornbearer, in the Great Church, and blame the Eparch for it. And then they mean to let the city tear itself apart, and they will stand outside the walls and offer to restore order."

"And you didn't want to write it."

"I didn't mind writing the others." Theophanes laughed, a thin, cracked sound. "Letters. Seals. Quarrels between old men. I told myself nobody would die of it. I told myself the Concord was right, that the City would be better governed from the Tower than by forty fools in a hall with no roof. I believed it. I still believe most of it." He looked down at the letter in his hand. "But I read the Patriarch's sermons, when I was forging his hand. I had to, to get the turns of phrase right. And I found I didn't want him dead. So I took the letter and ran."

Kale had gone to the armoury and come back with a jar of wine and a bandage. She poured the wine over Theophanes's shoulder without warning, and he yelped and swore in three languages.

"Where do they write them?" Stephanos asked. "These letters. Where does Kallinikos keep his workshop?"

Theophanes looked up at him through tears of pain. The habitual smile was gone now.

"Under your feet," he said. "Under all of you. There's a stair behind the Milion, in a crack in the oldest street in the City, that goes down into the Undercroft: the crypts of the emperors beneath the Great Church. Below the crypts are the Drowned Cisterns. The Master has a scriptorium down there, on a stone island in the dark, where the Eparch's watch never comes. He has eleven readers and four copyists and a Nephilim seer who comes and goes. And he has every letter that's passed through the City's post in the last two years, opened and copied and resealed." He swallowed. "If you want the pen, Hornbearer, it's down there. But you won't like what's waiting on the way."

"What's waiting?"

"The dead," said Theophanes. "The emperors of the Shepherd's house. Some of them don't sleep well."

Stephanos looked at Kale. She had finished binding the young man's shoulder and was sitting back on her heels, watching him with her dark, unreadable eyes.

"Well," she said. "I came to the City to warn the Eparch about giants. I suppose the dead are close enough."
