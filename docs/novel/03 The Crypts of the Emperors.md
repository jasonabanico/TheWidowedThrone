## Chapter 3: The Crypts of the Emperors

*Lord, now you let your servant go in peace.*

*The Song of Symeon*

---

The Great Church had no roof, and so the rain came in.

It was raining that morning, a thin grey drizzle off the Marmara, and it fell through the vast round hole where the dome had been and drifted down through the nave like smoke. The marble floor was dark with it. Puddles lay in the hollows worn by nine hundred years of feet. At the east end, where the apse still stood with its half-dome intact, the clergy had rigged a canopy of oiled canvas over the altar, and under it, in the dry, the Patriarch had lit seven candles, and he stood among them with the letter Theophanes had brought in his hands.

He read it twice. Then he folded it and gave it back.

"On the feast day," he said. "In front of the people."

"Yes, Holy Father," said Theophanes.

"And you wrote the other letter. The one to Tobias. In my hand."

Theophanes had not slept. The stitches in his shoulder showed dark through the clean shirt Kale had found for him, and his face was grey. He stood in front of the Patriarch the way a boy stands in front of a schoolmaster with a broken slate behind his back.

"Yes, Holy Father."

"You made my letters very well. You got the way I shape my kappa. Almost nobody notices that." The Patriarch looked at him a long moment. "Have you confessed it?"

"I don't... Holy Father, I haven't been to confession since I was fourteen."

"Then it is past time." The old man turned to the Eparch, who stood at the edge of the canopy with her arms folded, watching the rain. "Irene. What will you do?"

"What I always do," said the Eparch. "Too little, too late, with not enough men." She turned. Her face was hard. "I can't send the watch down there. I have two hundred men to hold fourteen miles of wall and keep the markets from rioting, and half of them are boys. If I take fifty to go crawling through crypts, the hill lords will know it by tonight." She looked at Stephanos. "The Domestikos says you found this one. He says the Tagma will see it through."

"The Domestikos said that?"

"He said it this morning, at the Magnaura, loudly, in front of Ioannes the silk merchant and half the guild masters." The Eparch's mouth twisted. "He said the Hornbearers would root out the forgers while the council dithered, and that it would show the City what a strong hand could do. He did not say whose strong hand he meant. He didn't need to."

Stephanos said nothing. He understood the Domestikos. If the Tagma broke the forgers, the Sword faction would carry it to the council as proof, and Bardas would be anointed on the Feast of the Cross with the forgers' heads on the Milion. It was a good plan. It was the plan he would have made himself.

"How many brothers can I take?" he asked.

"None," said a voice behind him.

He turned. A woman was standing in the nave in the rain, a little outside the edge of the canopy, as if she had not noticed that she was getting wet. She was old, sixty or more, small and brown and bent, with a face as lined as dried mud. She wore the black habit of the desert, belted with rope, and a black cloth over her grey hair, and she held in her hands a half-finished basket of palm leaf, which her fingers went on weaving as she spoke. Beside her stood Kosmas the physician, looking faintly apologetic.

"None," the old woman said again. "The brothers of your Tower must stay on the wall. You will take the ones God has already sent you."

"Amma," said the Patriarch, and came out from under the canopy into the rain to kiss her hand, which surprised Stephanos more than anything else that morning. "When did you come?"

"Last night, Father. On a grain ship from Alexandria." Her eyes, black and bright as a bird's, moved from the Patriarch to Stephanos, and stayed there. "I had a word to deliver. It is for this one."

"For me?"

"For you." She did not stop weaving. "There is a letter in the dark with your name on it. You will go down and get it. That is all."

Stephanos looked at Kosmas. The physician spread his hands. "She came to the hospice last night and asked for the Hornbearer who hurt his knee," he said. "I didn't tell her about your knee. She knew. This is Amma Thekla, of the laura of Saint Chariton. She has been in the desert forty years."

"Forty-two," said Amma Thekla. "Kosmas can't count."

---

So in the end there were six of them.

Stephanos had not chosen them. He noticed that at the top of the stair behind the Milion, in the grey noon light, with the rain dripping off the edge of the broken paving above them into the dark. He had not chosen any of them.

Kale had come because she had come. She did not explain it, and he did not ask. She had her bow and forty arrows and a long knife, and she had wrapped rags around her boots to muffle them.

Theophanes had come because he was the only one who knew the way. He had argued against it for an hour at the Great Church, in a rising voice, with his hands flying. He was not a soldier, he said. He could not fight. The Master's men would recognise him. He would be a burden. The Patriarch had listened to all of it and said at the end, gently: "My son, you have spent two years taking this City apart with a pen. Go and help put it back together with your feet." Theophanes had shut his mouth and gone pale and nodded.

Amma Thekla had come because she said so. She had tucked her basket into the rope at her waist and taken a lamp from the Patriarch and said nothing else.

Sergios had come because nobody could stop him. He had been sitting on the steps of the Milion when they arrived, eating a raw onion and singing a psalm to a cat, and when Stephanos lit the first lamp at the top of the stair, the fool had jumped up and scampered down ahead of them into the dark, and they had heard him laughing in the crypts below.

And the sixth was Lazaros.

He had been waiting for them at the Milion with the fool. He was young, twenty-four or five, slight, with a gentle, bony face and soft brown hair that fell into his eyes. He wore the grey smock of a painter's workshop, stained with pigment, and on his back in a leather case he carried a painted board as long as his arm. The Patriarch had sent him. He had spent the last month in the sanctuary of the Great Church, he said, renewing the icon of the Archistrategos Michael that sealed the crack in the floor, and he knew the crypts because the crypts ran beneath the sanctuary, and he had heard things through the floor at night.

"What kind of things?" Stephanos had asked.

"Singing," said Lazaros. "Backward."

His hands were scarred. Stephanos had noticed it at once. Both palms and the insides of both fingers were ridged and shiny with old burns, the skin pulled tight, as though he had held them in a fire. He moved them carefully, as a man moves something he has had to learn to use again.

He did not explain the scars either.

---

The stair went down a long way.

It had been a service stair once, for the clergy who tended the imperial tombs. The steps were worn into hollows and slick with rain that had run down from the street. The walls were brick, then stone, then living rock, cold and sweating. The lamps made small, frightened circles of yellow on the steps, and beyond them the dark pressed in close on every side.

At the bottom was a door of bronze, green with age, standing open. Beyond it was the first crypt.

Stephanos had heard of the Crypts of the Emperors all his life, but he had never seen them. Hardly anyone living had. The remnant had come home to find the Great Church burned and the crypts beneath it sealed with rubble, and there had been more urgent work than clearing a way into a tomb. He stood in the doorway now with his lamp raised and felt the hair on his arms stand up.

It was a long, low hall, vaulted in brick, and it ran away into the dark further than his light could reach. Down both sides, in arched niches, stood the tombs of the emperors of the Shepherd's house. They were made of porphyry, the imperial purple stone, carved from single blocks as big as a farm cart, each with a heavy lid shaped like the roof of a house. The lamplight made the stone glow a deep, dull red, like coals under ash. Names were carved on the lids in tall square letters, and under the names, the years of their reigns, and under the years, a line from the Psalms.

*Poimen, son of Iesse the herdsman. Forty years. The Lord is my shepherd.*

That one stood alone at the head of the hall, on a low step, with nothing in the niche around it but a painted cross on the wall above. The lid was plain. Somebody had laid a bunch of dried wildflowers on it, long ago, and the flowers had crumbled to brown dust that still held their shape.

"He's here?" Kale asked quietly. "The Shepherd himself?"

"Supposedly," said Theophanes. His voice echoed in the vault, and he lowered it. "The Tower's chronicles say he never existed. They say this is the tomb of a Thracian warlord called Pomenos who took the name of a shepherd to win the peasants. Master Kallinikos wrote that chapter himself." He hesitated. "He was very proud of it."

"And what do you think?"

"I think the Master is very clever and has never been wrong about anything he could look up in a book." Theophanes looked at the tomb and did not go closer to it. "I don't know what I think."

They went on down the hall. The tombs of Poimen's heirs stood in their niches, one after another. The first two or three were plain like the Shepherd's. After that the carving grew richer: vines and eagles and hunting scenes, lions and stags, gold leaf pressed into the letters. The lines from the Psalms grew shorter and the lists of titles grew longer. By the fifth tomb, the psalm had disappeared entirely, and in its place was a long inscription in verse about the emperor's victories over the Persians.

Amma Thekla stopped in front of the sixth tomb and looked at it for a long time, weaving.

"This one is not asleep," she said.

"How can you tell?" asked Lazaros.

"How can you tell a man is hungry?" The old woman tilted her head. "Listen."

They listened. For a while there was nothing but the drip of water somewhere far off in the dark, and the hiss of the lamps. Then Stephanos heard it. A scraping, very faint, like a fingernail drawn slowly across stone. It came from inside the tomb.

He put his lamp down on the floor and drew his sword.

"No," said Thekla. "Not yet."

The scraping stopped.

The lid of the tomb moved.

It did not slide or lift. It turned, grinding on its stone bed, a hand's breadth, then another. A smell came out of the crack, cold and dry and sweetish, like old incense and dust. Something pale showed in the gap. Fingers.

"Amma," said Stephanos.

"Wait."

The lid turned further, and fell. It struck the floor of the crypt with a crash that shook dust from the vault, and broke in two. Out of the tomb, slowly, in a long rustle of rotten silk, rose the sixth emperor of the Shepherd's house.

He had been dead two hundred years, and he looked it. His face was a brown skull with skin stretched over it like parchment. His hair and beard were long and white and matted, and on his head, sunk down over his brow, he wore a crown of gold set with pearls. His burial robes had once been purple and gold; now they hung from him in tatters. He held a sceptre in one hand, and in the other, a sword, and his eyes were two holes in his face full of a dim green light, like the light of rotten wood in a marsh.

He looked at them, and opened his mouth. What came out was not a voice. It was a hiss, dry as sand, full of words.

"*Mine,*" said the dead emperor. "*Mine. My throne. My city. My crown. Mine.*"

He stepped out of the tomb and came at Stephanos with the sword.

Stephanos met him. The dead man's blade was old and pitted, but the arm behind it was strong, stronger than any living man's, and the first blow drove Stephanos back two steps and numbed his wrist. He parried the second and struck back, and his sword cut through rotten silk and dry flesh and grated on bone, and the dead emperor did not seem to notice. He swung again. Stephanos ducked, and the blade struck sparks from the porphyry behind his head.

"Stephanos!" Kale shouted. She had an arrow on the string, but the two of them were too close together for her to shoot.

"Don't!" said Thekla. "He is not the enemy. The thing that woke him is."

"Amma, he's trying to kill me!"

"Yes. So would you, if they had done to you what they did to him." The old woman put down her lamp. "Lazaros. The icon. Now."

The painter had already swung the leather case off his back. He pulled out the board inside it with his scarred hands, and held it up.

Stephanos saw it over the dead man's shoulder: the Archistrategos Michael, the commander of the heavenly host, in gold and scarlet on a field of deep blue, with a sword in one hand and a globe in the other and his great calm eyes looking straight out of the wood. It was new, the paint still bright. In the lamplight it seemed to glow.

The dead emperor turned his head toward it. He stopped.

"*Mine,*" he hissed, but the word came out uncertain, like a question.

"No," said Amma Thekla. She had come forward between Stephanos and the emperor, a small bent old woman with her hands empty. "No, my son. It was never yours. It was lent to you, and you forgot, and it was taken back. That is all. Now it is time to sleep."

She began to pray. Stephanos knew the words. Every Christian in the empire knew them; they were the words the priests said at the graveside when the body went into the ground. *With the spirits of the righteous made perfect, give rest, O Saviour, to the soul of your servant.* The old woman did not raise her voice. She said the prayer the way a mother says a lullaby, over and over, and on the third time Lazaros joined her, and on the fourth, to Stephanos's surprise, Theophanes did, stumbling over the words, as if he were remembering them from a long way off.

The dead emperor stood still in front of the icon. The green light in his eyes flickered.

Then something else spoke through his mouth.

It was not the dry hiss of the dead man. It was a voice like a knife drawn across a slate, high and sharp and full of contempt, and it spoke Greek with the smooth vowels of the Concord schools.

"*Burial rites,*" it said. "*How quaint. Do you think a prayer will unbind him? I bound him with his own pride. He wanted to be remembered, and I have remembered him. What will you give him instead? Rest? He never wanted rest. He wanted to rule. Let him rule. Let him rule you.*"

The dead emperor's sword came up again.

"There," said Kale, very quietly. She was looking past the emperor, into the dark at the end of the crypt. "There's someone there. Behind the eighth tomb. Holding a candle and a string."

Stephanos could see nothing but the dark. But Kale's arrow was already gone.

It flew past the dead emperor's shoulder and past the seventh tomb and into the black beyond the lamplight, and there was a short, high scream and the sound of something falling. A small flame dropped to the floor in the dark and went out.

The dead emperor stopped. The green light in his eyes went out like a pinched wick. He stood swaying for a moment, a dry husk in rotten silk. Then he folded down onto the floor of the crypt, very slowly, into a heap of bones and cloth and dust, and the gold crown rolled off his skull and rang on the stone and lay still.

Amma Thekla finished the prayer. Then she knelt and picked up the crown and put it back on the skull, carefully, straightening it.

"He was a vain man," she said. "But he was baptised. Somebody should bury him again. Not now." She stood up. "Now we should see what the young woman shot."

---

It was a man in a grey robe like Theophanes's, lying on his back behind the eighth tomb with Kale's arrow through his throat. He was dead. Beside him on the floor was a guttered candle and a long thread of red silk, one end knotted around his wrist and the other running across the floor to the sixth tomb, where it went in under the broken lid.

Theophanes knelt beside him and turned the dead man's face to the light. Then he stood up quickly and went a little way off into the dark and was sick.

"You knew him," said Stephanos.

"Georgios. He was a reader in the second year when I was in the fifth. He was good at Aramaic." Theophanes wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. His voice shook. "He wasn't a seer. He can't have done that himself. It must have been the seer, the one who comes and goes. He must have set the binding and left Georgios to hold the thread." He looked at Kale. "How did you know he was there?"

"I knew," Kale said. She had come back from the body with her arrow, cleaned it on the dead man's robe, and put it back in her quiver. Her face was calm, but her hands were not quite steady. "It happens sometimes. I see a thing in my head before I see it with my eyes. My grandmother had it. She called it the word of knowledge. I call it a nuisance." She looked at Theophanes. "I'm sorry about your friend."

"He wasn't my friend," said Theophanes. "He was just someone I knew."

Lazaros had put the icon back in its case. He was looking at the red thread on the floor. "This was Blight-work," he said. "The binding. I've seen it before, in the Gardens. They take something the dead loved and tie it to them. A name, a crown, a grudge. It holds them to the world." His scarred hands tightened on the strap of the case. "It's an ugly thing."

"Are there more?" Stephanos asked. "Bound ones?"

"Probably," said Amma Thekla. "Lazaros will know."

The painter walked slowly back up the crypt, past each tomb in turn, with his lamp held high. At the seventh he stopped and put his palm flat on the porphyry lid for a moment, and took it away. He did the same at the ninth, and the eleventh. At each of them his face grew a little paler.

"Those three," he said. "Those three are bound. They're not awake yet. They will be, when the seer comes back and pulls the threads."

"Can you unbind them?"

"Not without breaking the lids." Lazaros hesitated. "The Amma could. With the rite. It would take a day, perhaps two."

"We don't have two days." Stephanos looked at the old woman. "Amma?"

"The dead will keep a little longer," Thekla said. "The living will not. Go on down. I'll pray here on the way back."

He nodded. Then, because he had seen something at the far end of the crypt, beyond the eighth tomb where Georgios lay dead, he picked up his lamp and walked on into the dark.

There was one more tomb there, in the last niche, larger than any of the others.

It was porphyry too, but it had been carved by a different hand, and a prouder one. The whole of the lid was a single figure lying on its back: an emperor in armour, with a sword on his breast and his hands folded over the hilt and a calm face turned toward the vault. Around the base of the tomb ran a long inscription in gold, and Stephanos did not have to read it to know whose it was. The face was the same face the bronze statue had worn, in all the pictures of it in all the chronicles of the Fall.

*Andronikos, Emperor and Autocrator of the Romans, Keeper of the People.*

The lid had been pushed aside. The tomb was empty.

Not robbed. There was no rubble, no scattered bones, no smashed grave-goods. The inside was clean and dry and bare, the stone floor of it smooth, as if no body had ever lain there. Andronikos had died in the fall of the City, somewhere in the smoke and the killing, and they had never found his corpse. His court had built this tomb for him ahead of time, while he still lived, the way emperors did. He had never come to fill it.

But someone had used it.

In the bottom of the empty tomb, neatly stacked, were bundles of letters. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Each bundle was tied with red silk thread. Each had a label in a small, precise hand. Stephanos lifted the nearest one out and held it to the lamp. *Eparch's office, Easter to Pentecost. Copied, resealed, returned.* He took another. *Patriarch, private. Copied. Forgery in preparation.*

"This is the post room," said Theophanes, who had come up behind him. His voice was flat. "The Master keeps the originals here, after they've been copied. It's safer than the scriptorium. Nobody would come here." He laughed, the thin cracked laugh again. "He said once that it was a fitting place to keep the secrets of a proud city. In a proud man's grave."

Stephanos said nothing. He was looking at the stone face on the lid of the tomb. It was calm and noble and a little sad, the face of a man who has done his duty and expects to be thanked for it. He thought of Lord Bardas, standing in the Magnaura with the wooden cross around his neck, saying: *I don't want the throne. I want the walls to stand.*

He shook the thought off. It was not the same thing. It was nothing like the same thing.

"We'll take them," he said. "All of them. The Eparch will want to see."

"There are hundreds."

"Then we'll carry hundreds." He handed a bundle to Theophanes. "Where's the way down?"

Theophanes pointed. Behind the empty tomb, where the vault of the crypt met the living rock, there was a low arch, and beyond it a stair going down. From below, very faintly, came the sound of water moving, and something else under it, a sound that went on and on without a pause for breath.

Voices, chanting. Stephanos listened for a moment before he understood why the words sounded wrong.

They were the words of the liturgy. And they were being sung backward.
