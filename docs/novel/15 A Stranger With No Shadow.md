# Part Three: The Tower

## Chapter 15: A Stranger With No Shadow

*Satan himself masquerades as an angel of light.*

*Paul, to the church at Corinth*

---

They argued about it for three days.

Stephanos had not expected an argument. He had expected to read the Domestikos's letter aloud in the refectory of Saint George of the Rock, and for everyone to understand, as he did, that it changed nothing. The fourth word was in the Wilderness. The Wilderness was south, through the Cilician Gates and down into Syria, and the City was west. Nebrodes would not reach the walls before midsummer. There was time to go south first, and find the last word, and come home with all four. It was obvious.

Petros did not think it was obvious.

"He's asked for me," he said. "The Emperor. By name. He wants to meet me." He was sitting up on a cot in the monastery infirmary with his ribs bound and his face still swollen purple and green on one side, where Anak's maul had struck it. "Stephanos, the Emperor wants to meet me. Do you know what that means?"

"It means Bardas has heard there's another heir of the Shepherd's blood walking around the empire, and he wants to see if you're a threat."

"Or it means he wants me beside him when Nebrodes comes." Petros leaned forward, winced, and leaned back. "I'm the Shepherd's heir. You said so. You told Hagop. You told the whole valley. And I went up that mountain and lost." His voice cracked. "A shepherd boy with a sling did more than I did. Sergios did more than I did. You killed him on your knees with a broken arm. I lay by a pillar and bled."

"You were beaten by a giant who'd been eating your boasts for a month."

"I know!" Petros shouted. Then he put his hand to his ribs and closed his eyes, and when he spoke again his voice was low. "I know. The Amma told me. I know what I did. That's why I have to go. I have to do something. Something real. Not in a cave. In front of people. With a sword, against the Hunter. I have to show them the Shepherd's heir isn't a braggart who lies by a pillar." He opened his eyes. "Please. Let me go to the City."

Stephanos looked at him.

He wanted to say yes. He saw it clearly: Petros at the Emperor's side, in armour, on a warhorse, riding out under the purple against the Hunter's host. Petros proving himself. Petros redeemed. The Tagma would see him. The City would see him. And when the fourth word was found, and the Horn came up out of wherever it had gone down, it would find him already standing in the right place.

"We go south first," he said. "All of us. The fourth word. Then we go home together."

Petros turned his face to the wall.

---

Amma Thekla did not argue at all. She only said, the first night, that the Wilderness was her country and she would go there whether anyone came with her or not, and that the laura of Saint Chariton had not had an elder in it for two years, and the monks were leaving one by one. "Something visits the cells at noon," she said. "I had a letter from one of them in the autumn. It was the letter that brought me north. I thought God meant me to go to the City and deliver a word to a Hornbearer first." She looked at Stephanos. "I've delivered it. Now I'm going home."

Kale said she would go with the Amma. Lazaros said the same. Sergios said nothing; he was lying on the floor of the refectory with a cat on his chest, and when Stephanos asked him, he said, "South is where the sun is," and went on stroking the cat.

Theophanes said the least of anyone. He sat at the end of the table each night with the purple book open in front of him, and listened, and did not speak until the third night, when Stephanos asked him outright.

"The Words are the important thing," Theophanes said slowly. "Three of four. We can't read them properly without the fourth. Whatever they mean. However you want to read them." He did not look up from the book. "I'll go south."

And Dora said: "I'm going wherever Petros goes."

"Petros is going south," said Stephanos.

"Is he?" said Dora. She looked at him with her dark eyes under the veil, and then she got up and went out.

---

Hagop gave them horses and mules and provisions, and Father Basil gave them his blessing, and the whole valley came out to see them off at dawn on the fourth day. Mikros walked beside Dora's horse all the way to the mouth of the valley. At the last chimney, where the road bent south toward the mountains, he stopped.

"Will you come back?" he asked her.

"I don't know," said Dora.

"I'll practise," said Mikros. "Every day. The way you showed me."

"Ring finger," said Dora.

"Ring finger," said Mikros. He stood at the side of the road with his sling in his hand and watched them go, and when Stephanos looked back from the top of the first rise, the boy was still standing there, very small against the rock towers, not waving.

---

The road south went up into the Taurus and through the Cilician Gates.

Stephanos had been through the Gates before. Every soldier of the east had. It was the great pass through the mountains between Cappadocia and the coast, a narrow gorge a dozen miles long, with cliffs rising on either side so high and so close that at noon the sun reached the bottom for only an hour. The road ran along the floor of it beside a white, roaring river. Armies had marched through it for two thousand years. Alexander. The Persians. The Arabs. The Shepherd.

"This is where," said Theophanes. He had stopped his horse in the middle of the gorge, at a place where the cliffs opened out a little and there was a flat stretch of ground beside the river, grassy, strewn with boulders. He had the purple book open on his saddle. "According to the older chronicle, this is where. *At the narrowest part of the Gates, where the river bends and the cliff on the eastern side is split like a loaf.*" He looked up at the eastern cliff. There was a great crack running down it from the top to the bottom, as if some giant hand had pulled it apart. "There. Gathos stood across the road here with his host behind him, and dared the Romans to send a champion. And the Romans had no champion. The Emperor of those days was hiding in Tarsus. And a shepherd boy who had come up from the hills with bread for his brothers in the army said he would go."

He was quiet a moment.

"The chronicle says the boy's brothers were angry with him," he went on. "The eldest said, *Why have you come down here? And with whom have you left those few sheep in the wilderness? I know your pride and the evil of your heart.* Almost the same words as the Scripture. The chronicler must have known it." Theophanes closed the book. "The Tower's chronicle says it never happened. Master Kallinikos said the shepherd was a peasant legend, and Gathos died of fever in his tent."

"And what do you think?" said Kale. "Now?"

Theophanes looked at the cracked cliff for a long time.

"I think I spent eleven years copying a book that was lying to me," he said. "And I think the man who wrote this one," he touched the purple binding, "didn't care whether anyone believed him. He only wrote down what he saw."

Dora had got down from her horse. She was standing on the flat ground by the river with the sling of Poimen around her wrist, looking up at the cracked cliff. The river roared beside her. After a while she unwound the sling from her wrist and put the loop over her finger. She bent and picked up a stone from the river's edge, a smooth one, round and grey.

She did not throw it. She stood there with it in the pouch, and the sling hanging from her hand, looking at the place where the giant had stood.

Then she wound the sling back around her wrist and put the stone in her pocket and got back on her horse.

"Why didn't you throw it?" Stephanos asked her, later, when they had ridden on.

"It didn't want to be thrown," said Dora. "Not there. Not yet."

He did not know what she meant. He did not ask.

---

They came down out of the Gates onto the coastal plain of Cilicia at the end of the week, and rode into Tarsus.

It had been a great city once. It was the city of Paul the Apostle, the tentmaker, and the old walls still stood, and the old church where Paul had preached still stood inside them, with its roof on. But half the houses were empty, and the harbour was silting up, and the people had the watchful faces of people who live on a frontier. They put up at an inn by the river, a big old courtyard inn with galleries around three sides and a stable on the fourth, full of merchants and pilgrims and soldiers on their way somewhere else.

Petros was quiet at supper. He had been quiet for a week, since Göreme. His bruises were fading. His ribs were mending. He ate little, and did not drink, and did not look at anyone.

After supper he went up to the gallery alone and sat on the rail, looking out at the river.

Stephanos saw the stranger come up the gallery stair.

He noticed him because he was tall. Not giant-tall; only taller than the other men in the inn, a head taller, and lean, and he moved with a kind of grace that made people turn to watch him. He wore a plain white travelling cloak with the hood back. His hair was pale gold, almost white, and his face was beautiful. That was the only word for it. It was a face from an icon, calm and clear and full of light, as if there were a lamp burning behind the skin. People in the courtyard below stopped talking when he passed. A serving-girl carrying a jug of wine stopped dead on the stair and stared at him with her mouth open.

He went along the gallery to where Petros sat on the rail, and stopped, and spoke to him.

Stephanos could not hear what he said. He was at the far side of the courtyard, by the well, with Kale and Lazaros. He saw Petros turn his head. He saw Petros's face change. He saw the stranger put a hand on Petros's shoulder and speak again, and Petros's face lit up, slowly, like a room when a lamp is carried into it.

"Who's that?" said Kale.

"I don't know."

"I don't like him," said Kale. She said it flatly, the way she said everything, but her hand had gone to the knife on her belt. "I don't know why. I can't see anything. That's the strange thing. Usually I can see something." She frowned. "It's like looking at the sun."

The stranger was sitting on the rail beside Petros now. They were talking. The stranger's face was grave and kind, and Petros was listening to him the way the children in Göreme had listened to Petros.

Stephanos began to cross the courtyard. Halfway across, someone caught his sleeve.

It was Dora. She had come out of the kitchen with a lamp in her hand, and her face under the veil was white.

"Stephanos," she said. "Look at him. Look at the lamp."

"What?"

"The lamps on the gallery. Look at the wall behind them."

He looked.

There were lamps burning in brackets along the gallery wall, one every few paces. In their light, every person on the gallery threw a shadow on the whitewashed wall behind them: the merchants, the pilgrims, the serving-girl on the stair, Petros on the rail. Petros's shadow lay across the wall, long and broad, with its elbows on its knees.

Beside Petros's shadow, there was nothing.

The stranger sat on the rail in the full light of the lamp, and the wall behind him was bare white plaster. He had no shadow at all.

Stephanos stood still in the middle of the courtyard.

"He's not an angel," said Dora. Her voice was shaking. "He's pretending. Grandmother said. She said the Adversary can dress up as an angel of light, and you can tell because he has to borrow the light from somewhere, so he doesn't have any of his own to block it. So he doesn't have a shadow." She was clutching Stephanos's sleeve. "Stephanos. He's talking to Petros."

"Dora..."

"Stop him. Please."

He looked up at the gallery.

The stranger had turned his head. He was looking down into the courtyard, at Stephanos. His beautiful face was calm and kind. He smiled. It was the most gentle smile Stephanos had ever seen.

Then he stood up, and bowed to Petros, and went along the gallery and down the far stair and out through the inn gate into the street, and was gone.

---

Petros came down from the gallery a few minutes later.

His face was shining. He came across the courtyard to where they stood by the well, and he took Stephanos by both shoulders, the way Hagop had at Göreme, and his eyes were bright with tears.

"He was an angel," Petros said. "Stephanos. An angel. He came to me. He came here, to this inn, to find me."

"Petros..."

"He knew everything. He knew about the mountain. He knew about the Garden. He knew about Grandmother, and the lamp, and the wolf I fought when I was twelve. He knew things nobody knows." Petros laughed, a sound halfway to a sob. "He said God has chosen me. He said the Horn is waiting for me. He said I was right to want to go to the City. He said the Hunter is coming, and the City needs a champion, and the Emperor Bardas is a good man but he's only a drop of the blood, a cup of water poured in the sea, and God is going to use him to prepare the way. For me." He let go of Stephanos's shoulders. "He said I should go to Bardas. Now. Tonight. He said I should stand at Bardas's side when the Hunter comes, and fight, and when the day is won, the Horn will come up out of the ground and find me there. In front of everyone. In front of the whole City."

"He didn't have a shadow," said Dora.

Petros looked down at her. He smiled. It was a kind smile, an elder brother's smile, the smile of someone humouring a child.

"Dora," he said. "He was an angel."

"He didn't have a shadow! I saw! Look at the wall! Everybody else had a shadow and he didn't!"

"Angels don't have shadows, Dora. They're made of light."

"They do! They do have shadows! Grandmother said! They're made, they're things God made, like us, they're real, so they have shadows! The only one who doesn't is the one who's pretending!" Dora's voice was rising. "Petros, please. Please. He's the one Grandmother warned about. The one who tells you to take the crown. That's what he always does. He always tells people to take the crown."

Petros's smile faded a little.

"You're jealous," he said.

Dora stared at him.

"You've always been jealous," said Petros. He said it gently, as if he were explaining something to her for her own good. "Of Grandmother saying I'd be a great man. Of the girls at the harvest feast. Of me. You were jealous in Thessalonike, when I didn't fast. You were jealous on the mountain, when they chose me for champion. And now an angel comes to me, not to you, and you're jealous of that too." He shook his head. "It's all right. I don't blame you. You're a child. You'll understand when you're older."

Dora did not say anything. Her face had gone very still.

Petros turned to Stephanos.

"I'm going," he said. "Tonight. I'm taking my horse and I'm riding for the City. You can come with me if you like. I hope you will." His voice softened. "You were the first one who believed in me, Stephanos. You saw it at the Mint. You said so, to Hagop, in front of everyone. You're the one who knew." He held out his hand. "Come with me. Let's go and meet the Emperor together."

Stephanos stood by the well in the courtyard of the inn at Tarsus, with the lamps burning on the gallery above and the stranger's empty wall behind them, and he looked at Petros's outstretched hand.

He wanted to take it. God help him, he wanted to take it more than he had wanted the red eagle of Taron on the Goldhand's floor.

He thought of Dora's face. He thought of the white wall.

He thought: *It was dark on the gallery. The lamps were flickering. She's thirteen. She was frightened. She's been frightened since Thessalonike. She wants her brother back the way he was. She's seeing things.*

He thought: *And he knew about the wolf.*

"We go south," he said. His voice sounded far away to him, and hoarse. "To the Wilderness. For the fourth word. That was the plan."

Petros's hand dropped.

"Then I'll see you in the City," he said. He did not sound angry. He sounded sorry for Stephanos, which was worse. "Bring the fourth word when you find it. I'll be waiting."

He went to the stable. Nobody stopped him. Dora did not move. Kale looked at Stephanos, and Stephanos looked at the ground, and after a while they heard hooves on the stones of the inn yard, and the gate opening, and the hooves going away down the street toward the western road, fast.

---

Dora found him at midnight on the gallery, by the rail where Petros had sat.

He was standing there looking at the white wall. The lamps had burned low. His own shadow lay across the plaster, faint and wavering. He had been standing there a long time.

"You didn't believe me," she said.

He did not turn around.

"You saw the wall," said Dora. "You looked. I watched you look. You saw it." She came and stood beside him. "And you didn't stop him. You didn't even try."

"I tried. I told him to come south."

"You told him the plan. You didn't tell him the man didn't have a shadow. You didn't tell him I was right." Her voice was flat. "You let him think I was a jealous little girl. Because you think he's the heir, and if the angel was real, then you're right about him. And if the angel wasn't real, then maybe you're wrong."

He turned then, and looked at her.

"You're thirteen," he said. "It was dark. The lamps were flickering. You were afraid for him. You've been afraid for him since the Garden. It's natural. It's..."

"Don't," said Dora. "Don't tell me what I saw."

"I'm only saying you might have..."

"I know what you're only saying." She was looking at him with her dark eyes under the veil of the Myrrh-bearer, and he saw that she was not crying. He had thought she would be crying. She was not. Her face was pale and set and very still, and older than he had ever seen it. "You think I'm a child. You've thought so since the ford. You think I came along because I had nowhere else to go. You think I'm here to find Petros on the slave block, and carry the lamb, and teach you to hold a sling. And you're right. I am a child. I'm thirteen. I keep sheep." She took a breath. "But I'm not stupid, Stephanos. And I'm not blind. And I'm not jealous of Petros. I love him. I've always loved him. I'd die for him. I'm the only one in this whole inn who wanted to stop him going, and you're the only one who could have."

He had nothing to say.

"The Amma says you're protecting the wrong one," said Dora. "I didn't know what she meant. I thought she meant me, that you should be protecting me instead of Petros. That's not what she meant." She turned away from the rail. "She meant you're protecting yourself. Your idea of who it is. You'd rather lose Petros than lose that."

She went away along the gallery, into the dark. He heard her door close.

He stood by the rail a long time after that, alone, with the lamps burning out one by one along the wall. He began to say the Question. He got as far as the first word, and stopped, and could not go on.

He did not know what he was afraid the answer would be.

---

Theophanes did not sleep that night either.

He was sitting in his room at the end of the gallery with a candle and the purple book, and in his hand, unfolded, a letter. It had come that evening, before supper, while the others were in the courtyard. A courier had brought it, a Concord courier in grey, on a fast horse, and put it in his hands at the inn gate without a word, and ridden away. It was sealed with the seal of the Chamber of Scribes, and it was written in the small, precise, beautiful hand that Theophanes had spent eleven years learning to copy.

*My dear Theophanes,*

*I do not blame you. I want you to know that first. You are young, and clever, and you have always been too kind for this work; I saw it in you when you were fourteen. You ran because you could not bear to see an old man die. That does you credit. I should have given the Patriarch's letter to Georgios.*

*But I must tell you plainly now what you are part of, because I do not think the Hornbearer has told you, and I do not think he knows.*

*The Hunter has read the lame lord's letter. His seers have read the Words you are gathering; they read them in the stars, in the smoke, in the entrails of lambs. They do not know what the Words mean, any more than you do. But they know this: that the Horn will rise for one of the Shepherd's house, and that the Hunter must be there when it does. And they know that one of the Shepherd's house is travelling with you.*

*Not the boy. The boy is nothing. The seers laughed when they heard he was being called the heir. They know what the Hornbearer does not.*

*The Hunter means to take the girl.*

*He will take her whatever you do, Theophanes. He is coming west with a host that no army of the Romans can stand against, and when he reaches the City, he will have her. That is certain. The only question is what happens to her after.*

*If the Hunter's giants take her, they will use her to open the door, and then they will do what giants do. You have seen what giants do.*

*If the Concord takes her, she will be kept safe. Fed, clothed, taught. A ward of the Chamber. She will never be harmed. You have my word, and you know that in eleven years I have never once broken my word to you.*

*Bring me the four Words when you have them. Bring me the Words, and tell me where the girl will be when the Hunter comes, and I will see that the Concord takes her before the giants can. That is all I ask.*

*You can save her, Theophanes. You are the only one who can.*

*Your teacher, who loves you,*

*Kallinikos*

Theophanes read it for the ninth time. Then he folded it very small and put it inside the back cover of the purple book, between the binding and the last page, where the three Words of Symeon were written in burnt twig.

He sat looking at the candle until it went out.
