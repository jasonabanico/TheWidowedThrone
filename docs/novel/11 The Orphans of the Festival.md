## Chapter 11: The Orphans of the Festival

*The children gather wood, the fathers light the fire, and the women knead dough to make cakes for the Queen of Heaven.*

*The prophet Jeremiah*

---

The *Theotokos* carried them north.

Captain Fotios would not hear of anything else. His ship had been lying in the Goldhand's harbour for four months, stripped of her sails and her crew, with her hold full of Egyptian grain slowly going musty; now the grain was aired and the sails were back and the crew were back, thinner and quieter than they had been, with the marks of the chains on their ankles. The Eparch's grain would go to the City. But first, Fotios said, it would go wherever the Hornbearer wanted.

"Thessalonike," said Amma Thekla.

They were sitting on the palace terrace on the last evening, looking out over the harbour. The white town below was dotted with lamps. Somewhere in the square, someone was playing a pipe.

"Why Thessalonike?" said Stephanos.

"Because Lazaros is afraid of it," said the old woman.

The painter looked up. He had been sitting a little apart, at the edge of the terrace, with the icon of the Archistrategos in its case across his knees, and he had not spoken all evening. His face in the lamplight was thin and pale.

"Amma..."

"You said it yourself, in the Mint. The Festival Council made you hold your icon over the fire." Thekla's fingers went on weaving. "The Gardens are the second theme. There'll be a word there. And there'll be orphans. And there'll be you." She looked at him with her black, bright eyes. "Is that not so?"

Lazaros was quiet a long time. Then he said, "Yes."

"Tell us," said Kale.

She said it gently, which surprised Stephanos. He had not heard Kale say anything gently before. She was sitting on the parapet near the painter with her bow across her knees, as she always sat, and she was not looking at him. She was looking out at the harbour.

Lazaros turned his hands over in his lap and looked at the scars.

"I was apprenticed in Thessalonike," he said. "To Master Panselinos, in the workshop behind the church of Saint Demetrios. Seven years grinding colours. Then he let me paint. Faces, first. Then whole icons. I was good. I don't say that to boast. I was very good. People came from Athens to buy my Theotokos." He paused. "When I was twenty, the Festival Council came to the workshop. They wanted a painter for the hippodrome. They were building a great image of the Queen of Heaven on the spina, where the obelisks used to stand, for the Feast of Fruits. They wanted it painted in the style of the icons, with gold leaf, so the people would bow to it the way they bowed to the Mother of God. The Council said the Queen was the Mother of God. Another name for the same lady, they said. A kinder face." His voice had gone flat. "They offered me more money than Master Panselinos made in ten years."

"And you refused," said Dora. She was sitting on the flagstones at Stephanos's feet with the lamb in her lap.

"I refused. Master Panselinos was dead by then; I had the workshop. They came back with the Lady Eudokia, the head of the Council. She was beautiful. She was kind. She asked me very gently to reconsider, and when I said no again, she had her men take the Theotokos I was working on off the easel and hold it over the brazier. She said: If you love her so much, hold her. Save her." Lazaros looked at his palms. "So I did. I took it from them and held it, over the fire, for as long as it took the Lady Eudokia to finish a cup of wine. I didn't drop it. The paint didn't even blister. Only my hands." He closed them. "Then they threw me out of the city. The workshop was burned that night. I walked to the City and the Patriarch took me in."

Nobody said anything for a while. The pipe in the square below had stopped.

"The Lady Eudokia," said Stephanos at last. "She's still there?"

"She's still the head of the Council. People say she hasn't aged a day in twenty years." Lazaros looked up. "People say the orphans of Thessalonike go to the Feast of Fruits in white garlands every year, and dance in the hippodrome for the Queen, and never come back. I didn't believe it when I lived there. I thought it was gossip. I thought they were adopted, taken into the great houses as servants. That's what the Council said."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen the Mint," said Lazaros quietly.

---

They sailed on the morning tide. The fog did not come. The Aegean lay blue and bright all the way north, and dolphins played in the bow wave off Skiathos, and Dora hung over the rail and shrieked at them until Captain Fotios came and stood beside her and showed her how to whistle them closer.

Petros spent the voyage on the foredeck with the sailors, wrestling. He won every match. He took on two at once and won that too. By the third day, the whole crew were betting on him, and he was laughing and slapping backs and letting the ship's boy ride on his shoulders, and even Fotios, who had seen Petros dive into the flooded Mint four times, watched him with a kind of wonder.

"He's a fine lad," the captain said to Stephanos. "Strong as an ox. Brave as a lion. The men would follow him anywhere."

"Yes," said Stephanos.

He watched Petros too. He could not help it. He watched him on the foredeck with the sun on his dark curly hair and the sweat shining on his shoulders, laughing, and he thought of the Abba's story about the house of Jesse, and the tall son, and Samuel. He thought it in the Abba's voice. *Man looks on the outward appearance.* And he answered it in his own voice: *But this one isn't only outward. He dived into the flood. He dropped the Emperor's sword to save the Amma. That's heart.*

He did not say any of this aloud. But once, on the second evening, Kale sat down beside him on the stern where he was watching and followed his gaze to the foredeck, and said: "You've made up your mind."

"About what?"

"About him." She nodded at Petros. "You look at him the way a man looks at a horse he's already bought."

"He's the lame lord's great-grandson. He's the right age. He has the strength and the courage. The Tower came for him first. What more do you want?"

"I don't want anything. I'm not the one choosing." Kale was quiet a moment. "Neither are you, I thought. Isn't that the whole point of your order? The Horn chooses?"

He had no answer to that. After a while, she got up and went away.

---

Thessalonike came up out of the sea on the fourth morning like a dream of paradise.

Stephanos had seen it once before, as a boy, from the deck of a trading ship. He remembered a great grey city of walls and towers climbing a hill above a curving bay. That city was gone. In its place was a garden.

The walls were still there, but they were green. Vines had covered them from the foot to the battlements: grapes and roses and honeysuckle and a flowering creeper he did not know, with blossoms the colour of blood. The towers wore crowns of flowers. The hill above the walls, which he remembered as bare brown rock, was covered with orchards, rank after rank of trees heavy with fruit though it was the dead of winter, apples and pears and figs and pomegranates, so thick on the branches that the boughs bent to the ground. Fountains glittered among the orchards. Birds sang. The air that came off the land was warm and heavy and sweet, so sweet that Stephanos felt it at the back of his throat like syrup.

"Oh," said Dora, at the rail. "Oh, it's beautiful."

"Don't breathe too deep," said Amma Thekla.

As they came into the harbour, Stephanos began to see the Blight.

It was harder to see here than on Paros. Paros had worn its Blight on the surface, coins on the hulls, serpents on the doors. Here it was hidden in the beauty, like a worm in an apple. The fruit on the trees was too ripe. It had been too ripe, he learned later, for years; it never rotted, never fell, only hung there swelling, glossy and perfect, and when a child picked one and bit it, the flesh inside was grey. The flowers on the walls had too many petals. And when the *Theotokos* tied up at the quay, and Stephanos stepped ashore, he stepped past a bed of red lilies at the foot of the harbour steps, and one of the lilies turned its head to follow him, and he saw that it had teeth.

They were very small teeth. White and sharp and even, like a cat's, in a ring around the throat of the flower.

He stood and looked at it. It looked back.

"Leave it," said Lazaros quietly, beside him. "They don't bite if you don't touch them. Mostly."

---

The orphanage of Saint Anysia stood in the lower city, near the sea walls, in a street that had once been poor and was now smothered in roses like every other street. It was a tall, narrow house with a chapel on the ground floor and dormitories above. There was a courtyard behind it with a fig tree and a well.

The courtyard was empty. The dormitories were empty. In the chapel, an old woman in a nun's black habit was kneeling alone in front of the icon screen, praying. When she heard them come in, she got up so fast she nearly fell.

"Who are you?" she said. "What do you want? If you're from the Council, I've told them, there are no more. There are no more."

"We're not from the Council, Mother," said Stephanos.

She saw the purple on his shield. Her face changed.

Mother Xene had run the orphanage of Saint Anysia for thirty-one years. She told them so over bread and figs in the empty refectory, with her hands clasped so tightly on the table in front of her that her knuckles were white. For the first twenty years it had been a good house, a poor house, full of children. She had taken in foundlings from the harbour and orphans from the plague and the children of sailors who did not come home. At one time there had been eighty of them.

"Then the Council came," she said. "Eleven years ago. The Lady Eudokia herself. She said the Council wanted to honour the orphans at the Feast of Fruits. They would dress them in white and give them garlands and let them lead the dance in the hippodrome. They would feed them the Queen's cakes. It would be an honour. The whole city would see them." Her mouth trembled. "And they would take the ones who danced best into the great houses. As pages. As maids. It was such a chance for them. Such a chance."

"And you let them go," said Dora.

Mother Xene looked at her. "The Council paid for the orphanage, child. The Council paid for everything. The bread, the roof, the fig tree. How could I say no?" She lowered her eyes. "The first year, twelve went. None came back. The Council said they had all been taken into the great houses. I asked which houses. They said the houses did not wish to be named; it would embarrass the children to be known as foundlings. I believed them." She was crying now, silently. "The second year, fifteen went. The third, twenty. I asked again. They stopped answering. And every year, fewer children came to the door, because every mother in the lower city had heard the story and would sooner leave her baby on a church step in Veroia than here." She wiped her face. "Last year, the last seven went. I tried to stop them. I tried to hide them in the cellar. The Council's men found them. They took them in the night. Four boys and three girls. The youngest was five."

"And the Feast of Fruits?" said Stephanos. "When is it?"

Mother Xene looked up at him.

"Tomorrow," she said. "At the full moon. They've taken them from every orphanage in the city this year. Every one. And from the poor houses by the harbour. They say the Queen wants more this year than ever." She put her face in her hands. "I've been praying for a month. I didn't think anyone would come."

---

That night, in the empty dormitory of Saint Anysia, Amma Thekla called a fast.

"From now until it's done," she said. "No bread, no wine, no fruit. Especially no fruit. Water only."

"Why?" said Petros. He had been eating figs from the bowl Mother Xene had brought. He had a fig in his hand now, half-eaten.

"Because of the Lady Eudokia," said the old woman. "What she serves is hunger. The Queen of Heaven promises plenty, and the Bowed who serve her feed on appetite: their own, and everyone else's. It's how she holds a city. Everyone is hungry for something, and she promises it all." Thekla held up her basket, half-woven. "A man who is fasting has already told his hunger no. He's practised. When the Queen's charm comes for him, it finds the door already shut. It slides off."

"I've never fasted in my life," said Petros.

"Then tonight you start."

Petros laughed. He looked around at the others, as if expecting them to laugh with him. Nobody did.

"Amma," he said, "I'm the biggest man here. I'm going to be doing most of the fighting. If I don't eat, I'll be weak as a kitten by tomorrow night. That's no good to anybody."

"You'll be less weak than you think."

"With respect, Amma, you're an old woman who lives in the desert on dates and prayer. I'm a shepherd. I've carried a ram on my back up a mountain. I know what I need." He finished the fig. "I'll pray, if you like. I'll pray hard. But I'm going to eat."

Thekla looked at him with her black eyes and said nothing.

Then she looked at Stephanos.

He knew what she wanted. He knew she wanted him to back her, to order Petros to fast as the leader of this band, as a Hornbearer. He opened his mouth to do it.

And he thought: *He's right. He is the biggest. He will be doing the fighting. He dived into the Mint four times after he'd worked the presses for two weeks with no food. He's not a monk. He's a warrior.*

"He's a fighter, Amma," Stephanos said. "He'll need his strength."

The old woman looked at him a moment longer. Then she went back to her weaving.

"As you like," she said.

Kale was watching Stephanos from the far bed. Her face showed nothing. But she got up a moment later and went out into the courtyard, and did not come back for a long time.

---

Dora came and found him, late, on the roof of the orphanage, where he had gone to be alone.

The moon was nearly full. It hung over the bay, huge and yellow, and the whole city lay below it smothered in flowers, and the smell came up from the gardens thick and sweet. Somewhere in the upper city, drums were beating, slow, in preparation for tomorrow's feast. Along the spina of the old hippodrome, visible from here over the roofs, torches were burning, and in the light of the torches Stephanos could see something tall standing in the middle of the long central wall, where the obelisks had once been. A statue. A woman, very tall, with her arms spread wide and a crown of stars on her head.

The image of the Queen of Heaven. The one Lazaros would not paint.

Dora sat down on the roof tiles beside him. She had not brought the lamb. She had her knees drawn up and her arms around them.

"I'm hungry," she said.

"So am I."

"Petros isn't." She was quiet a moment. "He ate three figs and a whole loaf of bread and half a cheese. He said he was eating for all of us." She rested her chin on her knees. "Why did you let him?"

"He's a fighter, Dora. He's strong. He needs..."

"The Amma said he didn't."

"The Amma is a holy woman, but she's never been in a battle."

"She's been in more battles than you have." Dora turned her head and looked at him. "I heard Kale say so. She said the Amma's been fighting demons in the desert for forty-two years. Every day at noon. Every day." She looked back at the hippodrome. "You didn't let him off because he's a fighter. You let him off because you think he's the heir. You think the heir shouldn't have to do what everyone else does."

"That's not..."

"It's all right," said Dora. "Grandmother used to do it too. She'd let him off chores because he was going to be a great man someday. She'd make me do his milking. She'd say, Dora, you're a good girl, you don't mind." Dora's voice was flat. "I did mind. I did the milking anyway."

He did not know what to say.

"He's my brother," Dora said. "I love him. He's brave, and he's strong, and he dived into the water for the Amma. But he's never had to go without anything in his whole life. Not really. Not when anyone was watching." She stood up. "I'm going to bed. I'm going to be very hungry tomorrow. I hope it helps."

She went down the ladder. Stephanos stayed on the roof, looking at the statue of the Queen on the spina, until the torches burned low and the drums stopped.

He did not ask the Question that night either. He meant to. He began it, silently, under his breath, and somewhere in the middle of it he found himself thinking about Petros diving into the flood, and he lost his place, and did not find it again.

---

The Feast of Fruits began at dusk.

They went to it in disguise, after all. Stephanos did not lie; he did not need to. The whole city went to the Feast in masks, and nobody asked anyone's name. Mother Xene brought masks from a chest in the orphanage cellar, old festival masks of painted leather that the children had worn, years ago, before the Council came. Stephanos wore his lamellar under a long dark cloak and carried his sword under it. Kale wore her bow on her back under a shawl. Lazaros wore a mask with a bird's beak and carried the icon of the Archistrategos wrapped in a blanket like a baby.

Petros's mask was a lion's face, gilded. It was the only one big enough.

The hippodrome of Thessalonike was a long stone oval, open to the sky, with tiers of seats rising on every side. It had been built for chariot races. There had been no races for a hundred years. Now it was a garden like the rest of the city, its tiers overgrown with roses and vines, its long track paved with flowers. Torches burned on poles along the spina. Lanterns hung in the vines. And the crowd was enormous.

Stephanos had never seen so many people in one place. The whole of Thessalonike was there, it seemed, fifty thousand of them or more, in masks and garlands and silk, packed onto the tiers, crowding the track, dancing. Music came from everywhere at once: drums and pipes and cymbals and lutes and a great deep horn that sounded from the spina at intervals, a long, low, shuddering note that went right through him. The air was thick with incense. It came up from braziers all around the track, sweet and heavy, and it mixed with the scent of the flowers and the sweat of the crowd until he could hardly breathe.

And everywhere there were cakes.

Women moved through the crowd with baskets of them: small round cakes, golden-brown, stamped on top with a crowned woman's face. They pressed them into every hand. The people ate them as they danced. Stephanos saw a man take one, and bite it, and his eyes go soft and faraway. He saw a woman eat three, one after another, and then begin to sway.

A woman with a basket came up to him and held out a cake. She wore a mask of white feathers, and her eyes behind it were glassy.

"For the Queen," she said. "Eat, and be filled."

"No," said Stephanos. "Thank you."

She stared at him with her glassy eyes. Then she smiled, very sweetly, and moved on to Petros, and held out the basket.

Petros took a cake.

"Don't," said Dora, at his elbow.

"It's a cake, Dora." Petros lifted his lion mask and grinned. "Everybody's eating them. We'll look strange if we don't." He took a bite.

Nothing happened. He chewed, and swallowed, and grinned again. "It's good. Honey and something. Cinnamon." He finished it and pulled his mask back down. "See? I'm fine."

Amma Thekla, walking at the back of the group in a plain black mask, said nothing.

---

The horn on the spina sounded again, long and low. And the crowd on the track began to part.

Down the middle of the hippodrome, between the dancers, came a procession of children.

They were dressed in white. Every one of them. White linen tunics that fell to their bare ankles, and on their heads, garlands of white flowers. They walked two by two, holding hands, in a long line that stretched from the starting gates at one end of the track almost to the spina. There were hundreds of them. The smallest in front could not have been more than four. The oldest at the back were perhaps twelve. They walked slowly, in step with the drums, and their faces were calm and happy and empty, and their eyes were half-closed, as though they were walking in their sleep.

Behind them came the masked women with their baskets, feeding them cakes.

Mother Xene, who had come with them in a nun's mask, made a sound like a wounded animal.

The crowd was cheering. The drums were beating faster. The children walked on toward the spina, toward the tall statue of the Queen with her arms spread wide and her crown of stars, and as they came nearer, Stephanos saw that the base of the statue was opening.

It was a door. A great bronze door, set into the spina beneath the statue's feet, and it was swinging slowly inward, and beyond it, lit from below with a warm golden light, a wide stair went down into the earth. Warm air came up out of it. It smelled of flowers.

At the top of the stair, waiting for the children, stood a woman.

She was very tall and very beautiful. She wore a gown of green silk embroidered with fruit and flowers, and her hair was dark red, piled high on her head and woven with pearls, and her face was pale and perfect as a statue's. She was holding out her arms, the same way the Queen above her held out hers. And she was smiling at the children with a look of such tenderness that for a moment, even knowing what he knew, Stephanos felt his heart turn over.

"The Lady Eudokia," whispered Lazaros. His voice shook. "She hasn't changed. Not a day. Not an hour."

The first children reached the top of the stair. The Lady Eudokia bent and kissed each one on the forehead, and each one went past her, smiling, down the golden stair into the earth, and out of sight.

"Now," said Stephanos. "We go now."

He started forward through the crowd.

A hand closed on his arm.

He turned. It was Petros. Petros had lifted his lion mask, and his face underneath was flushed and wet with sweat, and his eyes were strange. They were soft and wide and glassy, and they were fixed on the Lady Eudokia at the top of the stair.

"Wait," said Petros. His voice was thick. "Wait, Stephanos. Look at her. Isn't she beautiful? Isn't she the most beautiful thing you've ever seen?"

"Petros. Let go."

"She's so kind. Look how kind she is to them. She loves them, Stephanos. Can't you see she loves them?" Petros's grip tightened. It was like being held in a vise. "We shouldn't hurt her. We shouldn't. We should go down with them. We should all go down together. She'd love us too. She'd feed us. We'd never be hungry again."

"Petros!" Dora had hold of his other arm. She was pulling at it, uselessly. "Petros, it's the cake! It's the cake! Let him go!"

Petros did not seem to hear her. He was smiling. It was the same empty, happy smile the children wore.

And at the top of the stair, the Lady Eudokia lifted her perfect face, and looked across the crowd, and found them. She looked straight at Petros in his lion mask. And she smiled at him, and held out her arms.

"*Come,*" she said.

Stephanos heard it, though she was two hundred paces away and the drums were beating and fifty thousand people were cheering. He heard it inside his head, soft and warm and sweet as honey. He felt it pull at him, like the hand in the fog.

It slid off. It found nothing to hold. He was hungry, and he had been hungry all day, and he had already said no to his hunger a hundred times since dawn, and the voice found the door shut.

It did not slide off Petros.

Petros let go of Stephanos's arm. He shook off Dora as if she were a fly. And with a look of perfect, shining joy on his face, he turned and began to walk through the crowd toward the golden stair.
