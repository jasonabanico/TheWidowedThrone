# Part Two: The Judges

## Chapter 6: The Ford at Arkadiopolis

*The Lord who delivered me from the paw of the lion and the paw of the bear will deliver me from the hand of this Philistine.*

*David, to King Saul*

---

Thrace was dying.

Stephanos had ridden the Thracian roads before, years ago, on the Tagma's business. He remembered orchards, and vineyards on the south-facing hills, and villages with whitewashed churches where the priests came out to bless travellers and the children ran alongside the horses begging for news. He remembered it as poor country, hard-used by a century of raiders, but alive.

It was not alive now. Or it was alive the way a wound is alive.

On the first day out from the Golden Gate they passed through villages where the church doors were nailed shut and the people stood in their doorways and watched them go by without a word. On the second, the sky at dusk turned a colour Stephanos had never seen: a dull purple-brown, like old bruising, spreading up from the horizon until it swallowed the stars. Kale looked at it and said nothing, and Amma Thekla looked at it and crossed herself.

"The Blight," said Theophanes. "It's spreading. The Chamber's maps showed Thrace as Wavering when I left. That was a year ago." He looked at the sky. "That's not Wavering. That's worse."

On the third day, in a village called Bizye, they found the well. It stood in the middle of the square, and someone had carved a face into the stone of its rim, a long-jawed, grinning face with hollow eyes. When Stephanos looked closer, he saw that nobody had carved it. The stone had grown that way. The face pushed out of the rim like a bubble pushing out of dough, and the water in the well below it was brackish and smelled of iron.

"The dead stir on feast nights here," said the old woman who sold them bread. She said it flatly, the way she might have said it rained on Tuesdays. "Not much. They come up to the church door and knock. We don't open it."

On the third night, Dove was the only horse still fresh.

The others were blown. Kale's pony and Theophanes's hired hack stood with their heads down and their flanks heaving, and Lazaros's mule had gone lame in the afternoon. Amma Thekla had been riding pillion behind Kale since noon. Sergios had walked the whole way, barefoot, singing, and seemed less tired than any of them.

"Arkadiopolis is ten miles on," Kale said. She stood by the fire with her arms folded, looking west. "The ford's two miles past the town. You could be there before dawn, on the mare. We'll come as soon as there's light."

"The frost," said Stephanos.

Kale looked at the grass. It was white. It crackled under her boots.

"It came last night," she said. "While we slept at Bizye. I'm sorry. I got it wrong. It was a day early."

He was in the saddle before she had finished.

---

He reached the ford an hour before dawn, as the letter said.

The river ran black and loud over the stones. It was wider than he had expected, forty paces from bank to bank, and the water was fast, white where it broke over the rocks. On the far side, a line of willows bent down to it, their long leaves hanging in the current. Behind the willows, on a low rise, the ruins of a church stood up against the sky. Two walls and part of an apse still held. The rest was rubble grown over with bramble.

A fire burned in the apse. He could see its glow on the curve of the old wall.

Dove did not want to cross. She stopped at the edge of the water and threw up her head and blew, and when he pressed her with his heels, she backed. He did not blame her. The water was loud and cold and he could not see the bottom.

He dismounted, and took her by the bridle, and led her in.

The water came to his knees, then to his thighs, then almost to his waist. It pulled at him like a living thing. The stones underfoot were round and slick and shifted as he stepped on them. Twice he stumbled and Dove's shoulder kept him up. Halfway across he felt the current take his feet out from under him entirely, and for a heartbeat he hung from the bridle, and then the mare heaved and he found the bottom again.

He came up the far bank streaming water. He drew his spathion as he climbed.

A girl was sitting by the fire in the ruined apse, with a shepherd's crook across her knees and a dozen sheep asleep around her in the rubble. She looked twelve, perhaps thirteen, thin and brown, with a mass of dark curly hair tied back with a strip of red wool. Her dress was patched and her feet were bare. She looked at him, at the water pouring off his lamellar, at the purple on his shield, at the sword in his hand and the sling on his belt.

And she laughed.

"You're late," she said. "Grandmother said a knight would come at the first frost. The frost came yesterday."

"The roads," said Stephanos. He did not sheathe the sword. He had heard demons speak through the faces of children before, in the east, in the villages where Anak's men had been. "Who was your grandmother?"

"A lame man's daughter. She kept his letters." The girl poked the fire with the end of her crook, and sparks went up into the dark. "She said he wrote one more before he died, and gave it to a trader to carry, and that the trader would forget it until the day he remembered. She said a lot of things. Most of them came true, which was very annoying."

Dove lifted her head and stamped.

Stephanos turned. Across the river, the willows were moving against the wind.

He counted six shapes coming out of the trees. The smallest stood a head taller than any man he had ever met. Gibborim, the mighty ones, grandsons of the sons of the Watchers. They carried axes as long as oars. One of them held a chain with an iron hook on the end, and it swung the hook idly as it walked, the way a boy swings a stone on a string.

"Behind the wall," he told the girl.

She did not move. "They came last week too. They took my brother's goats."

"Behind the wall. Now."

The first giant stepped into the river. The black water that had nearly carried Stephanos away came only to its knees. It stopped there and looked across at him, and he saw its face in the firelight: broad and flat and heavy-browed, with small eyes and a mouth like a slot. It called across the water in a voice like a millstone grinding.

"Knight of nothing! Throne of nobody! Give us the shepherd and ride home!"

Stephanos understood then that the giants had not come for goats.

He understood, too, that six of them were more than one Hornbearer could fight. He had fought two at once in the east, once, with twenty cataphracts at his back, and they had lost four men. He had nearly died at Charisios fighting one. Six of them at a ford in the dark, with no lance and no friends and his knee half-ruined, was not a fight. It was a grave. The Tagma would find his bones in the spring with the letter still in his breastplate.

He put the spathion in his left hand. He took the sling off his belt with his right.

He did not know why he did it. Later he would say that he had thought it through, that he had remembered the stories and the rule and reasoned that a sword was no use at that distance and a sling might be. That was not true. He did not think at all. His hand went to the sling the way it had gone to it every morning for nine years on the practice ground below the Tower, in rain and snow and summer heat, because the rule required it and because Pelagios had said that every Hornbearer must remember the Shepherd. He had always thought it a sentiment. A knight's weapon was the lance.

His fingers found a stone in the mud at the water's edge. It was round and smooth and very cold. He fitted it to the pouch.

"The Lord who delivered me from the paw of the lion," he said.

He could not remember the rest of the line. He let the stone go.

It struck the lead giant above the left eye with a sound like an axe going into green wood. The giant stood for a moment in the middle of the ford with the black water pulling at its legs. Its small eyes looked at Stephanos with an expression of great surprise. Then it fell sideways into the current, slowly, like a tree falling, and the river took it. The water rolled it over and over and carried it down against the rocks below the ford, and did not give it back.

The other five stopped on the bank.

They stood there in a row among the willows, huge and dark, and Stephanos waited for them to come. He had no more stones in his hand. He bent and felt for one in the mud without taking his eyes off them, and found one, and fitted it to the sling.

They did not come.

The giant with the chain looked at the girl, standing now in the broken arch of the apse with her crook in her hands. Then it looked past her, up, at the sky above the ruined church. Stephanos did not turn to see what it was looking at. He did not dare take his eyes off it. But he saw its face change. He saw the heavy, brutal face go slack and grey, like the face of a man who has seen his own death walking toward him down a road.

The giant dropped its chain. It turned and went back into the willows. The others went after it. Stephanos heard them crashing through the brush, and then further off, running, and then further still, until the sound was lost in the noise of the river.

He found that he was shaking. He sat down on a fallen column by the fire, because his legs would not hold him.

The girl came out from behind the wall with a clay pot of water and handed it to him. He drank. The water was cold and tasted of the river.

"You didn't ask me anything," she said. "Grandmother said you would ask me a question."

He looked at the ruined apse. On the one patch of plaster still clinging to its curve, a faded Christ looked down, his hand raised in blessing, his face worn almost away by a hundred winters. He looked at the sheep, sleeping in the rubble. He looked at the girl with her crook and her red wool and her bare, dirty feet.

"Whom will the Horn anoint?" he asked.

She smiled the way people smile at a riddle when they already know the answer and do not intend to tell it.

"Come in the morning," she said, "and see."

---

The others found him at sunrise, asleep on the fallen column with his back against the apse and his sword across his knees. The girl had put a sheepskin over him. She was sitting by the fire again, toasting bread on a stick, and when Kale came up the bank with her bow in her hand, the girl looked up and said, "He killed one with a sling. The rest ran away. Are you hungry?"

Stephanos woke to find six faces looking down at him, and the sun in his eyes.

"There's something you need to see," Kale said. "Get up. Come and look."

She led him around the outside of the ruined church, through the brambles, to the side that faced away from the river. The frost was still thick on the grass there, where the sun had not reached it yet, white and crisp as salt.

In the frost were tracks.

They came up out of the east, from the direction of the hills, and circled the whole church once, all the way around, and went back the way they had come. They were the tracks of a cat. But they were the size of a dinner platter, each print as wide as Stephanos's two hands spread side by side, and they sank deep into the frozen ground, as if whatever made them had weighed as much as an ox. The four toes of each print were blunt and round. The pad was broad.

In every print, the grass was green.

Not the dull winter green of the grass around it, under the frost. It was the green of new grass in April, bright and soft and wet, and in some of the prints there were small white flowers, the kind that come up in the meadows after the snow melts. The frost had not touched them.

Stephanos stood looking at them for a long time. Behind him, one by one, the others came around the corner of the church and stopped.

Amma Thekla went down on her knees in the frost. She did not say anything. She put her hand, very gently, into one of the prints, and left it there.

"What is it?" said Theophanes. His voice was strange. "A lion? There are no lions in Thrace. There haven't been lions in Thrace for five hundred years."

Nobody answered him.

"It came in the night," the girl said behind them. She had followed them around the church with her bread on its stick. "After the giants ran away. I heard it walking. It walked all the way around, and then it lay down on the far side of the wall, by the sheep. I could hear it breathing. It sounded like wind in a chimney." She took a bite of bread. "The sheep weren't scared. That was strange. Sheep are scared of everything."

"Did you see it?" Stephanos asked.

"No. I didn't look." She chewed. "Grandmother said you shouldn't look, if it comes. She said it would show you itself when it wanted to, and not before. She said it used to come to her father's hall sometimes when she was a girl, in the night, and lie down by his bed, and in the morning the wound in his leg didn't hurt so much." She swallowed. "I didn't believe her. I thought it was a story for children."

"What's your name?" said Kale.

"Theodora. Everyone calls me Dora." She looked at Stephanos. "What's yours?"

"Stephanos."

"Of the Horn," said Dora. "I know. It's on your shield. You're the one who's always angry."

He stared at her. "Who told you that?"

"Nobody. You look angry. You looked angry at the giants and you looked angry at the river and you looked angry at me, when you woke up." She shrugged. "Grandmother used to say some people look angry because they're afraid, and some people look afraid because they're angry, and you can't tell which until you know them. I don't know you yet."

Sergios laughed so hard he had to sit down in the frost.

---

Later, by the fire, with the sun up and the frost melting and the sheep grazing on the slope below the church, Dora brought out a box.

It was an old wooden box, the kind farmers keep their deeds in, bound with iron and black with age. Inside it, tied with string, were letters: forty or fifty of them, on parchment and bark and scraps of cloth, all in the same shaky brown hand as the letter in Stephanos's breastplate. Theophanes took one out with trembling fingers, as though it might burn him, and began to read.

"They're to his daughter," he said after a while. "Your grandmother. Maria?"

"Maria," Dora said. "She died at midsummer. She was very old. Older than anyone in the village."

"He wrote to her after she married. About his fields, and the monks, and the pain in his leg. About the fool who came to his hall and didn't ask the question, and came back twenty years later and asked it." Theophanes turned the letter over. "About the Horn. He says here: *The Horn will not come to the strong. The strong have had it, and look what they did with it.* And here: *Keep the little ones close, Maria. The Tower will come looking.*"

"They did come," Dora said. "Two weeks ago. A man from the Concord came to the village with a paper and four of those." She nodded toward the river, where the giants had stood. "He said there was a labour levy for the Tower's roads in the east. Every man of eighteen or over in the village had to go. My brother is eighteen. They took him. They took six others too." She poked the fire. "Old Nikolaos in the village said they didn't go to any roads. He said the Concord sold them in Rhaidestos to a slave ship from the islands. The Goldhand's islands."

"Your brother," said Stephanos. "What's his name?"

"Petros."

"Tell me about him."

Dora looked at him with her head on one side. "He's tall," she said. "Taller than you. He's very strong. He can wrestle a bull calf to the ground, and he did it at the harvest feast last year, and all the girls screamed. He's brave. He fought a wolf once, with a stick, when it came for the lambs. He's handsome, everyone says so, and he knows it." She paused. "He's kind, mostly. When he remembers. Grandmother always said he'd be a great man someday."

Stephanos felt something tighten in his chest. He looked at Theophanes, and saw that Theophanes had understood it too.

"The lame lord's house," Stephanos said slowly. "Maria's grandson. Eighteen years old, tall and strong and brave, and the Concord took him two weeks ago, a few days after the Master Scribe opened a letter about an heir at the ford." He stood up. "They weren't here for goats. They weren't here for you. They were here for the shepherd, the giant said. They already had Petros. They came back to make sure there was no one else."

"There's me," said Dora.

"Yes. They'd have taken you too, if they'd found you, as a hostage. To hold him with." He looked east, toward the sea. "The Goldhand's islands. That's where they've taken him. That's where the Tower is keeping him until they decide what to do. We have to get him back."

"You think Petros is the heir," said Dora.

"I think the lame lord's letter said someone of his house would be at the ford. I think the Tower thought so too, because they came for your brother first. I think he's tall and strong and brave, and the Shepherd's blood is in him, and the Tower is afraid of him." Stephanos heard his own voice quickening. "I think the Horn has been waiting a hundred years for someone like him."

Dora looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed.

It was not a cruel laugh. It was the laugh of someone who has been told a very good joke, and who does not want to spoil it for the person telling it.

"You don't know Petros," she said.

"I will."

"Yes," she said. "I expect you will." She stood up and dusted off her dress. "I'm coming with you."

"No. You're a child. It's dangerous."

"The giants will come back," Dora said. "You said so. They'll come back for me, to hold Petros with. I can't stay here. Old Nikolaos has five children and a bad back; he can't protect me. There's nobody else. Grandmother's dead." She picked up her crook. "And I'm the only one who knows what Petros looks like. You'll need me to find him on the slave block. There'll be dozens of big boys there. Hundreds."

"She's right," said Kale.

"She's thirteen."

"I'm fourteen at Easter," said Dora. "And I can use a sling better than you. I saw you last night. You held it wrong."

Sergios fell over sideways in the grass and lay there, shaking with laughter, with his rags over his face.

---

They left Dora's sheep with old Nikolaos in the village, all except one lamb, which Dora refused to leave and which she carried under her arm. She had named the sheep after emperors. The old ram, who butted anyone who came near him and had once put the village priest in the river, was called Andronikos. The lamb was called Poimen.

"Because he's the youngest," she said. "And he's always getting lost."

That afternoon they rode south toward the coast and the port of Rhaidestos, where ships left for the islands. Stephanos wrote a letter at the first inn they came to, and gave it to a carter bound for Selymbria, for the two novices waiting there, to carry to the Abba. It was a short letter. He told the Abba about the ford, and the giant, and the tracks in the frost. He told him about Petros. He did not tell him about the girl's laugh.

He did not know why he left that out. He thought about it that night, lying awake by the fire with his knee aching and the lamb asleep against Dora's back on the other side of the embers, and he did not come to an answer.

He did not ask the question that night. He was too tired. It was the first night in nine years he had forgotten.
