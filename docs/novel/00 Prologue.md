# Prologue: The Night of the Statue

*The Emperor set his own likeness in the house of God, and on that night the Horn was taken from us. Some say it was stolen. Some say it fled. The deacon Isaakios said it went down, and no one believed him.*

*From the Chronicle of the Return*

---

The statue came into the Great Church on a wagon drawn by twelve white oxen, and it took forty men with ropes to stand it up.

Isaakios was nineteen that year, a deacon for less than a season, and he held a candle at the north door while they worked. He had been told to hold it. Nobody had told him when he might put it down, so he held it until the wax ran over his knuckles and set there like a second skin.

The statue was bronze, gilded over, and twice the height of a man. It showed the Emperor Andronikos in armour, with one hand raised in blessing and the other resting on the hilt of a sword. The sculptor had given him a calm face. Isaakios had seen the Emperor twice in the flesh, and neither time had his face been calm.

They set it beneath the dome, in the place where the people stood to hear the Gospel. Then the Emperor came in with his guard and his eunuchs and his bishops, and he walked around his own likeness three times and said nothing, and the bishops said nothing, and the Patriarch Gennadios stood at the Beautiful Gate of the icon screen with his arms at his sides and his face grey as ash.

"It will teach the people," the Emperor said at last. His voice filled the dome and came back down on them. "They will look at it and remember who keeps them."

"God keeps them," the Patriarch said.

"God keeps me," said the Emperor, "and I keep them. That is the order of things. My fathers forgot it, and the themes slipped from their hands. I have not forgotten." He turned to the guard captain. "Clear the church. The Patriarch will bless it at dawn."

The old man did not answer. The Emperor did not wait for him to.

---

They cleared the church, and Isaakios was forgotten in it, because the sacristan who should have sent him home was drunk, and the deacons who outranked him had gone to the palace to eat the Emperor's lamb.

He did not mind. He liked the Great Church empty. He liked the way the lamps hung from their long chains in the dark and made small gold islands in the air, and how the dome above them seemed to float on nothing, as the old builders had meant it to. He put his candle into a stand at last and peeled the wax off his fingers and walked up the nave to the icon screen, and he did not look at the statue as he passed it.

Behind the screen, in the sanctuary, on a shelf of green marble to the left of the altar, the Horn of Anointing lay on a cloth of purple silk.

Isaakios had seen it every day since he was made deacon. It was a ram's horn, old and dark and plain, sealed at the wide end with a stopper of cedar and bound at the narrow end with a band of worn silver. The oil inside had anointed Poimen the Shepherd three hundred years before, and the seven kings of Poimen's house after him, and it had never run low, though no one had ever filled it. The Patriarch said that the oil was older than the horn. The sacristan said that was a story for pilgrims. Isaakios did not know which of them to believe, and on most days it did not trouble him.

Tonight he knelt before the Beautiful Gate and looked through it at the horn, and he found that he could not pray. Every time he began, he felt the statue at his back, as if it were a man standing too close behind him in a line.

He got up. He went through the side door into the sanctuary, which a deacon should not do alone, and he stood before the green shelf.

The lamps above the altar were moving.

There was no wind. The doors were shut. But the seven silver lamps that hung over the holy table were swinging on their chains, slowly, all together, the way reeds bend in a current. Their flames did not waver. They leaned, all seven, toward the floor.

Isaakios took a step back and put his hand on the wall.

The horn on its shelf gave off a light. He would argue about this later with everyone who would listen, and he would never find the right word. It was not the light of a lamp, which pushes the dark away from itself. It was more like the light of a window, as if the horn were a hole in the world and the day on the other side was shining through. The purple silk under it burned with colour. The marble shelf showed every vein.

Then the floor of the sanctuary opened.

It did not crack. There was no sound of breaking stone. The marble between the altar and the shelf went soft and dark, like water in a well, and the horn lifted from its silk and went down into it. It went down slowly, a hand's breadth at a time, and the seven lamps went down after it on their chains, and the chains grew longer to follow, link after link after link, until the lamps were small gold sparks far below in a dark that had no bottom.

Isaakios fell on his face.

He lay there a long time. He smelled something he would remember until he died, an old man in a monastery on the Bosporus telling it to novices who rolled their eyes: the smell of rain falling on dry ground in the first storm after summer. It filled the sanctuary. It was so strong that he wept, and he did not know why.

When he lifted his head, the floor was marble again. The shelf was empty. The seven lamps hung above the altar on their short chains, as they always had, but they were dark, and the wicks were cold, as if they had not burned in a hundred years.

---

At dawn the Patriarch came to bless the statue and found the young deacon sitting against the green shelf with his knees drawn up.

"Where is it?" the Patriarch said.

"It went down, Father."

"Down where?"

Isaakios pointed at the floor.

The Patriarch looked at the floor for a long time. He was an old man, and he had served under three emperors, and he had watched each of them trade a little more of the empire for a little more peace. He did not ask the boy to explain. He went out through the Beautiful Gate and stood in the nave, in front of the Emperor Andronikos and his court and his bronze likeness, and he said in a loud voice that the Horn of Anointing had departed from the house of God and he would bless nothing in it that day.

The Emperor had him beaten on the steps. Then the Emperor had the sanctuary searched, and the deacons questioned, and Isaakios was flogged until he said he had been asleep and seen nothing. They never found the horn. The Emperor gave out that the monks had stolen it, and he burned two monasteries to prove his point.

Within the year, Nebrodes the Hunter came down out of the east with his giants and broke the Land Walls in three places. He burned the Great Church, and the dome fell in. His seers went into the sanctuary with iron bars and hammers and dug at the floor where the altar had stood, because they had heard a story about a horn that went down, and they believed stories more readily than the Emperor had. They cracked the floor open. Something came up out of the crack, and not all of the seers came back from it. After that Nebrodes let the hole alone.

He marched the people of the City east in chains, to build his tower.

Isaakios went with them. He lived to be very old in the brick fields of Nebrodia, and he told the story of the night of the statue to anyone who would listen. The children of the exile liked the part with the lamps. The grown men said he had been drunk, like the sacristan. The Scribes of the Tower wrote down that the Horn of Anointing had never existed, and that the Shepherd was a tale for peasants, and in time most of the exiles believed the Scribes, because the Scribes had the books.

Isaakios did not argue with them. He said only that the horn had not been stolen and had not been lost. It had gone down, the way a man goes down a stair to wait at a door, and one day someone would come down the stair after it.

He died in the sixty-eighth year of the exile, two years before the remnant came home. He was buried in a field of brick, and nobody marked the place.
