## Chapter 18: The Sword Crowned

*Put not your trust in princes, nor in the son of man, in whom there is no help.*

*Psalm 146*

---

They saw the smoke from the Marmara.

The ship was a Gazan spice trader called the *Saint Mark*, fast and narrow, and she had carried them north in eleven days with the wind behind her the whole way, as if something were pushing. On the eleventh morning, with the sun just up, Stephanos stood at the bow and watched the coast of Thrace come up out of the haze on the left hand, and above it, all along the horizon from west to east, a long low bank of brown.

It was not fog. It was not cloud. It was smoke. It rose from a hundred places at once along the coast and inland, thick columns and thin ones, and drifted east on the wind and spread out and hung over the whole land like a stain.

"Selymbria," said Kale beside him. She pointed at the nearest column. "And Rhegion. And Athyras. And all the villages between." Her voice was steady. "He's burning everything between the sea and the Long Walls. He wants the City to see it."

"Where is he?"

Kale closed her eyes. She stood very still for a long moment, with her hand on the rail.

"Melantias," she said. "On the plain by the Athyras bridge. Eighteen miles from the Golden Gate. He's made camp. He's waiting." She opened her eyes. "He's waiting for something."

"For what?"

"I don't know. I can't see. There's too much of him. It's like looking into a fire."

Stephanos looked at the smoke over Thrace. Somewhere under it, he thought, was the ford at Arkadiopolis, and the ruined church with its two walls and its faded Christ, and old Nikolaos's village where Dora had left her sheep.

He did not look round at Dora. He knew she was behind him, at the stern, with Amma Thekla. He knew she had been watching the smoke since dawn and had not said a word.

---

The City was ready for war.

He saw it as the *Saint Mark* came up past the Great Church toward the Harbour gate. The sea walls were manned from end to end: every tower had its watch, and the battlements between were thick with spears. On the Land Walls, far off, he could see the same. Banners flew from every gate. And not only the old banners of the City's wards and guilds; new ones, in a colour he had not seen on the walls in his lifetime.

Imperial purple. With a double-headed eagle in gold.

Crowds were in the streets. He could hear them from the water: a great roar, rising and falling, like the sea. It was coming from the Hippodrome.

"They're cheering," said Lazaros.

"For the Emperor," said Theophanes. He was standing at the rail with his satchel over his shoulder and his hand pressed against it, over the purple book. His face was grey. He had not slept, Stephanos thought, since the Well. "They're cheering for Bardas."

---

The Domestikos was waiting for them at the Harbour gate.

He had not come alone. He sat his big grey warhorse at the head of twenty Hornbearers in full armour, with lances raised and the purple of the Tagma snapping from the lance-heads, and behind them, in the gateway, a crowd of watchers had gathered to see. Nikephoros Skleros wore no helm. His grey hair was cropped close and his scarred face was set like stone.

Stephanos came down the gangplank and walked across the quay to him, and stopped at his stirrup, and knelt on the stones.

"Domestikos," he said.

Nikephoros looked down at him for a long moment.

"Stephanos of Taron," he said at last. His voice carried across the quay. "Named oathbreaker in the chapter of the Tagma of the Horn on the Feast of Saint Andrew. You rode out of the Golden Gate at night against the direct command of your Domestikos, given in chapter, which you had accepted in chapter. You were summoned home and did not come. You have been gone seven months." He paused. "Do you deny any of it?"

"No, Domestikos."

"Do you have anything to say for yourself?"

Stephanos lifted his head.

"There was a girl at the ford," he said. "And six giants in the willows. Brother Gregoras would have been four days too late." He held the Domestikos's eyes. "I found the lame lord's great-grandson on the Goldhand's presses. I've been to the Waters, and the Gardens, and the Heights, and the Wilderness. I've found the four Words of Symeon. I know where the Horn is." He did not look away. "I broke my oath. I'll answer for it. I'm not sorry I went."

A murmur went through the crowd in the gateway. Nikephoros did not move. His face did not change.

"The four Words," he said.

"Yes, Domestikos."

"Where is the Horn?"

"Under the Great Church. Beneath the sanctuary. At the Gate of Sheol."

The Domestikos was quiet for a long moment. Then something moved in his face, far down, and Stephanos could not tell whether it was anger or relief or something else.

"Get up," said Nikephoros. "The Emperor wants to see you."

---

The Emperor was in the Fool's Tower.

That surprised Stephanos more than anything. He had expected the Hippodrome, or the Magnaura, or the half-ruined palace of Blachernae where the old emperors had lived. He had not expected to climb the familiar stair of the Tower and come into the refectory and find it full: the whole chapter of the Tagma standing along the walls in their purple, and the Eparch and the Patriarch at the end of the long table, and the lords of the Sword faction in their furs, and in the middle of them all, in the Domestikos's chair at the head of the table, Bardas Argyros in the purple of the Caesars, with a circlet of gold on his wheat-coloured hair.

And standing behind the Emperor's chair, at his right hand, in armour gilded from helm to greaves, with a purple cloak on his shoulders and a sword at his hip with a great red stone in the pommel, was Petros.

He had grown. That was Stephanos's first thought, absurdly. He had not grown; he had been as tall as this at the Mint. But he stood differently. He stood like a man who has been told he is important and has come to believe it. His bruises from the barrow were gone. His face was handsome and sunburned and grave. When he saw Stephanos in the doorway, he smiled, a slow, warm, gracious smile, the smile a lord gives a servant who has done well.

"Stephanos," said the Emperor.

He rose from the chair and came around the table with his hands held out. He took Stephanos by both shoulders and looked into his face with his steady blue eyes, and he was smiling too, and his smile was kind.

"Welcome home," said Bardas. "Welcome home, Hornbearer. I've heard everything. Petros has told me. The Goldhand in his altar cloth. The Mother of Festivals. Anak son of Gathos, dead on his grandfather's tomb, with a sling stone in his temple." He shook his head. "You've done more for the empire in seven months than the rest of us have done in thirty years. I'd have given half my crown to have been with you."

"Majesty," said Stephanos. He did not know what else to say.

"Majesty," said Bardas, and laughed, a little ruefully. "They all call me that now. I still look round to see who they're talking to." He let go of Stephanos's shoulders. "The Domestikos has told you about the charge."

"Yes, Majesty."

"I've asked him to set it aside. I've no right to ask it; the Tagma's rule is the Tagma's business, not the Emperor's. But I've asked. And he's agreed, on one condition." Bardas looked at Nikephoros, who stood at the wall with his arms folded. "The Hunter is at Melantias. His host is eighteen miles from the Golden Gate, and it grows every day. Giant kings from the Caucasus. The Concord's Iron Host. Seers. Things I don't have names for." The Emperor's face was grave now. "I mean to meet him in the field. Tomorrow, at dawn."

The refectory was silent.

"In the field," said the Eparch from the end of the table. Her voice was flat. "Not on the walls."

"The walls held for fifty-two days once, Lady Eparch, and then they fell," said Bardas. "They'll fall again. Nebrodes broke them with a ram the size of a ship a hundred years ago; this time he has seers who can crack stone with a word. If we wait for him behind the walls, we'll be waiting for our own deaths." He turned back to Stephanos. "But if we meet him at Melantias, at the bridge, where his host has to cross the Athyras to reach us, we can hold the crossing with a few thousand. We can break him there. Before he reaches the City at all."

"And the Horn, Majesty?" said the Patriarch.

The old man had not risen. He sat at the end of the table with his large soft hands folded in front of him, and his face was very tired.

"The four Words say the Horn is beneath my church," he said. "Beneath the sanctuary. At the Gate of Sheol. The Hornbearer says so; I believe him. The Tagma has believed it for thirty years. And the Words say it will rise when the least of the Shepherd's house goes down." He looked at Bardas. "Will you send someone down, Majesty? Before you ride out?"

"No," said Bardas.

He said it quietly, but firmly, and he looked at the Patriarch with respect.

"I've been told," the Emperor said, "that the Horn will rise in front of the whole City, when the day is won. By a messenger I have every reason to trust." He hesitated, and for a moment his face was uncertain. "He came to me before the Sangarios, Holy Father. He told me where the raiders would cross, and when. He told me which ford. He was right in every detail. He told me the Domestikos would anoint me in the Hippodrome and the people would accept it, and they did. He told me Petros would come to me, and Petros came." Bardas glanced at the tall young man behind the chair. "And he told me the Horn is not to be sought by force. *The strong will seek it, and it will not rise for them.* He knew the third Word, Holy Father, before the Hornbearer ever found it on the mountain."

The Patriarch was silent.

"So I won't send anyone down," said Bardas. "Not the Tagma, not my soldiers. I won't seek it. I'll fight the Hunter at Melantias, and trust God, and when the day is won, the Horn will come up out of the ground in the Great Church and find its heir." He put his hand on Petros's shoulder. "And its heir will be there, in front of the whole City, as the least of the Shepherd's house. The one who went up the mountain proud and came down humbled. I'll give him my crown with my own hands."

Petros bowed his head.

Stephanos stood in the middle of the refectory and felt the words go through him like warm water. *The one who went up the mountain proud and came down humbled.* It was his own reading. It was what he had said in the salt cell with the lamp burning in the niche. It was exactly what he had said.

He looked at the Patriarch. The old man was looking at his hands.

He looked at the Abba.

He had not seen him until now. The old man was sitting on a stool in the corner of the refectory, by the fire, in his patched grey habit, with his white head bowed and his hands folded in his lap. He was facing the room, but his clouded eyes were looking at nothing. He looked smaller than Stephanos remembered. He looked very old.

He had not said a word.

"And your condition, Majesty?" said Stephanos. "The Domestikos's condition. For setting aside the charge."

Bardas turned back to him.

"Ride with me," said the Emperor. "Tomorrow. At the bridge. Carry the purple of the Tagma at my right hand." He smiled. "And carry the other one too. The red eagle of Taron. Petros says you brought it back from the Goldhand's hall. I'd be proud to have it on the field. Every Armenian in the host will fight twice as hard when they see it."

---

He said yes.

He said it standing in the refectory of the Fool's Tower with the chapter of the Tagma along the walls, and the Emperor's hand held out to him, and Petros smiling behind the Emperor's chair, and the Abba silent on his stool by the fire. He knelt, and the Domestikos came forward and put the purple cloak of the Tagma back on his shoulders, and the chapter struck their lance-butts on the floor three times, and it was done.

He did not look at Dora. She was standing in the doorway behind him, with Amma Thekla. He felt her there. He did not turn round.

---

She found him in the armoury that night.

He was polishing the rings of his mail by lamplight, the way he had on the night Theophanes fell through the postern. The red eagle of Taron lay unrolled on the bench beside him. He had been looking at it for an hour. He had taken the needle and thread from the armoury box and begun to mend the old torn corner properly, with small tight stitches, the way a sailmaker would, and he had got halfway and stopped.

"You're going," said Dora.

He did not look up. "Yes."

"You're going with the Emperor. To the bridge. You're going to carry that." She looked at the banner. "The Goldhand's banner."

"It's not the Goldhand's. It's Taron's."

"It was the Goldhand's for nine years. He kept it in a chest in his treasury next to the church silver he stole." Her voice was quiet. "You said on the ship it didn't matter what flag it was. You said your cousin died for the men behind it. You said..."

"I know what I said."

"Then why?"

He put down the mail. He looked at her. She was standing in the doorway of the armoury in her ragged dress with the veil of the Myrrh-bearer over her hair and the sling of Poimen wound round her wrist, and she was so small. She had always been small. He had not noticed until now how small.

"Because it's the only chance, Dora," he said. "The Hunter will break the walls. The Emperor's right about that. He'll break them in a day. And then he'll come into the City and it'll be the fall all over again, the burning and the chains and the long road east. The only chance is to stop him at the bridge. Every lance counts. Every man."

"And the Horn?"

"The Horn will come up when the day is won. In front of the whole City."

"Who says?"

"The Emperor."

"Who says to the Emperor?" said Dora. "The man with no shadow. You know it. You saw the wall." She came into the armoury. "Stephanos. You know it's him. You know he's the one who tells people to take the crown. You know he's the one who says don't look for the Horn, don't go down, just fight, just be strong, and it'll all come right. He's telling the Emperor exactly what you want to hear. He's telling Petros exactly what Petros wants to hear. And he's telling you." She stopped in front of him. "The strong will seek it, and it won't rise for them. That's what the Word says. And you're going to ride out tomorrow with the strongest army in the empire, under a golden eagle, to win it by being strong."

"We're not seeking the Horn. We're fighting the Hunter."

"It's the same thing. You know it's the same thing." Her voice was shaking now. "*When the least of the Shepherd's house goes down, the Horn will come up.* Goes down, Stephanos. Not rides out. Goes down. Somebody has to go down under the church. Somebody has to go down to the Gate. Not after. Now. Before."

"And who would you send?"

"Me," said Dora.

He stared at her.

"Me," she said again. "I'm of the Shepherd's house. I'm the youngest. I'm the least. I don't care if the Horn anoints me. I don't think it will. I don't care. Somebody has to go down, and I'm the one the Word says, and I'll go. Kale will come, and Lazaros, and the Amma. And you." She looked up at him. "Come with me. Please. Not to the bridge. Down."

For a long moment he could not speak.

He looked at her face. He looked at the small rough hands, and the sling wound round the thin wrist. He thought of the Gate of Sheol. He thought of what waited at the bottom of the Undercroft, below the crypts and the cisterns and the catacombs of the apostates, in the dark where the Nephilim seers had cracked the floor of the sanctuary a hundred years ago and not all of them had come back.

He thought of the Goldhand's Mint, and the Garden Below, and the barrow of Gathos. He thought of Petros on his knees by the pillar with his face broken.

"No," he said.

"Stephanos..."

"No. You're a child, Dora. You're thirteen years old. You don't know what's down there. Nobody does." He heard his voice getting louder and could not stop it. "You'll stay here. In the Tower. With the Abba and the Amma. It's the safest place in the City. The walls here are twelve feet thick and there are forty Hornbearers on them. If the worst comes, the Abba will get you out through the postern and down to the harbour. That's what you'll do."

"And the Horn?"

"The Horn has waited a hundred years. It can wait one more day." He picked up the mail again. His hands were shaking. "Tomorrow we'll break the Hunter at the bridge. And then we'll come home, and the Horn will come up, and you'll see. You'll see, Dora. It'll be all right."

She did not say anything.

He polished the mail. He did not look at her. After a long time, he heard her go out of the armoury and up the stair, and her footsteps went away above him, light and quick, and were gone.

---

He went to the Abba last, near midnight.

The old man was in his room at the top of the Tower, on his stool by the window, facing the dark. The window looked west, toward Thrace. The whole western sky was red with the light of burning villages.

Stephanos knelt beside the stool and kissed the old man's hand.

"You're riding out," said the Abba.

"Yes, Abba."

"With the Emperor. Under the red eagle."

"Yes."

The Abba nodded slowly. He did not say anything else. The red light from the window lay on his white hair and his clouded eyes.

"Abba," said Stephanos. "I know the answer."

The old man turned his face toward him.

"The Question," said Stephanos. "*Whom will the Horn anoint?* I know. I've known since the Mint. I didn't want to say it, because you always said nobody could know, and I thought I was being proud. But I know. The four Words say so. The Emperor's messenger says so. It's Petros. The lame lord's great-grandson. The one who went up the mountain proud and came down humbled. The least of the Shepherd's house." His voice cracked. "I found him, Abba. After thirty years. I found him. Tomorrow we'll win at the bridge and the Horn will come up and you'll hear the whole City cheering. You'll hear it from here."

The Abba was silent for a long time.

Then he reached out one thin dry hand and laid it on Stephanos's head, the way he had nine years ago on the day Stephanos took his vows.

"My son," he said. "Do you remember the story of Samuel?"

"Yes, Abba."

"Do you remember what Samuel said, when Eliab stood in front of him? Before God spoke to him?"

Stephanos was silent.

"He said, *Surely the Lord's anointed is before him*," said the Abba softly. "*Surely.* He was so sure. He'd been waiting so long, and he was so tired, and there was the tallest son in the room, and he was sure." The hand on Stephanos's head was very light. "Samuel was a good man, Stephanos. The best of his day. It wasn't wickedness that made him say it. It was hope."

"Abba..."

"Go and sleep," said the old man. "You ride at dawn." He took his hand away. "Come back. That's all. Come back."

Stephanos rose. At the door he stopped, as he had stopped on the night of the giant at Charisios, and turned.

"Abba. Will you ask it? The Question? Tonight?"

The old man had turned back to the window. The red light of the burning lay on his face.

"I ask it every night," said Pelagios the Fool. "I'll ask it tonight. I'll ask it for both of us."

---

Theophanes went out of the Fool's Tower an hour before dawn, by the postern, while the chapter was at Matins.

Nobody saw him go. He went down the lane along the inside of the wall, past the old cisterns of Aetios, and through the dark streets toward the Mese, where the silk merchants had their shops. He knew the way. He had walked it every week for two years, carrying letters.

The shop of Master Ioannes the silk merchant was shuttered. Theophanes knocked on the side door, three times, and then twice, and waited.

The door opened a crack. A lamp. A round, careful face with gold rings glinting on the fingers that held the lamp.

"You," said Master Ioannes.

"I have something for the Master," said Theophanes. His voice was steady. It surprised him how steady it was. "Something he asked for. Will you see it gets to him? Tonight? Before the Emperor rides out?"

Master Ioannes looked at him for a long moment. Then he held out his hand.

Theophanes opened the purple book. He took out from inside the back cover the folded parchment, sealed with candle wax. He held it for a moment. Then he put it in the silk merchant's soft hand.

"He'll know what it is," Theophanes said.

The door closed.

He stood in the dark street for a long time afterward, alone, with the purple book in his arms. Then he turned and walked back toward the Fool's Tower through the empty streets. The sky over the Land Walls was beginning to grey. He could hear, far off, from the direction of the Golden Gate, the first horns sounding, and the tramp of horses on stone.

He did not go in by the postern. He sat down on the step outside it, with his back against the old wall, and opened the book to the page where the four Words of Symeon were written in burnt twig, and read them over and over, until the sun came up and the horns had all gone quiet and the army of the Emperor Bardas had ridden out of the Golden Gate toward Melantias, and he was the only one left in the lane.

---

Stephanos rode at the Emperor's right hand.

He had the purple of the Tagma on his lance, and behind him, on a pole carried by an Armenian cataphract from the Emperor's guard, the red eagle of Taron, with its torn corner half-mended. The morning was bright and cold. The sun came up over the City behind them and threw their shadows out long across the road ahead, toward the smoke.

There were eleven thousand of them. It was the largest army the City had put in the field since the fall. Hornbearers and cataphracts and Akritai and the levies of the lords of the Sword faction, and the Emperor's own guard in gilded scale, and behind them, the men of the City's wards with spears and shields. Kale was not among them. She was on the Land Walls with the archers, where the Eparch had asked her to be. Lazaros was in the Great Church with the Patriarch, guarding the icon of Michael over the crack in the sanctuary floor. Sergios had vanished in the night, the way he did.

Petros rode on the Emperor's left, in his gilded armour, with the sword of the red stone at his hip. He looked across at Stephanos once, as they came out of the Golden Gate onto the Thracian road, and smiled, and raised his hand.

And behind the Emperor, a little way back, on a white horse, rode a tall man in a plain white travelling cloak, with pale gold hair and a face like a face from an icon. He rode with his hood back, in the full light of the morning sun. People turned to look at him as he passed, and their faces softened, and some of them crossed themselves.

Stephanos did not look at the ground behind the white horse. He did not look to see whether the stranger's shadow ran out long across the road ahead with all the others, toward the smoke and the waiting Hunter.

He did not look. He kept his eyes on the road. He rode on.
