## Chapter 14: The Barrow of Gathos

*The sling of Poimen. Braided from the wool of the Shepherd's first flock. His hand wore the grip smooth. Yours will not fit it until you stop wanting to.*

*Inscription on a reliquary case, church of Saint George of the Rock*

---

The east face of Argaeus was a graveyard.

Kale led them up it in the last light of the equinox day, by a path that was hardly a path at all: a goat track that wound up among boulders and scree and patches of old snow stained red with the rust-rain. The wind came down off the summit like a knife. Below them, the whole of Cappadocia spread out brown and grey in the dusk, and the valley of Göreme was a small dark crease far off to the west, with a few lights in it.

The graves began a thousand feet up. They were cut into the rock of the mountainside: rows of them, low rectangular openings with stone doors, like the doors of ovens. Many of the doors were broken. Some stood open. The dark inside them breathed out a cold, stale air that smelled of old bone.

"Giants," said Kale. "Gathos's people. His warriors, buried round him the way the old kings were. The barrow's in the middle of the mountain. These go down to it." She stopped at one of the broken doors and crouched and looked in. "This one. It goes all the way down. I can feel it."

Nobody asked how she knew anymore.

"Rephaim," said Amma Thekla. She was leaning on Lazaros's arm, breathing hard. The climb had been bad for her. "Where the giants are buried, the Rephaim climb out. Shades of the dead ones. They don't like the living."

"Can we get past them?"

"We can bury them," said the old woman. "It's what they want, mostly. A proper burial. They never had one. Nobody says the prayers over a giant." She straightened up. "I'll say them. Theophanes will help me. He remembers them now."

Theophanes, who had not said a word all the way up the mountain, nodded. He had the purple book in his satchel. He had been reading it every night by the fire since Thessalonike, and he had been quieter every night.

Stephanos lit the lamps. He looked back down the mountain once, toward the south face, where the road from Göreme climbed toward the barrow's great front door. He could not see it from here. He could not see Petros.

Then he stooped and went in through the broken door, into the dark.

---

The tombs went down a long way.

They were built for giants, and everything in them was too big. The passages were twelve feet high and eight wide. The steps were knee-high and uneven. The burial chambers opened off the passages on either side, and in each one a giant lay on a stone shelf, or what was left of one: great bones, yellow and brown, with scraps of rusted iron armour clinging to them, and grave-goods heaped around them. Axes. Maces. Helmets like cauldrons. Rings big enough to wear as bracelets. Gold, some of it. Stephanos saw a torque of solid gold as thick as his wrist, lying on the chest of a skeleton eleven feet long.

Nobody touched anything.

The Rephaim came in the third passage.

Stephanos felt them before he saw them. The air turned cold, colder than the tomb-cold, the kind of cold that goes into the bones and stays there. The lamps dimmed. Their flames shrank down to blue beads on the wicks, and would not grow no matter how Lazaros trimmed them. And out of the burial chambers on either side of the passage, out of the rock itself, came shapes.

They were tall and grey and thin, like shadows cast by a low sun, and they had no faces. Where their faces should have been, there was a darker grey, like smoke. They moved without sound. They drifted out into the passage and stood between the band and the way down, a dozen of them, twenty, swaying slightly, like weeds in a current. And the cold came off them in waves.

One of them reached for Stephanos. He did not see it move. One moment it was standing ten feet off, and the next its hand was on his arm, grey and weightless as fog.

It went through his armour as if the armour were not there. It went through his skin. He felt it touch the bone of his forearm, and the cold of it was so terrible that he screamed. He struck at it with his sword, and the sword passed through it and hit the rock wall and struck sparks. The hand stayed on his arm. The cold spread up toward his shoulder.

"Don't fight it!" Thekla's voice. "It's not the enemy!"

"Amma, it's killing me!"

"It doesn't know it's killing you. It doesn't know anything. It's lost." The old woman's voice was right beside him. "Lord, have mercy. Christ, have mercy. Lord, have mercy."

She was praying the burial prayers. The same ones she had prayed in the crypt beneath the Great Church, over the bound emperor. And Theophanes beside her, stumbling, then steadier, reading them out of the back of the purple book where he had copied them.

*With the spirits of the righteous made perfect, give rest, O Saviour, to the souls of your servants.*

"They're not servants of God," Theophanes broke off. "Amma, they're giants. Nephilim. The sons of the Watchers. They were never..."

"They were made by God before they were bent," said Thekla. "Everything was. Pray."

He prayed.

The hand on Stephanos's arm hesitated. He felt it. The cold stopped spreading. It stayed where it was, a band of ice around his forearm, but it did not move.

*Give rest, O Saviour, to the souls of your servants, in a place of light, a place of green pasture, a place of rest.*

The grey shape in front of him swayed. The dark smoke where its face should be shifted, and for a moment Stephanos saw something in it. A face. A young face, heavy-browed and broad, a giant's face, but young, and frightened, and very tired.

*From which all pain and sorrow and sighing have fled away.*

The hand let go.

The shape drifted back from him. It stood in the passage with the others, and they were all swaying now, all of them, like a field of grass when the wind drops. And then, one by one, they sank. They went down into the rock floor of the passage the way water sinks into sand, slowly, quietly, and were gone.

The lamps flared up again, bright and yellow.

Stephanos looked at his arm. There was a mark on it, through the sleeve, where the grey hand had been: a white band around the forearm, like frostbite. It did not hurt anymore. It did not feel like anything at all.

"That will not heal," said Thekla. She was leaning against the wall with her eyes closed. "Not without a physician who has the gift, or the burial of the one who touched you. And we've buried him. So it will heal." She opened her eyes. "Slowly."

"Thank you, Amma."

"Thank Theophanes. I only started it."

Theophanes was standing in the middle of the passage with the purple book open in his hands, staring at the floor where the Rephaim had sunk. His face was white. His lips were still moving, very slightly, as if he had not realised he had stopped.

---

The lamp niche was in the lowest of the burial chambers, where the giant tombs gave way to older stone.

It was not a giant's work. Stephanos saw that at once. The niche had been cut by men, by human hands, small and careful, into the rock of a cave wall that had been there long before the giants came to bury their dead. The plaster at the back of it was white and clean. The iron hook in the top of it was empty.

"There was a church here," said Lazaros quietly. "Before Gathos. A hermit's cell. Somebody lived here, alone on the mountain, and kept a lamp burning."

"And the giants buried their king on top of him," said Kale.

"Or he came here to watch the giants," said Thekla. "Some of the old ones did that. Kept a lamp burning in the enemy's house, to remind the enemy it was there." She sat down on a fallen stone. "Light it."

They had no clay lamp of Saint Mamas this time, and no orphans' candles. But Lazaros had bought oil in Göreme, a flask of it, the best olive oil in the valley, and he had blessed it himself the night before with the old prayers. He filled Kale's small bronze lamp from it and hung it on the hook.

The words came up out of the plaster.

*The strong will seek it, and it will not rise for them.*

They stood looking at it.

"The strong will seek it," said Theophanes slowly. He was copying it into the back of the purple book with the burnt twig. His hand was shaking again. "And it will not rise for them."

Stephanos felt something go through him like a cold wind.

Then he thought: *Bardas.*

He thought it clearly, and all at once, and it was a relief. He saw Bardas in the Magnaura, tall and golden and splendid, with the wooden cross around his neck, saying: *I will ask the Patriarch to anoint me.* He saw the Domestikos with his hand on Stephanos's shoulder, saying: *Think what the Tagma could do with a crown to ride behind.* The strong. The Sword faction. The ones who wanted to choose the heir themselves, by strength, and have God bless it afterward.

*It will not rise for them.*

"The Sword faction," he said. "The Domestikos. Bardas. They're seeking it. They want to crown Bardas with the Great Church's oil and call it the Horn. That's what this means. It won't rise for them. It won't come to a man they choose."

Theophanes looked up at him from the book. His face was very strange.

"Is that what it means?" he said.

"What else could it mean?"

Theophanes opened his mouth. Then he shut it again, and looked down at the book, and did not answer.

Dora, standing beside the niche with the veil of the Myrrh-bearer over her hair, was looking at Stephanos too. She did not say anything either. She looked at him for a long moment, the way she had looked at him across the Goldhand's hall when he picked up the banner. Then she looked away.

From somewhere below them, far down in the mountain, came a sound like a building falling down.

It was laughter.

---

The barrow of Gathos was a hall under the mountain, and it was the size of the Great Church.

They came into it from above, by a narrow crack in the roof that Kale found at the bottom of the lowest giant grave. They crouched on a ledge high up in the dark and looked down.

Torches burned on the walls, huge ones, whole pine trunks soaked in pitch and set in iron brackets, and their smoke rolled up into the vault above. The floor was black rock, polished smooth. Down both sides of the hall stood columns, carved into the shapes of giants, standing with their arms folded and their heads bowed, holding up the roof. Between the columns, iron was growing: great dark branching veins of it, spreading out across the floor and up the walls like the roots of a tree.

At the far end of the hall, on a dais of black stone, stood a sarcophagus. It was the length of three men laid end to end, carved from a single block of dark red stone, and its lid was a figure: a giant lying on his back with a sword on his breast, his stone face turned up to the vault. Gathos.

And in front of the sarcophagus, in the middle of the hall, Petros was fighting Anak son of Gathos.

Stephanos had never seen a giant like him. The Gibborim at Charisios and at the ford had been twice the height of a man. Anak was more. He stood nearly four metres tall, and he was massive, broad as a barn door across the shoulders, with arms like tree trunks and hands that could have closed around a man's waist. He wore iron: plates of it, overlapping, rusty and scarred, and iron grew through his skin at the joints, veins of it, dark and branching. On his head sat a crown, a plain heavy circle of black iron with nine points. His face was broad and heavy and handsome in a brutal way, with a black beard braided with iron rings, and his eyes were small and pale and full of a vast, delighted contempt.

On his shoulder, something sat.

Stephanos could not see it clearly. It was like a shadow, or a smudge, or the shape a heat haze makes on a summer road. It had no form. But it was there, perched on the giant's right shoulder like a falcon on a falconer's wrist, and when he looked at it directly, his eyes watered and slid away, and he felt a fear come up in him that had nothing to do with the giant at all. It was the fear of a small child in the dark who knows something is in the room.

The Prince of the Heights.

Anak held a maul. It was an iron hammer with a head the size of a wine barrel, on a haft as long as a man, and he swung it one-handed, lazily, the way a man swings a stick to knock the heads off thistles.

Petros was on one knee.

His shield was gone. It lay in pieces across the black floor, the purple paint scattered. His helm was gone. His face was bloody. His mail was torn at the shoulder, and blood was running down his left arm, and he was holding his sword in his right hand with the point on the floor, leaning on it, trying to stand.

"*Get up,*" said Anak. His voice was huge. It filled the hall like thunder. "*Get up, heir of the Shepherd! Get up! I heard you! I heard every word! You were going to cut off my head! You were going to set it on a pole on top of the church! Get up and do it!*"

He laughed. The whole mountain shook with it.

"*Every word you said came up the mountain, boy. Every boast. I heard them all. I ate them. They were delicious.*" Anak spread his arms. "*Do you know how strong you made me? Eleven years I've been eating the fear of that valley, and it was never as good as this. Your pride. Your mouth. Your big, brave, shining mouth. I'm stronger tonight than I've ever been.*"

Petros pushed himself up. He stood swaying. He raised his sword.

Anak hit him with the flat of the maul, almost gently. Petros flew twenty feet through the air and struck one of the giant columns and fell to the floor at its feet, and lay still.

"*Stay down, then,*" said Anak. "*I'll finish you in a moment. I want to enjoy it.*"

---

Stephanos went down the wall.

He did not remember deciding to. He did not remember how he got down; later, Kale told him that he had jumped from the ledge to the top of one of the giant columns, twenty feet, and slid down the carved giant's back to the floor, and she had never seen anything so stupid in her life. He remembered only the floor under his feet, and his sword in his hand, and the giant turning toward him.

Anak looked down at him. The small pale eyes widened with delight.

"*Another one!*" said the giant. "*Another knight in purple! Is this a procession? Are they sending you up one at a time now?*"

"I'm here for the boy," said Stephanos.

"*The boy is mine. He came up the mountain as champion. He lost. That's the rule.*" Anak hefted the maul. "*Unless you want to take his place. I can't refuse a challenge. That's the rule too. My grandfather couldn't either. It's how the shepherd killed him.*" He grinned. His teeth were iron. "*Do you challenge me, little knight?*"

"I challenge you."

"*Good!*"

The maul came down.

Stephanos threw himself sideways. The iron head struck the black floor where he had been standing and the floor cracked, and splinters of rock flew up and rattled off his armour like hail. He rolled and came up and cut at the giant's leg, at the back of the knee, the way he had at Charisios. The sword struck iron plate and rang and bounced off.

Anak laughed and kicked him.

He went across the floor on his back. His breath was gone. He could not see. He lay there with the vault of the hall spinning over him and the torches smearing into lines of fire, and he heard Anak's footsteps coming, huge and slow, shaking the rock.

A stone struck the giant in the face.

It was a small stone. Stephanos heard it, a sharp crack like a knuckle rapping wood. Anak stopped. He put his hand up to his forehead. When he took it away, there was blood on his fingers, a thin red line.

"*Who...*"

Another stone. It struck him on the cheekbone, just below the eye, and he flinched and roared.

Stephanos turned his head.

Mikros was standing in the great doorway at the far end of the hall, the front door, the one Petros had come in by. The shepherd boy was alone. He had no armour, no sword, nothing but his sheepskin coat and his sling. He was whirling it again. His face was white and set and terrified, and his hand did not shake at all.

He let fly. The third stone struck Anak in the mouth, and one of the iron teeth broke.

"*A child,*" said Anak. His voice had changed. It was thick with rage. "*A child. A shepherd's brat. With a sling.*" He turned toward the door. "*They sent me a child with a sling.*"

And the thing on his shoulder moved.

Stephanos felt it move. He felt the fear come off it in a wave, black and cold, like the Rephaim's cold but worse, a cold that went straight into the soul. He saw Mikros stagger in the doorway. He saw the sling drop from the boy's fingers. He saw the boy's face crumple into terror, and saw him fall to his knees, and put his hands over his head, and begin to scream.

It went through all of them. Stephanos heard Kale cry out on the ledge above. He heard Lazaros moan. He felt it himself, the fear, black and huge, pressing him down against the floor, telling him that he was small, and weak, and would die here under the mountain with his face in the dirt, and no one would ever remember his name.

And then a voice rang out across the hall, high and cracked and hysterical with laughter.

"Look at him! Look at the giant! Twelve feet tall and afraid of a boy with a stick!"

Sergios was standing on the lid of Gathos's sarcophagus.

Nobody had seen him get there. He was standing on the stone chest of the dead giant, on the carved stone sword, in his rags, with his arms flung wide and his beard sticking out in every direction, and he was laughing so hard he could barely stand.

"Look at him!" the fool shrieked. "Look at the great big crowned king of the mountain, bleeding from a pebble! Oh, his poor tooth! His poor little iron tooth! Did the shepherd boy hurt you? Do you want your grandfather? He's right here! I'm standing on him!" Sergios jumped up and down on the sarcophagus lid. "Wake up, Gathos! Wake up! Your grandson's crying! A goatherd threw a stone at him!"

Anak turned around.

His face was dark red under the black beard. The veins of iron in his skin were standing out like cords. The thing on his shoulder was writhing.

"*Get off,*" said Anak. "*Get off my grandfather.*"

"Make me!" Sergios danced. "Make me, big man! Oh, you can't, can you? You can't touch me! I'm a fool! Fools don't count! Nobody's afraid of a fool! And you, you're afraid of everything! You're afraid of a boy with a sling, because a boy with a sling killed your grandfather! You've been afraid of it your whole life! That's why you make them send champions! So you can kill a boy with a sling every year and prove you're not afraid!" The fool's voice cracked with laughter. "And you still are! You still are! Look at your hands shake!"

Anak charged.

He went down the hall toward the sarcophagus in three huge strides, with the maul raised over his head in both hands, roaring. He did not look where he was going. He did not see the great lintel of stone over the dais, where the roof came down low above his grandfather's tomb.

He struck it with his forehead at full speed.

The sound was like a church bell dropped from a tower. The whole hall shook. Dust and stones rained from the vault. Anak's head snapped back. The iron crown flew off and spun away across the floor, ringing. The giant stood swaying under the lintel with his eyes rolled up white, and the maul slipped out of his hands and fell on his own foot, and he did not seem to notice.

Then he fell to his knees.

He knelt at the foot of his grandfather's tomb with his head hanging, and blood running from his forehead into his beard, and the thing on his shoulder was gone. Stephanos did not see it go. It was not there anymore.

Sergios was standing on the sarcophagus above him, looking down, not laughing now.

"There," said the fool softly. "There you are. You're not so big when you're kneeling, are you?"

---

It was not over.

Stephanos knew it was not over the moment he got to his feet. Anak was kneeling, and bleeding, and stunned. But he was not dead. As Stephanos watched, the giant's white eyes rolled back down. The small pale pupils found him. And Anak reached up, with both enormous hands, and took hold of the lid of his grandfather's sarcophagus, with the fool still standing on it.

He tore it off.

Sergios went flying. The lid of red stone, the carved figure of Gathos with the sword on his breast, came up off the tomb in Anak's hands like a door torn from its hinges, and the giant heaved himself to his feet with it held in front of him like a shield. It was ten feet long and must have weighed as much as a cart full of stone. He held it as if it were a plank.

"*Now,*" said Anak thickly. Blood was running into his eyes. "*Now. Come on. Come on, all of you.*"

Kale's arrows struck the stone lid and shattered. Stephanos ran at him, and Anak swung the lid like a club and struck him full on the shield, and he flew backward across the hall and hit the floor and slid, and his shield arm went numb and then began to scream. He heard something crack in his wrist.

He lay on his back among the scattered bones and grave-goods that had tumbled out of the open sarcophagus. Gold everywhere. Rusted iron. A giant's skull, grinning at him. And his own sword, somewhere far off across the floor, out of reach.

Anak was coming.

He could not get up. He tried. His right knee, the one he had hurt at Charisios, would not hold him. He got as far as his knees and stopped there, kneeling in the grave-goods with his left arm hanging useless, and he looked up at the giant coming toward him with the stone lid raised.

He thought: *This is where I die.*

He did not feel angry. It surprised him. He had always thought that when he came to die, he would be angry. He felt only very tired, and very small, and he thought of the Abba sitting by his window, and he thought, *I didn't find him. I thought I had. I thought it was Petros, and look at him, lying by the column. I don't know, Abba. I don't know whom the Horn will anoint. I never knew.*

His right hand went to his belt.

The sling was there. The old sling, the plain leather one that he had carried for nine years and practised with every morning because the rule required it. He took it off his belt without thinking. His fingers found a stone among the grave-goods, round and smooth, a giant's gaming piece or a bead from a broken necklace, he did not know.

Ring finger. Pinch the other end. Like a feather.

Don't think about it.

He did not think about it. He knelt in the gold and the bones with his arm broken and his knee ruined, a small man on his knees in the dark, and he whirled the sling once over his head, and let go.

The stone struck Anak son of Gathos in the temple, just behind the left eye, where the skull is thin.

The giant stopped.

He stood very still in the middle of the hall with the stone lid held up over his head. His face did not change. Then, slowly, the lid began to tilt. It tilted backward, and his arms went with it, and his body went with his arms, and Anak son of Gathos fell over backward like a tree, the way his grandfather had fallen at the Cilician Gates three hundred years before, and the lid came down on top of him with a sound like the end of the world.

He did not get up.

---

Stephanos did not remember much of what came after.

He remembered Kale's face above him. He remembered Lazaros binding his wrist with strips of his own cloak, and the pain of it, white and sharp. He remembered Amma Thekla kneeling beside Petros by the column, with her hands on his chest, praying, and Petros opening his eyes and weeping. He remembered Mikros, in the doorway, sitting against the wall with his knees drawn up and his sling in his lap, staring at the fallen giant, not moving.

And he remembered Dora.

She had come down from the ledge with the others. She had gone first to Petros, and knelt by him, and held his hand while the Amma prayed. Then she had gone to Sergios, who was sitting on the floor by the empty sarcophagus rubbing his head and humming. And then she had gone to the sarcophagus itself, and looked in.

"There's something in here," she said.

Stephanos made himself get up. Kale tried to stop him. He shook her off and limped across the floor, and stood beside the girl, and looked down into the open tomb.

The bones of Gathos lay in it. They were enormous, yellow, very old. The skull was the size of a wine cask, and in the middle of its forehead, above the eye sockets, there was a hole. A small, round hole, the size of a river stone.

And on the giant's breastbone, where his hands had once been folded, lay a sling.

It was very old and very plain. It was braided from wool, undyed, the natural cream-white of a sheep's fleece, gone grey and brown with age. The pouch in the middle was leather, worn soft and shiny as silk. One of the cords had a loop at the end for the finger. The other was plain.

Nobody had taken it. For three hundred years it had lain on the breast of the giant it had killed, and the giants had buried it with him, so no one could ever use it again.

"The sling of Poimen," whispered Theophanes.

Stephanos reached down into the tomb and picked it up.

It was light. It was lighter than he expected, lighter than his own sling, as if it were made of nothing. The wool was soft as a lamb's fleece. He held it in his right hand and looked at it, and he felt something rise in his chest so strong and so sudden that he could not breathe. It was the feeling he had had on the island in the Cisterns with the lame lord's letter in his hand. Hope. And under the hope, close behind it, hunger.

*The Shepherd's own sling. His hand wore the grip smooth.*

He put the loop over his ring finger.

It did not fit.

It was too small. It had been made for a smaller hand than his. He pushed, and it would not go past the first knuckle. He tried his little finger, and it slid on easily and hung loose, too big, useless. He took the other cord between his thumb and the side of his finger, like a feather, the way Dora had taught him, and it slipped. It would not stay. The worn leather of the grip was polished smooth by another hand, a hand that had held it ten thousand times, and it did not know his.

He tried again. He tried for a long time, standing there in the ruin of the barrow with his broken arm in its sling and the others watching him in silence. It would not fit.

At last he held it out to Petros. Petros had got to his feet, leaning on Kale. His face was bruised and bloody. He took the sling of Poimen and looked at it, and put the loop on his finger. It was far too small for him. It would not go on at all. He looked at it a long time. Then he handed it back without a word, and turned away.

Stephanos stood holding it.

"Let me see," said Dora.

He gave it to her. He did not know why. She was standing at his elbow, looking up at it, and he gave it to her.

She put the loop over her ring finger. It slid on as if it had been made for her. She took the other cord between her thumb and the side of her finger, and it settled there, and stayed. The worn leather of the pouch lay in the curve of her small, rough, calloused palm, and it fit. It fit exactly.

She looked at it for a moment, surprised. Then she bent and picked up a pebble from the floor, and whirled the sling once over her head, lightly, without looking, and let go.

The pebble went the whole length of the barrow hall, a hundred paces, through the torchlight and the smoke, and struck the iron crown of Anak where it lay on the floor by the far wall, and the crown rang like a bell.

Nobody said anything.

Dora took the sling off her finger. She held it out to Stephanos.

"It's yours," she said. "You found it."

He did not take it. He stood looking at her, and at the old sling in her small brown hand, and he felt a thing he had no name for. It was not anger. It was nearer to fear.

"Keep it," he said at last. "For now. You're better with a sling than any of us. You can keep it until we get home."

She looked at him a long moment with her dark eyes under the veil. Then she nodded, and wound the sling of Poimen around her wrist, and tucked the end in.

Across the hall, in the doorway, Mikros was watching her too.

---

They came down the mountain at dawn, carrying Petros between them on a litter made of spears and a cloak. Behind them, far up on the south face of Argaeus, smoke was rising from the barrow's great front door, black and thick. Hagop's knights had gone in after them, when the herald did not come back to the valley, and found the giant dead under his grandfather's coffin lid, and they had burned the barrow with pitch and brushwood and sealed the door.

The people of Göreme were waiting at the foot of the valley road. All of them. The whole valley had come out of their caves and their chimneys in the grey light, and they stood along the road in their sheepskins and their black wool, silent, watching the band come down.

When they saw the iron crown in Kale's hands, a sound went up from them that Stephanos had never heard from a crowd before. It was not a cheer. It was a great ragged sob, from a thousand throats at once.

The Concord's Scribe of the Treasury was standing in the square beside his carriage, with his leather folder under his arm. He watched the band come down the road. He watched the crowd. He watched Father Basil, the old blind priest, walk out from among them to the Scribe's soldiers and say something too quiet for Stephanos to hear. Then the Scribe got into his carriage, and the soldiers fell in behind it, and they went away down the valley road with their six empty wagons, and nobody watched them go.

The iron crown of Anak went up on the top of the church chimney that afternoon. Petros did not put it there. He could not stand. Mikros climbed the chimney with it on his back and set it on the top, and came down again, and the whole valley watched him do it, and nobody cheered, and nobody needed to.

---

They were still in Göreme two days later, in the refectory of Saint George of the Rock, when the riders came.

There were three of them, Hagop's scouts, on lathered horses, and they had ridden all night from the west. They came into the refectory still in their cloaks with the dust of the road on them, and the first one knelt in front of the Count and spoke fast, in Armenian, and Hagop's face went slowly grey.

Stephanos understood Armenian. He heard all of it.

Lord Bardas Argyros had won a great victory on the Sangarios. He had caught Anak's raiders at the fords at dawn, with four hundred men and twenty Hornbearers, and broken them, and taken back the children, all of them, alive. He had come home to the City in triumph at the Feast of the Theophany, with the children riding on the knights' saddles in front of them and the people weeping on the walls.

A week later, on the Feast of the Presentation, in the Hippodrome of the City, before twenty thousand people, the Domestikos of the Tagma had anointed Lord Bardas Argyros Emperor of the Romans with oil from the lamps of the Great Church. The Patriarch had not been present. The Patriarch had refused. But the Sword faction had carried it. The lords had knelt. The Hornbearers had raised their lances. And Bardas had stood on the kathisma of the Hippodrome in the imperial purple and wept, and promised to serve until God took the crown back.

"And Nebrodes," said the scout. "Nebrodes has crossed the Euphrates. His host is on the march. The Concord's Iron Host is with him, and giant kings from Armenia and the Caucasus, and seers, and they say the ground shakes for a mile around where he walks. They say he is coming west. They say he means to reach the City by midsummer."

Hagop said nothing for a long time.

Then he looked at Stephanos, and said in Greek: "There's a letter for you. From the City. It came with them."

The scout held it out. It was sealed with the seal of the Tagma of the Horn, the horn above the stone. Stephanos broke it with his good hand.

It was from the Domestikos. It was short.

*Brother Stephanos. The Emperor has need of every lance. You are summoned to the City to answer for your broken oath, and to ride with the Emperor against the Hunter. Bring the Thracian boy. The Emperor has heard of him, and wishes to meet the Shepherd's other heir. Nikephoros.*

Stephanos read it twice. Then he folded it and put it inside his breastplate, beside the lame lord's letter, against the word Justice.

At the end of the table, Theophanes was sitting with the purple book open in front of him, at the page where he had copied the three Words of Symeon. He was not reading them. He was looking at Stephanos. And Stephanos saw in his face, clearly, for the first time, the thing that had been there since the niche in the mountain.

It was doubt. And it was not doubt about the Words.
