## Chapter 5: The Question Unasked

*Obedience is the first vow and the hardest. Anyone can die for Christ once. Try doing what you are told for forty years.*

*Abba Pelagios, to his novices*

---

They came up out of the Undercroft at dusk on the second day, filthy and soaked to the waist, with nine scribes roped together between them and eleven sacks of letters on their backs.

Amma Thekla was waiting for them at the top of the stair. Behind her, in the crypt, the three bound tombs stood open, and in each one the dead lay still with their hands folded, and a new candle burned on each lid. The old woman had been praying for thirty hours. Her voice was a whisper and her hands were shaking, but the basket in her lap was finished, and she had started another.

"They sleep," she said. "Somebody should close the lids."

"Somebody will, Amma," Stephanos said.

"Good." She looked up at him, and her black eyes went to his breastplate, to the place over his heart where the letter was. "You found it."

"How did you know?"

"I told you. It had your name on it." She held up a hand, and he helped her to her feet. She weighed nothing. "Now the hard part starts."

---

The Eparch took the scribes. She took them quietly, in the middle of the night, through a side gate of the Praetorium, and by morning they had told her everything: the letters, the seals, the seer, the plan for the Feast of the Cross. By noon the captain of the Golden Gate had been arrested, and two guild masters, and a deacon of the Great Church who had been carrying the Patriarch's private correspondence to the Milion every evening for two years and handing it to a man in a grey robe.

Master Ioannes the silk merchant was not arrested. Nothing tied him to the scribes. But he did not come to the council that afternoon, and his seat on the guild bench stood empty, and people noticed.

The council met in the Magnaura under the sailcloth roof. The Eparch laid the forged letters on the floor in rows, dozens of them, with the originals beside them, and let the councillors walk up and down between the rows and look. It took two hours. Nobody shouted. By the end, most of the men on the benches looked sick, and a few of them looked at each other with the faces of men who have understood too late that they were cheated.

Then the Domestikos rose, and told them who had broken the forgers.

He told it well. He told of the Hornbearer who had jumped from the scaffold at Charisios to save a mason's boy, and who had taken a runaway scribe in off the street at midnight with Concord knives behind him, and who had gone down into the Crypts of the Emperors and the Drowned Cisterns with a handful of companions and come back up with the Tower's forgers in a rope. He told it with his hand on Stephanos's shoulder, and the councillors looked at Stephanos, and Stephanos stood still under the weight of the hand and the eyes and tried not to look at anyone.

"This is what the Tagma can do," Nikephoros said at the end, "with a handful. Think what it could do with a banner. Think what it could do with a banner and a crown to ride behind."

He did not say Bardas's name. He did not need to. When the council broke up that evening, the talk on the steps of the Magnaura was all about the Feast of the Cross, and whether the Patriarch would bend, and what colour the new emperor's cloak should be.

Stephanos said nothing about the letter in his breastplate. He had not been asked.

---

He took it to the Abba that night.

The old man was sitting where he always sat, on the stool by the window, facing the dark. The lamp was not lit; he had no need of it. Stephanos lit it anyway, from habit, and knelt on the floor beside the stool, and put the letter in the Abba's hands.

Pelagios did not unfold it at once. He held it, turning it over, running his thin fingers along the edges and over the broken seal. He found the device with his thumb, the crook and the sceptre and the horn, and his face changed.

Then he unfolded it and lifted it to his face, and breathed in, slowly, with his eyes shut, as if he could smell the ink.

"His hand," the Abba said. "Twenty years dead, and his hand."

"You can't see it, Abba."

"I don't need to see it. I held his hand the night he died. It smelled of sheep and woodsmoke and the ointment the monks used on his thigh. This smells of sheep and woodsmoke." The old man lowered the letter to his lap. His clouded eyes were wet. "What does it say?"

Stephanos read it to him. He read it slowly, all of it, down to the picture of the sheep and the sling at the bottom. When he had finished, the Abba sat for a long while without speaking, and the tears ran down his face into his white beard, and he did not wipe them away.

"Arkadiopolis," he said at last. "The ford below the ruined church. That's where his hall was. That's where I asked him." He was quiet again. "Someone of his house. He had a daughter. I never met her. She was married, by then, and gone to another village. The monks said she had children."

"Abba, I want to go."

The old man turned his face toward him.

"The first frost," Stephanos said. "Kale says it's close. A week, perhaps less. Kallinikos sent copies of the letter east two nights ago. The Tower will send someone. If they get there first, they'll find whoever is waiting, and they'll kill them. Or take them." He heard his voice rising and pressed it down. "I found the letter. It came to me. The Amma said it had my name on it. Let me go."

The Abba held out the letter. Stephanos took it.

Then the old man folded his hands in his lap and turned back to the window, and said, very softly, the way he said it every night:

"Whom will the Horn anoint?"

Stephanos waited. The Abba did not say anything else.

"Abba. Please. Tell me to go."

"I can't tell you that."

"Then tell me not to."

"I can't tell you that either." The old man's voice was gentle. "I'm not the Domestikos, my son. I don't give orders in the field. I gave that up thirty years ago, when I made him Domestikos, because I knew I would be bad at it. Go and ask him."

"He'll say no."

"Perhaps." The Abba's face was turned to the dark. "And then you will have to decide what the vow of obedience means. That is a hard question. It's not the one I ask every night, but it's close."

---

The Domestikos said no.

He said it in the refectory of the Fool's Tower the next morning, after the office of Prime, in front of the whole chapter. He sat at the head of the long table with his big scarred hands folded around a cup of watered wine, and he listened to Stephanos all the way through without interrupting, and he read the letter twice, and then he handed it back and said no.

"Brother Gregoras will go to Thrace," he said. "With two novices. They'll leave this afternoon. They know the Thracian roads better than you do, and Gregoras has been to Arkadiopolis. If there's an heir of the lame lord's house at that ford, Gregoras will find them and bring them to the City under guard, and the Grammatikoi will examine their claim, and the council will weigh it. That's the proper way to do this."

"Domestikos..."

"You," said Nikephoros, "will ride east with Lord Bardas on the Feast of Saint Demetrios. Anak's raiders have crossed the Sangarios again. They burned two villages last week and carried off every child under twelve. Bardas means to meet them at the fords with four hundred men, and I've promised him twenty Hornbearers. You'll carry the banner."

The refectory was very quiet. Twenty knights sat along the benches with their spoons in their hands and did not look at Stephanos, except for Gregoras, at the far end of the table, who looked at him steadily and shook his head a very small amount.

"The banner," Stephanos said.

"The banner of the Tagma of the Horn. The purple with the horn and the stone." Nikephoros's voice softened a little. "I know what it means to you, Brother. I know what your count did with the last banner you carried. I'm giving you this one because I trust you to bring it home."

It struck Stephanos like a fist under the ribs. He had not expected it. He had expected to be ordered to stay on the wall, or sent to guard the Harbour gate, or told to lay stones at Charisios until his knee healed. He had not expected to be handed the banner.

For nine years he had not let himself think about Count Artavasdos and the day in the courtyard at Kaisareia when the count had stood in front of his cataphracts and told them that the Goldhand had bought their service, and their horses, and their banner, and that each man who wanted to stay would be paid in island gold. The banner had been an old one, the red eagle of Taron, carried against the Persians and the Arabs and the giants for two hundred years. Stephanos had carried it for six. He had watched a Goldhand factor roll it up and put it in a chest and nail the lid shut. Then he had taken off his count's colours and laid them on the ground and walked west, alone, with a horse and a sword and nothing else, for two months, until he came to the City.

"The raiders took the children," Nikephoros said. "You know what Anak does with children."

"I know."

"Then you know where a Hornbearer ought to be. The heir can wait a week for Gregoras. The children of the Sangarios can't."

Stephanos looked at the letter in his hand.

He saw himself riding east under the purple, beside Bardas on his tall horse, with four hundred men behind them and the banner snapping in the wind. He saw the fords of the Sangarios and the raiders on the far bank, and the charge, and the children afterward running across the water to their mothers. He saw himself bringing the banner home to the City with the dust of the east on it, and the people on the walls cheering. He saw the Feast of the Cross, and Bardas kneeling in the Great Church, and the Patriarch's hands lifting the oil.

It would be good. It would be the best thing he had ever done.

And far off in Thrace, at a ford below a ruined church, someone of the lame lord's house would be waiting in the frost for a knight who did not come.

"Yes, Domestikos," he said.

---

Kale found him that night on the wall above the Tower, sitting in the embrasure with his back to the stone and his bad leg stretched out in front of him. He had not gone to the evening office. He had not eaten.

She sat down on the parapet beside him without asking and looked out west, toward Thrace.

"Gregoras left at noon," she said. "With two boys who've never been out of the City."

"I know."

"He's sixty-one. He has three fingers on his left hand. He's a good man, and he'll do his best." She paused. "The frost is coming in three days. Not a week. Three days."

Stephanos turned his head.

"I saw it," Kale said. "This afternoon, in the market, buying bread. I saw it the way I saw the scribe in the crypt. A ford, black water. A ruined church on the far bank with two walls standing. Willows. White on the grass. A fire, in the apse, and a girl beside it with a crook, and sheep asleep all around her." She looked at him. "And six shapes coming out of the willows on the other side of the river. Big shapes. With axes."

He did not ask whether she was sure. He had seen the arrow go into the dark in the crypt.

"Gregoras can't make it in three days," he said. "Not with two novices on post horses. Four at the least."

"No."

"I could. On Dove. Riding through."

"You could," she agreed. "If you went tonight."

The wind came in off the Marmara, cold and wet. Below them, in the City, the lamps were going out one by one. Somewhere in the Tower, a brother was singing the last psalm of Compline, alone, in a cracked old voice.

"I gave my word," Stephanos said. "This morning. In front of the chapter. I said, yes, Domestikos."

"You did."

"The vow of obedience. It's the first vow. The Domestikos commands in the field. That's the rule. Pelagios made it. If every Hornbearer rides wherever he likes whenever he thinks God is telling him to, there's no order. There's just a crowd of men in armour."

"That's true."

"And the children on the Sangarios. Anak takes children, Kale. You know what he does with them. You've been in the Heights. You've seen it."

"I've seen it," she said quietly.

"So tell me what to do."

"I'm not a Hornbearer. I don't have to keep your vows." Kale stood up. "I'm going to Thrace tonight, whatever you do. I don't know why. I saw the girl by the fire, and I can't stop seeing her, and I've learned that when I can't stop seeing a thing, I'd better go and look at it. The Amma's coming. She says she has to, and I believe her. Lazaros is coming because he says the Patriarch told him to go where the Amma goes. Sergios is coming because he's already at the Golden Gate, sitting on the road, waiting for us." She shrugged. "And Theophanes is coming, because Kallinikos has men looking for him in every tavern in the City and he says he'd rather be killed by giants than by his old schoolmates."

"You're going without me."

"We're going. You can come or not." She turned to go, and then turned back. "Stephanos. I'm not telling you it's right. I don't know if it's right. I only know I saw a girl by a fire, and six of them in the trees."

She went down the stair.

He sat in the embrasure a long time after she had gone. The last lamps went out in the City. The brother in the chapel stopped singing. The stars came out over Thrace, very bright and cold.

Then he got up, and went down the stair on his bad knee, to the top room of the Tower.

---

The Abba's door was open. The Abba was asleep.

He lay on his narrow bed on his back with his hands folded on his chest, and his breathing was slow and light as a child's. Stephanos stood in the doorway and looked at him. He had meant to wake him. He had meant to kneel beside the bed and ask him, one last time, to tell him what to do.

He did not wake him.

Instead he knelt in the doorway, and looked at the old man and the dark window and the smoke-blackened icon on the wall, where the Mother of God held her child and looked out at the world with her patient eyes. And he asked the question, the Abba's question, silently, without moving his lips.

*Whom will the Horn anoint?*

Nobody answered. Nobody ever did.

But he found, kneeling there, that he could not see himself riding east under the purple anymore. He tried, and the picture would not come. All he could see was a ford in the frost, and a ruined church, and a fire.

He got up. He went down to the stable.

---

Gregoras was there, in the dark, sitting on an upturned bucket by Dove's stall with a lantern at his feet.

Stephanos stopped in the doorway. The old knight looked up at him. He had not gone to Thrace at noon. He was in his shirt, without armour, and his sword was nowhere in sight.

"I sent the boys on without me," Gregoras said. "Told them I'd catch them up. They'll wait at Selymbria. They're good lads, but they'd have been no use at the ford. Neither would I. My knees are worse than yours."

"You knew I'd come."

"I've known you nine years, Brother. You've been angry the whole time. But you've never once been a coward." Gregoras stood up, slowly, with a hand on the stall. "The Domestikos will call you oathbreaker. He'll have the right. He might strip you of the purple for it. He'd have the right to do that too."

"I know."

"Do you? Good." The old knight picked up the lantern. "I've saddled her. There's bread and cheese in the left bag and oats in the right. Your lamellar's on the rack. I cleaned it." He held out the lantern. "Go on. The Golden Gate opens at the third hour for the milk carts. They'll be waiting for you on the road."

Stephanos took the lantern. His throat was tight.

"Brother Gregoras. Why?"

Gregoras was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "Thirty years ago I rode out of this City with the Abba to find the lame lord's hall. Everyone said it was madness. The Domestikos of those days said so too. He ordered us to stay." He smiled in the lantern light, and his face was brown and creased as a walnut. "The Abba asked the question anyway. I've never been sorry."

He went out of the stable, and his footsteps went away across the yard, uneven and slow.

Stephanos armed himself in the dark. Lamellar over mail, the old weight settling on his shoulders like a hand. The belt of Truth. The breastplate of Justice, with the letter inside it. Helm, shield, lance. The sling on his belt, which the rule required, and which he had practised with every morning for nine years and had never once used in a fight.

He led Dove out of the stable and through the postern of the Tower and down the lane along the inside of the wall, toward the Golden Gate. The grey mare's hooves were loud on the stones. Nobody stopped him. Up on the wall, the watch looked down, and saw the purple on his shield, and looked away.

At the gate, the milk carts were waiting to be let in, and among them, in the grey before dawn, stood four figures on horseback and one on foot.

Sergios was the one on foot. He was sitting in the middle of the road, eating a turnip.

"You're late," he said.

Stephanos swung up into the saddle. His knee screamed at him. He ignored it.

"Let's go," he said.

The gate opened. They rode out into Thrace.
