## Chapter 16: The Noonday Guest

*The demon of acedia, which is also called the noonday demon, is the one that causes the most serious trouble of all. He makes it seem that the sun barely moves, if at all, and that the day is fifty hours long.*

*Evagrius of Pontus, On the Eight Thoughts*

---

The Wilderness began where the salt figures stood along the road.

They had ridden south for three weeks, down the coast of Syria through Antioch and Laodikeia and Tripolis, then inland through the hills, then south again through a country that grew drier and browner and emptier every day. The villages shrank to hamlets and the hamlets to a few mud houses round a well, and then there were no villages at all, only the road, white and dusty, running south between hills of bare rock the colour of bone. On the twentieth day they passed the last well. On the twenty-first, they saw the first salt pilgrim.

It stood at the side of the road, facing back the way they had come. It was the figure of a man, life-sized, in a pilgrim's hooded cloak, with a staff in one hand and a gourd hanging from his belt. It was made of salt. Every fold of the cloak, every strand of the beard, every finger on the hand that held the staff: all of it was salt, white and crystalline, glittering faintly in the hard sun. The face under the hood was turned back toward the north. Its mouth was open. Its eyes were wide.

"He looked back," said Amma Thekla.

She did not stop her mule. She rode past it without turning her head.

There were more after that. One every mile or so, at first, and then more often: men and women and once, terribly, a child, standing at the edges of the road in their pilgrim cloaks, all turned north, all with that same look of longing and horror frozen on their white faces. Some had stood so long that the wind had worn them down to shapeless lumps. Some were so fresh that the salt still sparkled.

"Who were they?" asked Lazaros.

"Pilgrims," said Thekla. "Going to the holy mountain. The road to the mountain is long, and the desert is hard, and at noon the Wilderness whispers to them. It tells them they're fools. It tells them the mountain's a lie, and the saints are dead, and they'll die out here of thirst for nothing. It tells them to go home. And some of them stop, and turn round, and look back at the way they came." She rode on. "And then they stand there."

"Forever?"

"Until someone blesses water and pours it on them. Then the salt melts, and they can rest." The old woman shrugged. "Nobody does. Nobody has holy water to spare out here. Water's life. You don't pour it on a statue."

Kale had stopped her horse beside the salt child. It was a little girl of six or seven, in a cloak too big for her, with her hood fallen back and her hair in two braids down her back. Her salt face was turned north. Her small salt hand was reaching up, as if for someone's hand to hold.

"Amma," said Kale. "Do you have any?"

Thekla stopped her mule. She looked back at Kale for a long moment. Then she took from her belt the small clay flask she had carried all the way from the City, stoppered with wax. It was half full. She had used the other half on the Nephilim seer in the Cisterns, a long time ago.

"The Jordan," she said. "Or so the Patriarch says."

She got down from her mule, slowly, stiffly, and walked back to the salt child, and knelt, and unstoppered the flask. She said something under her breath. Then she poured three drops on the child's salt head.

The salt ran. It ran like wax running from a candle, like snow running from a roof in spring. It ran down the child's face and her braids and her cloak, and pooled at her feet, and soaked into the sand. In a few breaths there was nothing left but a small wet patch on the white road and a pilgrim's token lying in the middle of it, a little lead badge in the shape of a mountain.

Thekla picked it up. She held it out to Kale.

"It shows the way to the mountain," she said. "They all carry one." She got back on her mule. "Keep it."

---

The laura of Saint Chariton lay in a gorge in the hills, five days south of the last well.

It was not a monastery the way the Fool's Tower was a monastery, or Saint George of the Rock. There were no walls. There was no gate. There was a gorge, deep and narrow, with a thin stream running down the bottom of it, green with reeds, and on both sides of the gorge, in the cliffs, there were caves. Dozens of them. Some were natural. Some had been cut or widened by hand. Some had little walls of stone built across their mouths, with a wooden door, and some had a ladder of rope or wood leading up to them from the gorge floor. And in the middle of the gorge, where the stream widened into a pool, there was a small stone church, with a bell hanging in a frame outside it, and a bakehouse, and a well.

The monks lived alone in the caves, one to a cell, and came down to the church on Saturday and Sunday to pray together and share a meal. The rest of the week they prayed alone, and worked with their hands, and fought with the thoughts that came to them in the heat of noon.

That was how it had been for three hundred years.

When the band came down the path into the gorge at sunset, the laura was silent. No smoke rose from the caves. No monk came out to greet them. The rope ladders hung slack. The church door was shut.

"How many?" said Stephanos.

"When I left, forty," said Thekla. She had stopped at the head of the path, looking down into the gorge, and her face had gone very still. "When Brother Iakovos wrote to me in the autumn, eleven."

They went down. They found three.

Two were in the church. One was very old, older than Thekla, a bent brown shrivelled man with a beard to his waist, sitting against the wall by the icon screen with his eyes open, staring at nothing. The other was young, perhaps twenty, lying on his back on the floor of the nave with his arms spread wide like a man crucified, staring at the roof. Neither of them moved when the door opened. Neither of them looked round.

The third was in the bakehouse. He was sitting on the floor by the cold oven with a half-kneaded lump of dough in his lap, grey and dry and cracked, as if it had been sitting there for days. He was about forty. His hands lay on the dough, still, and he was looking at them.

"Iakovos," said Thekla, from the doorway.

He looked up. It took him a long time. His eyes were dull, and it seemed to take him a long time to understand what he was seeing. Then something moved in his face, far down, like a fish moving at the bottom of a murky pool.

"Amma," he said. "Amma Thekla. You came back."

"I came back."

"It's too late." He looked down at the dough again. "It's too late, Amma. They've all gone. They went one by one. Brother Zosimas went first, in the winter. Then the brothers from the upper cells. Then the old ones. They walked out of the gorge at noon and they didn't come back." He was quiet. "I found Brother Paul on the road north, three miles out. He'd turned to salt."

"And these?" said Thekla. "The two in the church?"

"Abba Kyriakos and Brother Neilos. They stopped moving at midsummer. They don't eat. They don't sleep. They don't pray. They just sit there." Iakovos turned the dry lump of dough over in his hands. "I've been trying to make bread. For the Liturgy. For Sunday. I've been trying for a week." He looked at it. "I can't remember how."

---

It came the next day, at noon.

Stephanos was sitting in the shade of the church porch with his back against the wall and his splinted arm in his lap. He had meant to go up and look at the caves. He had meant to help Kale and Lazaros carry water up to the old Abba Kyriakos in the church, and try to make him drink. He had meant to talk to Dora. He had not spoken to her properly since Tarsus. She rode with Kale now, and ate with Lazaros, and slept near the Amma, and when he spoke to her she answered him politely, like a stranger, and looked away.

He had meant to do all of those things. He was sitting on the porch.

The sun was very high. It stood straight overhead, white and small and blinding, and the shadows of the cliffs had shrunk down to nothing, and the gorge lay bare under it. The stream had stopped making any sound. The reeds did not move. There was no wind.

He looked at the shadow of the bell frame on the ground in front of him. It was a short black stub, pointing north. He looked at it for a long time.

It did not move.

He watched it. He watched it for what felt like an hour. He knew it should move. The sun moved; shadows moved. He had spent his whole life in the open air and he knew exactly how fast a shadow crept across the ground at noon, a finger's width in a quarter hour. He watched the edge of the shadow against a pebble on the ground. He watched it and watched it.

It did not move at all.

And he thought: *What is the point?*

It came into his mind quietly, the way thoughts do. It did not feel like a voice. It felt like his own thought, reasonable and tired and true.

*What is the point of any of it? Petros is gone. He's in the City by now, at the Emperor's side, with a stranger who has no shadow telling him he's chosen. You let him go. You couldn't stop him. You didn't try. The Domestikos has named you oathbreaker in chapter, and he was right. You broke your oath to ride to a ford and find a boy who wasn't there, and a girl who won't speak to you. You've been gone half a year. You've found three words that mean nothing. And the Hunter is coming, and the City will fall, the way it fell before, and nothing you do will change that. Nothing anyone does. The Horn went down a hundred years ago and it's never coming back up. The Abba's been asking the Question for thirty years, and nobody has ever answered. Nobody ever will. Why would they?*

He found that he was lying down.

He did not remember doing it. He was lying on his side on the stone of the church porch, with his cheek against the warm stone and his splinted arm under him, and it hurt, and he did not care enough to move it.

*You should go home,* the thought said. *Go back to the City. Kneel before the Domestikos and take your punishment. Lay stones. Hold a spear. Ride behind Bardas when the Hunter comes, and die at the walls, and be done. It's what you were for. You were a lance. You were never anything but a lance. All this, the Horn and the heir and the Question, it was a dream, and it's time to wake up.*

The shadow of the bell frame lay on the ground in front of his eyes. It did not move.

*Or don't go home,* said the thought. *Why bother? It's a long road. It's hot. Just lie here. Just lie here a while. It doesn't matter. Nothing does.*

He lay there.

He did not know how long. The sun did not move, so there was no way to know. He heard, far off, as if through water, someone singing a psalm, and then stopping. He heard someone else crying. He heard a door open and close. He did not lift his head.

Then something dropped into his lap.

It was a handful of palm leaves. Long, dry, green-brown strips of them, split and flattened, the kind that Amma Thekla wove her baskets from. They fell across his splinted arm and his knees.

He looked at them for a long time. Then he looked up.

Amma Thekla was standing over him. She was holding a basket, half-woven, in her left hand, and her right hand was working it, over and under, over and under, without stopping. Her face was grey and sweating and old, older than he had ever seen it, and her eyes were half-closed. Her lips were moving.

"*Lord Jesus Christ,*" she was saying, over and over, on the breath. "*Son of God. Have mercy on me.*"

"Amma," he said. His voice came out a croak. "I can't."

"*Lord Jesus Christ.*" Over, under. "Sit up."

"I can't. I can't, Amma. What's the point? It doesn't..."

"*Son of God.*" Over, under. "I know what it's saying to you. It's been saying it to me for forty-two years. Every day. At noon." She did not stop weaving. "Sit up, Stephanos of Taron, and pick up the palm, and weave."

"I can't weave. My arm's broken."

"You have one good hand and one bad one. That's one more than you need." Over, under. "*Have mercy on me.* Sit up."

He sat up. It took everything he had. He leaned back against the church wall, dizzy, sick, with the sun beating down on him and the shadow of the bell frame lying on the ground, not moving.

"Pick up two strips," said Thekla. "Lay one over the other, crossways. Like a cross. Good. Now take a third and go under the first and over the second. *Lord Jesus Christ.* Say it with me."

"I don't..."

"Say it."

He said it. His voice was a whisper. "*Lord Jesus Christ.*"

"Over. Under. *Son of God.*"

"*Son of God.*"

"Over. Under. *Have mercy on me.* Now again. And again. Don't think about it. Don't think about anything. Just the palm, and the words. That's all. Just this strip, and this breath." She sat down beside him on the porch, heavily, and went on with her own basket. "The noonday demon can't bear hands that work while the mouth prays. It doesn't know why. Neither do I. The fathers found it out a thousand years ago and nobody's found anything better since. *Lord Jesus Christ.*"

"*Son of God,*" said Stephanos.

He did it badly. His left hand was clumsy and his right was splinted and could only hold the strips in place with its fingertips. The palm cut his fingers. The weave came out crooked and loose. He had to undo it twice. But he did it. Over, under. *Lord Jesus Christ.* Over, under. *Son of God.* Over, under. *Have mercy on me.*

He did not know how long. The sun did not move.

But after a while he found that the thought was not there anymore. The reasonable, tired, true voice that had been telling him there was no point. It had not gone. It was still there, somewhere, at the edge of his mind, like a man standing in a doorway. But it could not get in. His mind was full. It was full of palm leaves and over and under and the words.

He looked up.

The shadow of the bell frame had moved. It had crept a finger's width to the east, off the edge of the pebble.

---

They worked all afternoon.

Kale was already weaving when Stephanos went to find her, sitting in the shade of the bakehouse with a basket in her lap and her jaw set, muttering the words through her teeth as if they were an insult. Lazaros was weaving beside her, slowly, with his scarred hands; he was better at it than any of them. Theophanes had not come out of the cave where he had slept. When Stephanos climbed up to him, he found the scribe lying face down on the stone floor with the purple book open under his head like a pillow, not moving, and it took Stephanos and Kale together to sit him up and put the palm in his hands.

"It doesn't matter," Theophanes kept saying. "None of it. The Words don't mean anything. They're riddles a mad old stylite made up to feel important. The Horn's gone. The Master was right. He was right about everything. None of it matters."

"Over," said Kale grimly. "Under. Say it."

"I can't."

"Say it or I'll tie you to a rope ladder and drop you off the cliff."

He said it. He wept while he said it, and his hands shook so badly he could hardly hold the palm, but he said it.

Sergios did not need to be told. When Stephanos came down from the cave, the fool was sitting on the floor of the church by the old Abba Kyriakos, braiding reeds from the stream into a long green rope, and singing to the old man under his breath. The old Abba's eyes had moved. They were watching the fool's hands.

Dora was in the bakehouse.

She was standing at the kneading trough with her sleeves rolled up to the shoulder and her arms white with flour to the elbow, and she was kneading dough. Brother Iakovos was sitting on the floor by the oven, watching her. She was talking to him while she worked. Stephanos stopped in the doorway to listen.

"Grandmother did it like this," she was saying. "You push it away with the heels of your hands, see, and then you fold it back over, and turn it, and push again. Push, fold, turn. Push, fold, turn. You don't have to think about it. Your hands know. And you say the prayer while you do it. Push, *Lord Jesus Christ*. Fold, *Son of God*. Turn, *have mercy on me*." She glanced at him. "You try."

Iakovos looked at the dough. He looked at his hands. Slowly, very slowly, he got up off the floor and came to the trough, and put his hands in the dough beside hers.

"Push," said Dora.

He pushed.

"*Lord Jesus Christ,*" said Dora.

"*Lord Jesus Christ,*" said Iakovos. His voice cracked in the middle.

"Fold. *Son of God.*"

Stephanos went away without letting her see him. He sat down on the church porch again with his crooked basket, and went on weaving, over, under, until the sun went down behind the western cliff of the gorge, and the shadows came out long and blue across the stream, and the reeds began to stir in a little evening wind.

That night there was bread in the laura of Saint Chariton for the first time in a month. The loaves came out of Iakovos's oven lopsided and heavy and slightly burnt on the bottom, and the old Abba Kyriakos ate half of one sitting against the wall of the church, slowly, with the fool holding it for him.

---

"It was him," said Amma Thekla.

It was late. They were sitting outside the church by a small fire of dried reeds, the whole band and Brother Iakovos, with the stars very bright and close overhead. Stephanos's hands were raw and cut from the palm. So were everyone's, except the Amma's and Lazaros's.

"Who?" said Kale.

"The noonday demon. The thing that comes at noon. It's not a demon. Not an ordinary one. It's a person." Thekla was not weaving now. Her hands lay still in her lap, and she was looking into the fire. "It's Abba Makarios."

Iakovos made a small sound.

"He was the elder of this laura when I came here," said Thekla. "Forty-two years ago. I was twenty. He was my abba. My father in God. He taught me everything I know. He taught me the Jesus Prayer, and the palm weaving, and how to fight the thoughts at noon." She was quiet a moment. "He was the holiest man I ever knew. People came from Egypt and Syria and Constantinople to ask him for a word. He could see into people's hearts the way you see into water."

"What happened to him?" said Dora softly.

"He fought the noonday demon every day for sixty years. And he won, every day. And one day he didn't." Thekla shook her head. "I don't know why. Nobody knows why. He was eighty-one. He had outlived everyone he loved. The Blight was coming into the Wilderness, and the pilgrims were turning to salt on the road, and the raiders had burned two of the other lauras, and the monks were leaving. And one noon, twenty years ago, he walked out of his cell and down the gorge and out into the desert, south, toward the dry well." She paused. "I followed him. I was fifty by then. I followed him all the way. I begged him to come back. He didn't answer me. He walked to the well, and stood on the lip of it, and looked down. And then he turned round and looked at me." Her voice had gone very quiet. "And he said: *There is no point, Thekla. There never was.* And he stepped off."

Nobody said anything. The reed fire crackled.

"The Well of Azazel," said Iakovos, almost in a whisper. "It's a mile south of here. It's dry. It's been dry for three hundred years. There was a city down there once, they say. Under the sand. A city of the old giants. God sent fire on it, like Sodom, and it turned to salt." He hugged his knees. "Things come up out of it at noon."

"He comes up out of it," said Thekla. "Makarios. What's left of him. Azazel took him. Azazel takes everyone who gives up in the desert, the way the Dragon takes the ones who drown in debt. Makarios gave up. He gave Azazel his hope, the last thing he had. And Azazel filled the empty place. He's the noonday king now. He's the thing that comes to the cells and stops the sun and tells the monks there's no point." Her hands tightened in her lap. "He's been doing it for twenty years. And he's good at it, because he was the best there ever was at fighting it. He knows every trick. He taught me all of them."

"And the fourth word?" said Theophanes.

It was the first thing he had said all evening. They all turned to look at him. He was sitting a little apart, with the purple book on his knees, and his face in the firelight was grey and hollow-eyed. His hands were cut from the palm, like everyone's.

"The fourth word of Symeon," he said. "It's down there. Isn't it. In the well. With him."

"Yes," said Thekla. "I think so. There was a hermitage at the bottom of the well once, before it went dry. A cell cut in the rock beside the water. There'd have been a lamp."

Theophanes looked down at the purple book. He did not say anything else.

---

Stephanos found Dora later, at the pool in the middle of the gorge, washing the flour off her arms in the starlight.

He stood at the edge of the water. She knew he was there. She did not look round.

"The bread was good," he said.

"It was burnt."

"It was good." He was quiet a moment. "You got Iakovos kneading."

"He got himself kneading. I only showed him how." She rinsed her arms and shook the water off them. "The Amma got you weaving."

"Yes."

She stood up and turned around. In the starlight, with the veil of the Myrrh-bearer pushed back off her face, she looked very young. She was thin. They all were. Her arms were scratched from the palm and from the thorns on the road, and her feet were bare and cut, and there were shadows under her eyes.

"I lay down on the church porch today," he said. "At noon. I lay down and I didn't want to get up. I thought, there's no point. Petros is gone. I let him go. I didn't believe you. And there's no point." He looked at the water. "It was true. That was the worst of it. It wasn't lying to me. Everything it said was true."

Dora said nothing.

"The Amma put palm leaves in my lap," said Stephanos. "And I picked them up. I didn't want to. I did it anyway. And after a while, it was still true. But it didn't matter as much." He turned his cut hands over and looked at them. "I don't know what I'm trying to say."

"You're trying to say sorry," said Dora.

He looked at her.

"You're not very good at it," she said. "It's all right. Petros isn't either." She came up out of the water onto the bank and stood in front of him. She had to tilt her head back to look him in the face. "I'm still angry at you."

"I know."

"I'll probably be angry at you for a long time."

"I know."

She studied him a moment longer. Then she reached out and took his splinted right hand in both of hers, the way she had on the ship when she moved his fingers on the sling. She turned it over and looked at the cuts from the palm on his fingers.

"You held the strips wrong," she said. "You should hold them with the flat of your thumb, not the tip. Then they don't cut you." She let go. "I'll show you tomorrow."

She went away up the path toward the Amma's cave. Stephanos stood by the pool for a while longer, alone, under the stars. Then he sat down on a rock by the water, and took the half-finished crooked basket out of his belt, and went on weaving in the dark by feel, over and under, saying the words under his breath, until he fell asleep sitting up.

He did not say the Question. But he did not forget it either. It was there, at the edge of his mind, waiting, like the thing in the doorway. Only it did not feel like a threat anymore. It felt like a friend who had come a long way, and was prepared to wait a little longer.
