# Part One: The Wall

## Chapter 1: The Trowel and the Spear

*Those who built the wall worked with one hand and held a weapon in the other.*

*Nehemiah*

---

Stephanos had been laying stone since before the light, and his hands had gone past aching into the steady numbness that meant he could work another four hours if he did not think about them.

The breach below the Gate of Charisios was the worst on the Land Walls. Nebrodes had broken it a hundred years ago with a ram the size of a ship, and the remnant had filled it thirty years ago in a hurry, with rubble and brick and whatever they could carry from the ruins of the Blachernae palace. The fill had held for a generation. Last winter the rains had got into it, and in the spring a whole face of it had slid into the ditch in the night with a sound like thunder. Now the masons were rebuilding it properly, course by course, in dressed limestone brought down from the quarries by the Golden Gate, and every able body in the City who was not on watch or in the fields took a turn.

Hornbearers took two turns. The rule said so.

He set a block, tapped it level with the handle of his trowel, and scraped away the mortar that squeezed out of the joint. Beside him on the scaffold, a mason's boy named Niketas slapped fresh mortar onto the next course with more enthusiasm than skill. The boy was nine and had lost his front teeth, and he talked the way the wind blew, without pause or purpose.

"My father says the giants won't come today because it's a feast," Niketas said. "Is it a feast? He says it's the feast of Saint somebody. He says giants don't come on feasts because they don't like the bells. Is that true? Do giants hate bells?"

"Some do," said Stephanos.

"Which ones?"

"The ones who have been to church."

The boy thought about this, frowning, and then laughed so hard he dropped his trowel. It clattered down three levels of scaffold and landed in the ditch. A mason below them swore and threw it back up, and Niketas caught it against his chest and grinned.

Stephanos's spear leaned against the parapet at his left hand, where he could reach it without looking. His shield hung from a peg driven into the old wall. His lamellar lay folded in a basket at the foot of the ladder, because no man could lay stone for four hours in thirty pounds of iron and horn, but he wore the belt and the breastplate. He always wore them. The belt was plain leather, faced with bronze plates, and the word Truth was cut into the buckle in the old square letters. The breastplate carried the word Justice over his heart. The Abba had blessed both nine years ago, the week Stephanos took his vows, and for nine years he had not taken either off except to sleep and bathe.

Down the length of the wall, as far as the eye could follow it in the grey light, the remnant was building. Masons and farmers and deacons and girls from the silk shops stood on ladders and scaffolds, passing stone from hand to hand. Every one of them had a weapon within reach: a spear, a pruning hook, a sling, a bread knife tied to a pole. The watch walked the top of the wall in pairs. On the towers, the horn-blowers sat with their horns across their knees.

It had been this way for thirty years. Stephanos had not been here for the first twenty-one of them. He had been in the east, riding under another banner, and he did not like to think about that now, so he laid another stone.

"They're on the hill again," said Niketas.

Stephanos looked up.

West of the walls, beyond the ditch and the ruined suburbs, the ground rose in a long bare slope toward the villages of the Thracian plain. On the crest of the slope, where a burned chapel stood with its roof gone, a line of horsemen sat watching the City. There were perhaps thirty of them. Their banners hung limp in the still air, but Stephanos did not need to see the banners. He knew the men by the way they sat their horses: the lords of the hill villages and their retainers, men who had fed on the empty lands around the City for a hundred years and did not want the City strong again.

At their head, on a tall bay, sat Lord Tobias of Selymbria in a red cloak.

"Good morning, builders!" Tobias called. His voice was thin at this distance, but the morning was very still, and it carried. "What a fine wall you're making! Look at it! If a fox climbed up on it, he'd knock it down!"

His men laughed. A few of the masons on the wall laughed too, uneasily. Most kept working and did not look up.

"The feeble remnant!" Tobias called. "Will they finish it in a day? Will they bring the stones back to life out of the heaps of rubbish? Will they make sacrifices? Has anyone told the Patriarch he has no roof?"

This time the laughter from the hill was louder. One of Tobias's men stood in his stirrups and made a gesture Niketas was too young to understand. The boy looked at Stephanos to see whether he should be offended.

Stephanos found that his jaw ached. He had been clenching it. He put down the trowel and picked up the spear, and the familiar weight of the ash shaft in his palm made his heart slow.

"Brother," said a voice behind him. "Don't."

He turned his head. Brother Gregoras, the oldest Hornbearer at the Fool's Tower, stood on the next scaffold with a hod of mortar on his shoulder. His beard was white and his face was brown as a walnut, and he had lost two fingers of his left hand at the siege of Nikaia before Stephanos was born.

"I'm not going to do anything," Stephanos said.

"You're holding a spear and looking at a man a quarter mile away. The last time you did that, you rode out alone and chased Tobias's nephew into a bog."

"He was stealing sheep."

"He was. And you lost your horse in the bog and walked home in the dark with a broken toe, and the Domestikos had to pay Tobias forty nomismata to keep him from calling it a raid." Gregoras shifted the hod to his other shoulder. "Put the spear down. Pick up the trowel. The Lord will deal with Tobias."

"When?"

"Ask him. He doesn't tell me."

Stephanos laid the spear back against the parapet. He picked up the trowel. His hands were not numb now; they were hot, and the blood beat in his fingertips.

"The Tagma of the Horn!" Tobias shouted from the hill. "Is that one of the Tagma I see? With the purple on his shield? Tell me, brother knight, how long have you been looking for your little horn? Thirty years? Have you tried under the bed?"

The men on the hill roared. Niketas laughed too, and then stopped, because Stephanos had not.

"Why don't you go and hit him?" the boy asked in a low voice.

"Because Brother Gregoras told me not to."

"Are you afraid of Brother Gregoras?"

"Yes," said Stephanos, and Gregoras snorted into his beard.

---

The horn on the tower of Charisios sounded an hour later, three short blasts and a long one.

Stephanos had his spear in his hand before the long note ended. Along the wall, people dropped their tools and ran for the ladders or snatched up their weapons and crouched behind the parapet. Someone was screaming. The masons in the ditch below the breach threw down their stones and scrambled for the scaffolds.

He looked west. The riders on the hill were gone. In their place, coming down the slope through the ruined suburbs at a lumbering run, were two giants.

Gibborim. Stephanos had fought them before, in the east, and he knew the look of them at once: men grown wrong, twice the height of a tall soldier, heavy in the shoulders and long in the arm. They wore mail made of rings as big as bracelets and carried axes on poles like the masts of fishing boats. One had painted his face with white clay. The other wore a string of skulls around his neck, little ones, and Stephanos did not let himself think about whose they were.

They were not coming for the gate. They were coming for the breach, where the new stone stood only half as high as the old wall and the scaffolds leaned against it like a ladder made for them.

"Get off the scaffold," Stephanos told Niketas. "Down the ladder. Now."

"But..."

"Now!"

The boy went. Stephanos watched him to the ladder and then turned back to the giants. They were in the ditch. The one with the painted face planted his feet and swung his axe at the lowest level of scaffold, and the timber burst apart like kindling. Planks and mortar tubs and a stack of dressed stone came down together in a roar of dust.

The whole scaffold lurched under Stephanos. He grabbed the parapet. Below him, the ladder swung loose from the wall, and Niketas, halfway down it, shrieked and clung to the rungs as it tipped outward over the ditch.

The painted giant looked up and saw the boy.

Stephanos did not decide to jump. He found later that he had no memory of it at all. There was the scaffold under his feet, and then there was the swinging ladder and the boy's arm in his fist, and then there was the ground, much too soon, and a pain in his left knee like a spike driven into it. He rolled. He came up with Niketas behind him and his spear somehow still in his right hand, and the painted giant was standing over them with its axe raised against the sky.

He thrust up at its belly. The spear point skidded on the great rings of its mail and slid off.

The giant laughed. Its breath smelled of rotten meat. The axe came down.

Stephanos threw himself and the boy sideways into the rubble. The axe blade buried itself in the earth where they had been, so deep that the haft stood upright. The giant grunted and heaved at it. Stephanos got his feet under him, pushed Niketas behind a fallen block of limestone, and looked for something, anything, that would hurt a thing that size. His shield was on the wall. His mace was at the Tower. He had a spear that would not bite through its mail and a trowel in his belt.

The second giant, the one with the skulls, was coming along the ditch toward them.

"Hey!" someone shouted from the top of the breach. "Hey! Big man! Big stupid man with the paint!"

The painted giant looked up.

On the broken edge of the new wall, silhouetted against the pale sky, stood a scarecrow of a man in rags. He had a beard like a bird's nest and bare feet black with dirt. He was holding a basket, and as Stephanos watched, he reached into it and threw a handful of walnuts at the giant's head.

The walnuts bounced off the giant's face. It blinked.

"You look like a cake!" the ragged man shouted. "A big wet cake that fell off the table! Did your mother paint you? Did she run out of paint before she got to your brains?"

He threw more walnuts. The giant roared and let go of its axe and turned toward the wall. It reached up with one huge hand for the ragged man, who danced backward along the top of the breach, cackling, and threw a walnut into its open mouth.

Stephanos did not waste the moment. The giant's back was to him, and it had bent to reach, and its mail had ridden up. Between the hem of the mail and the top of its leather boot was the back of its knee, pale and bare as a child's.

He put the spear into it with both hands and all his weight.

The giant screamed. Its leg folded, and it fell forward against the wall, and the new stones held. Stephanos pulled the spear free and drove it in again, higher, under the mail. The giant thrashed, and one flailing arm caught him across the chest and threw him ten feet into the rubble. He felt the breastplate take it. He felt the word Justice press into his ribs.

Then an arrow went into the painted giant's eye, and another went in beside it, and the giant stopped moving.

Stephanos lay on his back and looked at the sky. It was a clean, pale blue, with one small cloud in it. He was aware, distantly, that the second giant was still in the ditch and that he was lying down and ought to get up.

He heard a third arrow, and a fourth. Then horns on the wall, and a great deal of shouting, and the sound of something heavy running away through the suburbs.

A face appeared above him. It belonged to a woman of about his own age, lean and brown, with black hair cut short as a boy's and a long scar that ran from her left ear to the corner of her mouth. She had a horn bow in her hand, the double-curved bow of the eastern borderers, and a quiver on her hip with four arrows left in it.

"You're the Hornbearer who jumped off the scaffold," she said. Her Greek had the flat vowels of the Taurus mountains.

"Yes."

"That was stupid."

"Yes."

"The boy's alive. He's crying, but he's alive." She held out a hand. "Kale. Of the Akritai, from the Kilikian passes. I came to warn the Eparch that the Gibborim are moving on the western roads. I seem to be a little late."

He took her hand and let her haul him up. His knee buckled and he caught himself on her shoulder. She did not flinch.

"The other one?" he asked.

"Ran. I put two arrows in his back. He'll live, if giants can be said to live." She glanced up at the breach. "Your friend with the walnuts will want thanking."

The ragged man was sitting on the edge of the wall with his legs dangling, eating the last of the walnuts out of his basket. When he saw them looking, he waved.

"Sergios," Stephanos said. "They call him the Sparrow. He sleeps in the porch of the Great Church. He's a salos."

"A holy fool." Kale considered him. "My grandmother used to say the fools see the things everyone else is too clever to see."

"He threw walnuts at a giant."

"He did," she agreed. "And it worked."

---

Niketas's father came running along the ditch with his face white, and snatched up the boy, and wept on him, and then tried to kiss Stephanos's hands, which Stephanos did not allow. The masons came down from the wall and stood around the dead giant at a careful distance, pointing at it and talking in low voices. It was bigger lying down than it had been standing up. Its face under the white clay was the face of a young man, with a young man's patchy beard.

Stephanos made himself look at it. The Abba said a Hornbearer should look at what he had killed and remember that it had been made by God before it was bent by the rebels. He looked. He found that he could not feel anything but a great tiredness and, under the tiredness, the old familiar heat.

He looked up at the hill. The burned chapel stood empty on the crest. Tobias and his riders were nowhere to be seen.

"He sent them," Stephanos said.

Kale followed his gaze. "Who?"

"Tobias of Selymbria. He was on the hill this morning, laughing. An hour after he leaves, two Gibborim come straight to the weakest place on the wall. They didn't come for plunder. They came to break the scaffold and kill the masons." He heard his own voice rising and did not try to stop it. "He's sold himself to the Tower. They all have. Every one of those hill lords would open the gates to Nebrodes tomorrow for a bag of Concord silver, and we let them sit up there and laugh at us."

"Brother," said Gregoras, who had come down the ladder with a bloody cloth wrapped around his hand.

"No. Not this time. Someone should ride up there and..."

"And what? Burn Selymbria? Kill Tobias's wife and children?" Gregoras's voice was mild. "The Domestikos will ask the Eparch to send a letter of complaint, and Tobias will say the giants were hunting on their own account and he's shocked, shocked, that such a thing could happen so near his lands. And he'll be lying, and everyone will know it, and it won't matter. That's the game, Stephanos. You know it's the game."

"Then the game is rotten."

"Yes," said Gregoras. "Now go to Kosmas and get that knee looked at before you fall down."

---

The Fool's Tower stood on the Land Walls a mile north of Charisios, where the wall bent toward the Golden Horn. It had been a watchtower once, square and plain, five storeys high. Pelagios the Fool had taken it for his order thirty years ago because no one else wanted it; the top two floors had burned in the fall of the City and the roof had fallen in. The first Hornbearers had rebuilt it with their own hands. Now it held a chapel on the ground floor, a refectory and armoury above that, cells for twenty knights above that, and at the top, under a new roof of red tile, a single room where the Abba lived.

Stephanos climbed the stair in the evening, after Kosmas the physician had bound his knee and told him to stay off it for a week. He had not told Kosmas he meant to climb five storeys. Kosmas would have found out anyway. Kosmas found out everything.

The Abba's door stood open. It always stood open.

The room was bare. It held a bed, a stool, a clay lamp, a jar of water, and an icon of the Mother of God so old and smoke-darkened that only her eyes and her hands still showed. The Abba sat on the stool by the window, facing out, with the last of the sun on his face.

Pelagios the Fool was seventy-four years old. He had been blind for eleven of them. His hair and beard were white and fine as a child's, and he wore the same patched grey habit he had worn when he rode into the City at sixteen in armour stripped from a dead raider. He did not look like the founder of anything. He looked like a shepherd who had wandered in from the hills and sat down to rest.

"Stephanos," he said, without turning his head.

"Abba." Stephanos knelt on his good knee and kissed the old man's hand. It was dry and light as paper. "How did you know?"

"You climb stairs like a man carrying a sack of grain up a hill. Everyone else climbs them like men." The Abba smiled. "Sit. Sit on the bed. Kosmas says you are not to stand."

"Kosmas has been here?"

"Kosmas has been everywhere. It is his gift." The Abba turned his face from the window toward the sound of Stephanos's breathing. His eyes were white and clouded, and they did not quite find him. "You killed a giant today."

"Kale of the Akritai killed it. I put a spear in its knee. And Sergios threw walnuts at it."

The Abba laughed, a sound like a dry leaf blown across stone. "Good. Good. That is the way of it. A spear in the knee, an arrow in the eye, a handful of nuts. The giants never understand it. They think the world is made for the tall." He was quiet for a moment. "And the boy?"

"Alive."

"Then you did well. Why are you angry?"

Stephanos opened his mouth, and shut it. He looked at the icon on the wall. The Mother of God looked back at him with her dark, patient eyes.

"Tobias of Selymbria sent those giants," he said at last. "I'm sure of it. He mocked us from the hill this morning, and then they came. He's Concord-bought, Abba, or worse. And we do nothing. We lay stones and wait, and the hill lords laugh, and the Gibborim come down whenever they like and kill our children. We have been waiting for thirty years."

"Yes."

"The Domestikos says we could end it. He says that if we had an emperor, a real one, crowned in the Great Church, the hill lords would bend the knee in a week. The themes would rise. The Tower would have to answer for itself. And there is a man who could do it, Abba. Lord Bardas Argyros. He has the Shepherd's blood; the Grammatikoi have traced it. He's pious. He's brave. He fought Anak's raiders at the Sangarios with forty men and beat them. The Domestikos says the Sword faction could crown him by the Feast of the Cross, and the people would follow him."

"Yes," said the Abba. "The Domestikos says that."

"And you? What do you say?"

The old man turned his face back to the window. The sun had gone below the hills now, and the sky over Thrace was the colour of a bruise, purple and yellow. Stephanos could see the burned chapel on the far slope, black against it.

"When I was young," the Abba said, "before I found the lame lord's hall the second time, I spent a year in a monastery in the hills above Amaseia. The abbot there had me read the books of Samuel to him, because his eyes were going. Do you know the story of the anointing of David?"

"The prophet went to Bethlehem," said Stephanos. "To the house of Jesse."

"Yes. God told Samuel to go to Jesse, for he had chosen a king among his sons. And Jesse brought out his sons one after another. The first was Eliab. He was tall, like Saul, and handsome, and strong, and Samuel looked at him and thought, surely this is the Lord's anointed." The Abba folded his hands in his lap. "And God said to Samuel: do not look at his appearance or at the height of his stature, because I have rejected him. The Lord does not see as man sees. Man looks on the outward appearance, but the Lord looks on the heart."

"I know the story, Abba."

"Do you? Samuel was the greatest prophet in Israel. He had anointed Saul with his own hands. And still he looked at the tallest son in the room and thought, this one." The old man turned his clouded eyes toward Stephanos again. "You are looking for the tallest man in the room, Stephanos. So is the Domestikos. So is every lord in the empire. It is the most natural thing in the world."

Stephanos said nothing. The heat was back in his chest, and he did not want the Abba to hear it in his voice.

"Lord Bardas is a good man," the Abba said. "Perhaps God has chosen him. I do not know. I only know that I have not been told, and neither have you, and neither has the Domestikos. And I know that when I sat in the lame lord's hall at sixteen and saw the horn go by, I did not ask the question, because I thought a good guest keeps quiet. I waited twenty years to ask it." He smiled. "Ask it with me now."

Stephanos bowed his head. He had said it every day for nine years, at every chapel and every altar and every shrine on every road he had ridden. He said it now with the old man, softly, in the dim room at the top of the tower.

"Whom will the Horn anoint?"

The room was quiet. Outside, a gull cried over the Golden Horn. Somewhere below, in the chapel, the brothers had begun the evening psalms.

No one answered. No one ever did.

"Good," said the Abba, as if something had been settled. "Now go and sleep. Kosmas will be angry with me for keeping you."

Stephanos got up. At the door he stopped.

"Abba. What if no one answers? What if we wait another thirty years, and the Tower comes, and the City falls again, and the Horn never comes back? What then?"

The old man did not turn from the window.

"Then we will have asked the right question," he said, "and they will have answered the wrong one. I would rather be in our place than theirs." He lifted one thin hand. "Go and sleep, my son. God is not in a hurry. It is one of the most annoying things about him."

Stephanos went down the stairs. His knee hurt at every step. At the bottom, in the dark doorway of the chapel, he stood a long while listening to the psalms, and he found that he was still angry, and that he did not know at whom.
