## Chapter 22: The Last Night of the Wall

*Bear with each other and forgive one another if any of you has a grievance against someone. Forgive as the Lord forgave you.*

*Paul, to the church at Colossae*

---

They buried the Abba at dusk, in the chapel of the Fool's Tower, under the floor in front of the altar, where he had asked to lie.

There was no time for more. The Hunter's host had come up to the ditch at noon and stopped there, and the Eparch's archers had held it there all afternoon, but everyone on the walls knew it was only waiting. The seers were at work. Kale said she could feel them, a dozen of them, standing in a ring behind the front ranks of the host with their hands raised toward the walls, and the green fire gathering. "Tonight," she said. "Tonight they'll break something."

So the brothers dug the grave in the afternoon, the eleven who were left, taking turns with the spade between their watches on the wall. They wrapped the Abba in his patched grey habit and laid him on a plank and carried him down the stair from the room at the top of the Tower, the room with the stool and the window and the old smoke-blackened icon, and he weighed so little that two of them could have carried him alone. They laid him in the ground.

The Patriarch came from the Great Church to say the prayers. He stood at the head of the grave with the censer swinging in his large soft hands and his face grey with grief, and he read the service for the burial of a monk, all of it, without hurrying, while the war-drums of the Hunter beat outside the walls like a second heart.

At the end, when the prayers were done and the brothers stood around the open grave with their heads bowed, the Patriarch looked at Stephanos.

"He would want it asked," the old man said. "Here. Over him."

Stephanos stepped forward to the edge of the grave. He looked down at the small grey shape at the bottom of it.

He had asked it every day for nine years. He had asked it at every shrine on every road. He had asked it in anger and in weariness and in hope and in despair, and he had asked it demanding an answer, the way a man knocks on a door and knocks again and shouts through the keyhole. He had asked it as if the question were a key and he could make it turn.

He asked it now as the Abba had asked it. Quietly. To the dark. Expecting nothing, and not minding.

"Whom will the Horn anoint?"

The eleven brothers said it with him. Then Kale said it, and Lazaros. Then Amma Thekla. Then Theophanes, in a whisper. Then Petros, who did not know the words and stumbled. Then Sergios, loud and cheerful, as if it were a riddle at a feast.

Then Dora.

She was standing at the foot of the grave with the veil of the Myrrh-bearer over her hair. She said it last, alone, after everyone else had finished. She said it very softly, as if she were asking it of the old man in the grave and not of the dark at all.

Nobody answered. Nobody ever did.

But Stephanos, looking at the small grey shape in the ground, thought that the Abba would have been pleased.

---

Theophanes found him after, in the empty refectory.

The others had gone up to the walls, or to the kitchen, or to sleep for an hour before the night came. Stephanos was sitting at the long table cut from the rock with his helm in front of him, oiling the leather of the chin strap, because his hands wanted something to do.

The scribe stood in the doorway for a long time before he came in. He had washed his face and changed his robe, but he still looked terrible. He was holding the purple book against his chest with both arms, the way he had held it since the Goldhand's Mint.

"Stephanos," he said.

Stephanos put down the helm.

Theophanes came to the table. He did not sit. He stood across from Stephanos with the book in his arms, and he looked at the floor, and then he made himself look up.

"I wrote to Kallinikos," he said. "At the laura. After the Well. I wrote down the four Words, and what they meant, and that the Horn was under the Great Church, and that the least was Dora. And I wrote that you'd leave her at the Tower with the Abba when the Hunter came, and ride out with the Emperor, because I knew you would." His voice was very steady, as if he had rehearsed it. "I gave the letter to Master Ioannes the silk merchant the night before the bridge. He took it to the Master. And the Master came with the seer, and the Abba stood in the door, and died. And they took her." He paused. "I stood in the lane and watched them take her. And then I went with them."

Stephanos said nothing.

"I told myself I was saving her," said Theophanes. "I told myself the giants would take her whatever I did, and the Concord would keep her safe, and I was choosing the lesser evil. I told myself I was the only one who could." He swallowed. "Sergios says that's the stranger's best trick. He says it works on the good ones better than the bad." He laughed, very briefly. "I'm not sure I was ever one of the good ones. But I thought I was."

He put the purple book down on the table between them. He opened it to the back, where the four Words were written in burnt twig, and beside them, folded small, the letter from Kallinikos. He pushed it across the table.

"Read it," he said. "Please. You should know all of it."

Stephanos did not pick it up.

"The Abba died because of me," said Theophanes. His steady voice cracked at last. "Gregoras died, and the Emperor, and all the brothers on the stair. Dora nearly... She was in chains for a day and a night because of me. I don't have anything to give you. I can't pay it back. I don't know what else to say except that I'm sorry, and that I'll do anything, anything at all, and that if you want me to go down into the Undercroft first and let whatever's at the bottom eat me, I'll go."

He stood there across the table with his cut hands at his sides, and waited.

Stephanos looked at him.

He thought of the fog off Naxos. He thought of the hand on his wrist, pale and swollen and barnacled, and the voice counting his debts. He thought of the Goldhand's hall, and the red eagle of Taron on the marble floor, and his count's smiling face in the courtyard at Kaisareia. He thought of the Goldhand on the wet rock of the Mint, naked, weeping, *my son, I gave my son*, and his own sword raised over him, and the words he could not say.

He thought of the lion in the circle of starlight, asking him three times.

He stood up.

He came around the end of the long table, limping, and he stood in front of Theophanes. The scribe flinched, very slightly, as if he expected to be struck.

Stephanos put his hands on Theophanes's shoulders.

"I forgive you," he said.

The words came out easily. That was the strange thing. He had carried them like a stone for nine years and more, and he had not been able to lift them, not in the fog, not in the Mint, not on the deck of the *Saint Phokas* with everyone around him shouting them into the sea. And now they came out of him as easily as breath, as if they had been waiting a long time just behind his teeth.

"I forgive you, Theophanes," he said again. "It's gone. I don't want it. You don't owe me anything."

Theophanes stared at him. His face worked. He opened his mouth and shut it again.

"How?" he said at last. "How can you? After everything?"

"Because I was forgiven," said Stephanos. "Last night. For the same thing. For thinking I was the only one who could, and for leaving her behind to do it." He let go of the scribe's shoulders. "Everybody picks the wrong way sometimes. The fool told you that. The trick is to pick another one."

He went back to his side of the table and sat down, and picked up his helm, and went on oiling the chin strap. His hands were shaking a little. He did not mind.

After a while, Theophanes sat down across from him. He did not say anything. He took the letter from Kallinikos out of the back of the purple book, and unfolded it, and read it once more by the light of the lamp. Then he held it to the lamp's flame, and watched it burn, and dropped the last corner of it on the stone floor and ground it out with his heel.

"There's something else," he said. "Something I didn't tell the Master. Something I didn't understand myself until the salt city." He turned the purple book around on the table so that Stephanos could see the page. "The fourth Word. *When the least of the Shepherd's house goes down, the Horn will come up.* I told him the least was Dora. I told him she had to go down. But I didn't tell him what I think it means." He put his finger on the word *down*. "The Gate of Sheol, Stephanos. The Horn keeps the door. It went down to keep the door shut, the night the proud one stood in the church, because the door was opening. And it's been down there a hundred years, holding it. If it comes up..."

"Then the door opens," said Stephanos slowly.

"Unless someone holds it instead." Theophanes looked at him. "I think that's what the Word means. The least goes down, and takes the Horn's place at the door, so the Horn can come up. I think whoever goes down with her has to be ready to stay."

Stephanos looked at the page for a long moment.

"Have you told her?" he said.

"No."

"Don't," said Stephanos. "Not yet."

---

The Domestikos came up the stair of the Fool's Tower an hour after dark.

He came slowly, one step at a time, with Kosmas the physician holding his right elbow. His left arm ended in a thick wad of linen above the elbow, bound tight across his chest, and there was blood seeping through it. His face was grey and sunken and old. He had not been old two days ago.

He stopped in the doorway of the refectory and looked at them all: at Stephanos at the long table, and Theophanes beside him, and Kale and Lazaros by the fire, and Amma Thekla in the corner with her basket, and Sergios on the floor with the lamb. And Petros, standing by the wall with his arms folded and his head bowed, in a borrowed shirt too small for him. And Dora, sitting on the end of the bench with her feet not touching the floor.

He looked at Dora longest.

Then he came into the room and, with Kosmas holding him, lowered himself onto one knee on the stone floor in front of Stephanos.

"Domestikos," said Stephanos. He was on his feet. "Don't. You're hurt."

"I'm hurt," Nikephoros agreed. His voice was a rasp. "I'm hurt, and I'm old, and I'm a fool, and I've lost my arm and my Emperor and eleven hundred knights in one afternoon because I wanted a crown on a strong man's head more than I wanted to wait for God." He looked up. His scarred face was wet. "I named you oathbreaker, Stephanos of Taron. In chapter. In the register. I had the right. You broke your oath." He paused. "You broke it to go where God was sending you. I kept mine to go where I wanted to go. I don't know which of us was the oathbreaker in the end."

"Domestikos..."

"I anointed him with lamp oil," said Nikephoros. "In the Hippodrome. With the Patriarch's back turned and the whole City watching. I poured it on his head with my own hands, and I thought I was making an emperor. I was making a corpse." He shut his eyes. "The Abba sat in the corner of this room and said nothing. He never told me I was wrong. He only asked his question. I thought it was weakness. I thought he was a blind old fool who didn't understand how the world works." He opened his eyes. "He understood it better than any of us."

He looked past Stephanos, to the end of the bench, where Dora sat with her feet dangling.

"Is it her?" he said.

The room was very quiet.

"I don't know," said Stephanos. "I don't know, Domestikos. It's not mine to know." He glanced at Dora, and then back. "But she's the least of the Shepherd's house. And she's going down."

The Domestikos looked at the girl for a long time. She looked back at him steadily, with her dark eyes under the torn veil.

"The walls will fall tonight," he said at last, still looking at her. "Kale's right; the seers are gathering at Charisios. At the new stone. It's the weakest place on the Land Walls; they'll know that. When it breaks, the Hunter's host will come into the City through the breach, and they'll go straight for the Great Church. They know what's under it. The Master Scribe told them." He turned back to Stephanos. "What's left of the Tagma will hold the doors of the Great Church. As long as we can. That's what we can do. Let us do it."

"I'll hold them with you," said Petros.

Everyone turned.

Petros had unfolded his arms. He was standing away from the wall now, very tall, with the bandage around his head and the borrowed shirt stretched across his shoulders. His face was pale and set and older than Stephanos had ever seen it.

"I'm not the heir," Petros said. He said it to Stephanos, plainly, the way a man says a thing he has said to himself a hundred times in the dark until the saying of it has stopped hurting. "I know that now. I think I knew it on the mountain. I knew it for certain in the Hunter's camp. The seers laughed at me. They stood outside my tent and laughed. They said, *The knight thinks this one is the heir. The knight thinks the Horn will come up for a boy who ate the Queen's cakes and boasted to Anak's face.* They laughed so hard one of them fell down." He did not smile. "They knew it was Dora all along. From the letter. From the stars. From whatever it is seers read. They knew the moment the Master opened the lame lord's letter. They only took me first because the knight believed it was me, and they wanted him to go on believing it."

"Petros," said Dora.

"No. Let me say it." He turned to his sister. "I'm Eliab. Aren't I? From the story. The tall one. The one the prophet looked at first." He swallowed. "Grandmother used to read it to us. I always thought I was David. I thought you were... I don't know what I thought you were. The girl who did my milking." His voice cracked. "I'm sorry, Dora. I'm sorry for all of it. I'm sorry I called you jealous at the inn. I'm sorry I went with the man with no shadow. I'm sorry for the milking."

Dora slid off the bench. She went across the room to her brother and stood in front of him, and she had to tilt her head all the way back to look him in the face.

"You made me a lamp," she said.

"It was terrible."

"It was the best lamp in Thrace." She took his big hand in both of hers. "You held them off in the camp. With a giant's axe. With no armour. You stood in the mud and told me to run. I saw you." Her voice was shaking. "That's not Eliab, Petros. Eliab was angry at David for coming down to the battle. He didn't stand in front of him."

Petros looked down at her. His face crumpled. He went down on his knees on the stone floor of the refectory, the way the Domestikos had, and put his arms around his little sister and his face against her shoulder, and wept like a child.

Dora held him. She looked over his head at Stephanos.

Stephanos looked away, because he could not bear to look, and found that Amma Thekla was watching him from the corner. The old woman's hands had stopped on her basket. She was smiling.

---

The seers broke the wall at Charisios two hours before midnight.

Stephanos was on the battlement of the Fool's Tower when it happened. He saw the green fire gather in the dark beyond the ditch, a ring of it, a dozen points of sick light in a circle, like the eyes of wolves around a fold. He saw the ring tighten. He saw the fire flow together into a single bar of light, green and white and blinding, and leap out across the ditch toward the wall a mile south of him.

It struck the new stone below the Gate of Charisios. The stone he had laid with his own hands, course by course, on the morning a mason's boy called Niketas nearly died.

The wall exploded.

It went up in a fountain of rubble and flame, higher than the towers, and came down in a roar that shook the Fool's Tower to its foundations. When the dust cleared, there was a gap in the Land Walls a hundred paces wide, and through it, black against the red glow of the Hunter's fires, the host was pouring into the City.

The horns on the walls sounded, all of them, from the Golden Gate to the Blachernae, the long low note of disaster.

"Now," said Stephanos.

---

They went through the burning City to the Great Church.

The Hunter's host had come in at Charisios and turned south, toward the Golden Gate and the Studion, burning as it went. The Eparch's watch was falling back before it street by street, fighting at every corner. Stephanos could hear it over the roofs as the band ran east through the dark along the Mese: the roar of the giants, the crash of falling houses, the screams, the horns. Fires were springing up behind them. The whole western sky was orange.

They ran. Stephanos in front, in his lamellar, with his sword drawn and the purple of the Tagma on his shield. Kale behind him with a new bow from the armoury and every arrow she could carry. Lazaros with the icon of the Archistrategos on his back. Amma Thekla, who could not run, on Petros's shoulders, weaving. Sergios skipping alongside with the lamb. Theophanes with the purple book. And Dora, in the middle of them, with the sling of Poimen on her wrist and, in her hands, wrapped in a scrap of cloth, the ugly clay lamp her brother had made her out of river mud when she was nine.

"We'll need lamps," she had said in the refectory. "The Word says. *It went down with the lamps.* We'll need to light them." And she had taken it out of the bundle she had carried all the way from Arkadiopolis to Paros to Thessalonike to Göreme to the Wilderness and back, and filled it with oil from the Tower's store, and wrapped it in cloth. "It leaks," she had said. "It'll do."

Behind them, what was left of the Tagma came running: eleven brothers of the Fool's Tower in their purple, and twenty more from the chapter houses of the City, and the Domestikos at their head with his sword in his one hand and his face like iron. Kosmas came too, with a bag of bandages over his shoulder. He said nobody was going to bleed to death on the steps of the Great Church if he could help it.

The square in front of the Great Church was full of people.

Stephanos had not expected that. He stopped at the mouth of the Mese and stared. The whole square was crowded, from the steps of the church to the Milion and beyond: men and women and children, old people, the sick carried on litters, babies in arms. They had fled from the west of the City as the host came in, and they had come here. To the church with no roof. Because there was nowhere else to go.

They were singing.

It was the Trisagion. The thrice-holy hymn, the oldest hymn of the Great Church, the one the monks said the angels sing around the throne. *Holy God, Holy Mighty, Holy Immortal, have mercy on us.* The people in the square were singing it, thousands of them, over and over, in the dark with the City burning behind them and the war-drums coming closer, and the sound rose up out of the square and went up into the night like smoke.

The Patriarch was standing on the steps of the church in his vestments with the Gospel book held up in both hands. The Eparch stood beside him. Her black veil had come off and her iron-grey hair was loose and there was blood on her face, and she had a sword in her hand.

"The watch is broken," she said as they came up the steps. Her voice was hoarse. "What's left is falling back to here. The host will be in the square within the hour." She looked at Dora, and then at Stephanos. "Go. Whatever you're going to do. Go now."

---

The sanctuary was dark except for the seven candles around the icon of the Archistrategos, still burning on the floor where Lazaros had left them.

The line of grass was still there. It ran from the circle under the dome, through the nave, through the Beautiful Gate, across the sanctuary floor to the lip of the crack. It had not withered. The small white flowers in it were closed for the night.

Dora stopped at the Beautiful Gate and looked at it for a long time.

"He walked here," she said.

"Yes."

"And lay down there. Across the crack." She looked at the icon on the floor.

"Yes."

She did not say anything else. She walked along the line of grass, very carefully, not stepping on it, to the edge of the crack. She stood there looking down into the dark.

Lazaros knelt beside the icon. He put his scarred hands on the frame. He looked up at Stephanos, and Stephanos nodded, and the painter lifted the icon of the Archistrategos off the crack in the floor.

The dark came up out of it like cold water.

Stephanos felt it on his face: a breath of air from very far below, cold and dry and old, smelling of dust and stone and something else, something sweetish and rotten, like the smell that came out of the bound emperor's tomb in the crypt the first time they went down. He heard, far, far below, very faint, the sound of singing. Backward.

Behind them, through the nave and the open doors of the Great Church, he heard the singing of the people in the square falter. He heard, over it, the roar of the Hunter's host coming down the Mese.

The Domestikos stood in the Beautiful Gate with his sword in his one hand. Behind him in the nave, the Tagma was forming up across the width of the church, a thin line of purple from wall to wall, facing the doors. Petros stood at the end of the line, with a mace from the Tower's armoury in his hands and a Hornbearer's shield on his arm.

"Go down," said Nikephoros. "We'll hold the doors."

"How long?" said Stephanos.

"As long as it takes." The Domestikos did not smile. "The Abba stood in a door. So can we."

Stephanos looked at Petros. Petros looked back at him from the end of the line, and lifted the mace a little, and nodded.

Then Stephanos turned to the crack in the floor of the sanctuary, where Dora was standing with her brother's ugly lamp in her hands.

He knelt beside her.

"I'll go first," he said.

"No," said Dora.

He looked at her.

"The Word says the least goes down," she said. "Not the knight. Not the strong. The least." She unwrapped the clay lamp from its cloth. "I'll go first. You come behind me."

"Dora..."

"You said you'd go down with me," she said. "Not in front of me. With me." She held out the lamp to him. "Light it."

He took a candle from the floor beside the icon and held it to the wick of the ugly lamp. The wick caught. The small flame burned up, yellow and steady, in the lopsided clay bowl with its crooked spout and its crack down one side. Oil beaded at the crack and dripped. The flame did not waver.

Dora took it back. She held it up.

Then she stepped over the edge of the crack, onto the first of a flight of rough steps cut in the rock that Stephanos had not seen until the light touched them, and began to go down.

He went after her. And behind him, one by one, the others: Kale with her bow, Lazaros with the icon, Theophanes with the book, Sergios with the lamb, and last, Amma Thekla, lowered by Kosmas the physician onto the first step, weaving.

Above them in the Great Church, the doors crashed open, and the Hunter's host came in, and the Tagma of the Horn met it in the nave with a sound like the end of the world, and the people outside in the square went on singing, *Holy God, Holy Mighty, Holy Immortal*, until the sound of it faded above them into the dark, and there was nothing but the steps going down, and the little flame in the ugly lamp, and the backward singing coming up from below.
