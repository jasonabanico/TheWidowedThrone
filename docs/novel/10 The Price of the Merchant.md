## Chapter 10: The Price of the Merchant

*Look, Lord! Here and now I give half of my possessions to the poor, and if I have cheated anybody out of anything, I will pay back four times the amount.*

*Zacchaeus, the chief tax collector of Jericho*

---

They broke the upper shelf first.

Petros did most of it. He went along the line of presses with the chain in his fists, smashing the rings that held the other prisoners, and every time a ring broke he shouted, a great wordless roar of joy that rang off the roof of the cave. Stephanos followed with his sword and cut the chains that Petros could not break. Kale and Lazaros and the eleven freed men from the chapel came behind and helped the prisoners up, and led them back toward the tunnel mouth, and the prisoners stumbled and wept and could not believe it.

There were forty-three of them on the upper shelf. The six other Thracian boys were among them, thin and frightened, clinging to Petros when he freed them as if he were their father. So were the crew of the Eparch's grain ship, the *Theotokos*, and their captain, Fotios, a grey-bearded man with a cross branded on his forehead by the Goldhand's men as a joke. When Stephanos cut the chain from his ankle, Fotios fell on his knees in the water and kissed the purple on the Hornbearer's shield.

"We'll get you out," Stephanos told him. "All of you."

Behind him, the presses had stopped.

He did not notice it at first. The thud of the iron weights had been so constant, so much a part of the cave, that its absence felt only like a change in the air, a pressure lifting. Then he noticed the silence. On the lower shelves, the wights had stopped turning their wheels. They stood beside their presses in the dark water with their white faces turned downward, toward the black pool at the bottom of the cave.

"Stephanos," said Kale. "Look."

The pool was rising.

It did not rise like a tide. It swelled, as if something beneath it were pushing up, and the surface bulged in a dome, and broke. Water poured off in sheets. And out of the black pool, slowly, came the Goldhand.

---

Stephanos had thought he understood what the Bowed became, when they went all the way. He had heard the stories of the Idol-born: lords who gave their archon the thing they loved most, and were filled with it, and swelled. He had not believed them. They were the kind of stories the Akritai told around fires on the border, to frighten the young ones.

He believed them now.

The thing that came up out of the pool was the height of three men. It was made of coins. Gold and silver and copper, millions of them, fused together into a mass that heaved and shifted as it moved, like a heap of sand trying to stand up. Harbour chains hung from it, green with weed. Barnacles crusted it. Anchors dangled from it like charms from a bracelet. It had no legs; it rolled forward on a wave of coins that poured out from under it and ran back into it, clinking, an endless golden tide. It had arms, long, made of chain wound around coin, ending in hands as big as a ship's boat, with gold fingers.

And set in the middle of it, where a chest would be on a man, small and pale and human, was the Goldhand's face.

It was the same face that had smiled at Stephanos across the table in the hall above. Lean and brown and pleasant and clever. It looked out of the heaving mass of gold like a man looking out of a window in a burning house.

"Stephanos of Taron," it said.

Its voice came from the face, small and human. But under it, from the whole mass of gold, came another voice, vast and deep and slow, like the sound of the sea in a cave. The two voices spoke together, one on top of the other.

"*You broke my presses. You woke my debtors. You lit a lamp in my crypt.*" The face smiled. "*That was rude. I invited you to dinner.*"

"Let them go," said Stephanos. "All of them. Every man on every shelf."

"*They owe me.*"

"They owe you nothing. You took everything they had. You took their lives."

"*I lent them money,*" said the Goldhand reasonably. "*They agreed to the terms. I have the contracts. Would you like to see them?*" It lifted one great golden hand, and a shower of parchment fell out of it, scrolls and sheets, fluttering down onto the water. "*Every one signed. Every one witnessed. That's not theft, Hornbearer. That's business. You'd understand it if you'd stayed with me.*"

"I'm not here to argue with you."

"*No. You're here to kill me.*" The small face looked sad. "*Everyone always is. But I'm a merchant, Stephanos. I don't fight until I've made an offer. It's bad for business.*" The face turned, slowly, toward the others on the upper shelf. "*So. An offer. For each of you. Anything you like, in exchange for walking away. And I mean it. Every offer is real. I don't cheat on a deal once it's made. Ask anyone.*"

Its eyes went to Petros first.

"*You,*" said the Goldhand. "*The lame lord's great-grandson. The one they took from Thrace for me.*" The small face considered him. "*You're strong. You're brave. You want to be a great man. Your grandmother told you so every night.*" One great gold hand came down toward Petros, and opened, and in its palm lay a sword.

It was a beautiful sword. The blade was long and straight and bright as a mirror, and the hilt was gold, set with a great red stone in the pommel, and the scabbard lying beside it was purple leather worked with gold eagles. It was the sword of an emperor.

"*Take it,*" said the Goldhand. "*It was the sword of Andronikos. I bought it from the Concord, who took it from the ruins of the palace. It's the finest blade in the empire. It will never break and never dull. Take it, and you'll walk out of here a free man, and I'll give you Thrace to rule, and ships to carry your men. The Shepherd's heir should have a sword.*"

Petros stared at it.

Stephanos saw his face. He saw the hunger in it, plain as daylight. He saw the boy who had wrestled a bull calf at the harvest feast while the girls screamed, and who had grown up in a shepherd's hut on bread and olives, hearing every night that he would be a great man.

"Petros," said Dora sharply. "No."

"It's only a sword," said Petros. His voice was hoarse. "Dora, it's the Emperor's sword."

"It's his sword. It's the Goldhand's."

"He said I could have it." Petros reached out. His hand closed on the golden hilt. "He said the Shepherd's heir..."

He lifted it out of the gold palm. He held it up, and the light from the tunnel mouth ran down the blade like water. His face was shining.

"*There,*" said the Goldhand softly. "*Done. That's a deal.*"

And Petros stood still.

He did not move. He did not lower the sword. He stood there on the rock shelf with it raised in both hands, and a look of surprise came slowly over his face, the look of a man who has walked into a room and forgotten why.

"I can't," he said. "I can't... Dora, I can't swing it. I can't move it at him. I tried."

"*Of course not,*" said the Goldhand. "*You took my gift. A man can't bite the hand that feeds him. That's the other half of every deal.*" The small face turned away from him. "*Next.*"

It went down the line.

To Theophanes it offered a book. A thick book in a purple binding, worn at the corners, with gold clasps. "*The older chronicle,*" it said. "*The one your Master burned in the Chamber, except for this copy, which he sold to me. It proves the Shepherd lived. It proves every forgery in the Tower's library a lie. Take it, Theophanes. Take it back to the City and make a fool of Kallinikos in front of the whole council. Isn't that what you want? To prove him wrong?*"

Theophanes stood looking at the book for a long time. His face was grey. His hands were shaking.

"It's real?" he whispered.

"*Every word. I don't cheat.*"

"Theophanes," said Stephanos.

"I know," said Theophanes. "I know." And he stepped forward and took the book out of the golden hand, and held it against his chest with both arms, and stood still. Tears ran down his face. "I know. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I've wanted it for eleven years."

To Kale it offered names. A list of names, on a sheet of parchment. "*Your village,*" it said. "*In the Kilikian passes. The one Anak's raiders took when you were nineteen, while you were out hunting. You came back and found it burned. You thought they were all dead. They're not. Nineteen of them are alive, in Crete, on my estates. I'll give them back. All nineteen. Walk away.*"

Kale's face went white under its brown. The scar from her ear to her mouth stood out like a line of chalk. For a long moment she did not move.

Then she drew an arrow and put it to the string.

"If they're alive," she said, "I'll find them myself."

The Goldhand looked at her with its small, sad face. "*You'll never find them. Crete is a big island.*"

"Then I'll pray," said Kale, "and God will find them. He's better at it than you are."

To Lazaros it offered hands. "*New ones,*" it said, "*whole. The way they were before the Festival Council made you hold your own icon over the fire. You could paint again the way you used to. Fine work. Gold leaf. Faces so real people weep.*"

Lazaros looked down at his scarred palms. Then he looked up.

"My Lord kept his scars," he said quietly. "After the resurrection. He showed them to Thomas. I think I'll keep mine."

It did not offer anything to Amma Thekla. It did not even look at her. And when its small face came to Sergios, the fool made a rude noise with his lips, loud and wet and long, and the face flinched and turned away.

It came to Dora.

"*And you,*" it said. "*The little one who laughed at my banner.*" The face studied her. "*What do you want, child? Tell me. Anything.*"

"I want you to stop," said Dora.

The face blinked.

"I want you to stop taking people's boats and people's brothers," said Dora. "I want you to stop the fog. I want you to let all these men go home. That's what I want."

"*That's not a thing I can sell.*"

"No," said Dora. "I didn't think so." She shifted the lamb in her arms. "Then I don't want anything."

The face looked at her a long moment. Then, slowly, it turned to Stephanos.

"*And now you,*" it said.

Stephanos waited. He thought he knew what it would offer. The banner. It would offer the banner again, and the men who remembered it, and ships to carry them. He braced himself for it.

"*The name,*" said the Goldhand.

He did not understand at first.

"*The name of the heir,*" said the small face. Its voice was gentle. "*The one the Horn will anoint. My master knows it. The Sea-Dragon knows many things; he was a member of the council, once, before he rebelled, and he still hears things from the other side of the door. He knows whom the Horn will choose. And I know, because he told me.*" The face smiled. "*You've been asking for nine years, Stephanos of Taron. Every night. At every shrine. Whom will the Horn anoint? And nobody has ever answered. I'll answer. Now. Tonight. Here. I'll say the name, and you'll know, and you'll never have to ask again.*"

The cave was silent.

Stephanos stood on the rock shelf with his sword in his hand. He felt the water lapping around his boots. He felt the letter inside his breastplate, against the word Justice. He felt the belt of Truth around his waist, and it was not cold now. It was warm.

He thought about the Abba sitting by his window, asking the question to the dark.

*Then we will have asked the right question, and they will have answered the wrong one.*

He looked at Petros, standing frozen with the golden sword in his hands.

"I don't need your name," he said. "I've found my heir."

The small face in the gold looked at him. And then it laughed. It was a quiet laugh, almost kind, and under it the vast voice of the sea laughed too, a long slow boom that shook the water on the shelves.

"*Have you?*" said the Goldhand. "*Have you really?*"

Then it came for them.

---

The first blow of the great gold hand struck the shelf where Stephanos had been standing, and the rock split. He was already gone. He rolled through the water and came up with his sword and cut at the arm as it lifted, and the blade bit into the chain and the coins and rang like a bell and bounced off. It was like hitting a wall.

Coins burst out of the Goldhand's body.

They came in a storm, a roaring, glittering hail, millions of them, flung out in every direction as though from a sling. They struck the rock and the water and the presses and the men on the shelves. Stephanos got his shield up and felt them hammering against it like hail on a roof, and felt them strike his helm and his shoulders and his legs, and they were hot, burning hot, as if fresh from the dies. Around him men were screaming. He saw a prisoner fall with a coin buried in his cheek. He saw Kale go down on one knee with her arm over her face.

"Back!" he shouted. "Back to the tunnel!"

The coin-storm stopped. And out of the water on every shelf, the wights began to rise.

They came up out of the dark water in their hundreds, the drowned debtors, white and swollen and wrapped in gold, reaching. The coins that had fallen from the storm were rolling toward them across the rock, and leaping up, and fastening onto their chains, and the wights grew heavier and stronger with every coin that struck them.

"Forgive them!" Sergios was shouting. "Forgive them!"

But there were too many. The ones nearest heard and stopped and swayed. The ones behind them came on.

Stephanos cut down one wight and another. He stood at the head of the upper shelf with Kale beside him and Ioannikios's brother Demetrios on his other side swinging his candlestick, and the wights came up out of the water at them in a white tide. Behind the wights, the Goldhand rolled forward on its wave of coins, laughing.

He struck at it again when it came close. The blade rang off the coin-armour. It did not even scratch.

"It's no use," Kale gasped beside him. "Stephanos, it's no use. We can't cut it."

"Then what?"

She had no answer.

Dora did.

Stephanos did not see her go. He saw her after she had gone, out on the lower shelf below him, among the wights, with the lamb tied to her back in her shawl. She was bending down in the water and picking up coins. She had her skirt held up in front of her like a basket, and she was filling it with the Goldhand's gold, scooping it up in handfuls from the rock where the storm had thrown it.

And she was giving it away.

She went to the wights, one after another. She went right up to them, into the reach of those white drowned arms, and she held out coins to them in her small brown hands.

"Here," she said. "This is yours. It's yours. It was always yours. He took it. Have it back."

The wights stopped.

They looked down at her with their drowned faces. They looked at the coins in her hands. And one after another, they reached out their white fingers, and took the coins, and held them.

And the coins did not stick to their chains. The chains loosened.

"*No,*" said the Goldhand. Its small face was turned toward Dora. The laughter had gone out of it. "*No. Stop that. That's mine. Put that down.*"

"It's not yours," said Dora. She did not look up. She put a coin into another white hand. "It's theirs. You said you don't cheat. You cheated. You took their boats and their lives and you gave them nothing. This is theirs."

The mass of gold heaved. And Stephanos, watching, saw something he had not seen before.

A crack.

A long, thin crack, running across the coin-armour of the Goldhand's chest, just below the small human face. It had not been there a moment ago.

"Give it away!" he shouted. "All of you! Give it away! The coins! Give them to the wights! Throw them in the sea! Give it all away!"

They understood. Kale dropped her bow and began scooping coins off the rock and flinging them into the black water. Lazaros knelt and filled his arms with them and waded out toward the wights. The freed prisoners on the upper shelf, who had been cowering against the rock, came down into the water and began to pick up coins and give them to the drowned men who had once been their neighbours, and their brothers, and their friends.

Sergios had climbed onto a press. He was throwing coins from the top of it, great glittering handfuls, laughing and weeping at the same time, shouting, "Here! Here! Have it all!"

Stephanos stood still for a moment with his sword in his hand.

He had nothing to give. He had taken the vow of poverty nine years ago. He had no purse. He had no gold. He owned a horse and a sword and a suit of armour, and even those belonged to the Tagma.

He sheathed the sword. He bent down and picked up a coin from the water at his feet. It had the serpent on it. It was hot. He held it out to the nearest wight.

"Take it," he said. His voice was hoarse. "It's yours."

The wight took it.

The crack in the Goldhand's chest widened. Then another opened beside it, and another. The coin-armour was flaking, peeling away in great clinking sheets that fell into the water and sank. The Goldhand reeled. Its huge arms thrashed. Its small face was twisted with pain.

"*My money,*" it cried. "*My money. My money.*"

And then the water rose.

---

It came up from the black pool at the bottom of the cave in a single surge, and it did not stop. It rose over the lowest shelf, and the next, and the next. It rose around the wights, and they stood still in it and let it cover them. It rose over the presses. It came up around Stephanos's waist, cold as winter, and kept coming.

"The drowning," gasped Theophanes. He was on the upper shelf, clutching the purple book to his chest. "It's the last of it. He's going to drown us all."

"The tunnel!" Stephanos shouted. "Everyone! Up! Now!"

The water was at his chest. He found Dora beside him and grabbed her and lifted her up out of it, lamb and all, and set her on his shoulders. She clung to his helm. He turned toward the tunnel mouth. It was thirty paces away, uphill, across the flooded shelf, and the water was rising faster than he could walk.

He saw Amma Thekla. She was on the lowest part of the upper shelf, where she had been giving coins to the wights, and the water was at her neck. She was very small. She was not trying to swim. She was standing still with her face turned up and her eyes shut, and her lips were moving.

"Amma!"

Petros reached her first.

He was still holding the golden sword. He dropped it. It went into the black water and sank without a sound. Then he dived, and came up beside the old woman, and took her in his arms like a child and lifted her clean out of the water and set her on his shoulders, and began to swim.

He swam like a fish. He swam with one arm and his legs, holding the old woman on his shoulders with the other arm, and he cut through the rising water toward the tunnel mouth faster than Stephanos could wade. When the water came up over his head, he went on swimming under it, and came up again, and the Amma on his shoulders never went under at all.

He reached the tunnel mouth and climbed out onto the rock and set her down. Then he turned and dived back in.

Stephanos watched him do it four more times. Four times Petros went back into the flood and came out with a man on his shoulders: a Thracian boy, then Fotios the captain, then two of the prisoners who could not swim. The fifth time, the water had reached the roof of the tunnel mouth and there was no more going back.

Stephanos was the last one in. He came up the tunnel on his hands and knees with Dora clinging to his back and the water pouring down around him, and Petros reached down and grabbed him by the collar of his lamellar and hauled him up onto the rock as if he weighed nothing.

They lay there on the wet stone, gasping. Below them, the water had filled the tunnel. It lay still and black a few feet down. Nothing moved in it.

"Is he dead?" said Dora. "The Goldhand? Is he drowned?"

Nobody answered her.

Then the black water in the tunnel stirred, and bulged, and broke. And something crawled up out of it onto the rock.

---

It was a man.

He was small and thin and naked, and he was old, older than Stephanos had thought the Goldhand was, with loose brown skin hanging in folds from his bones and a few wisps of grey hair plastered to his skull. He crawled up out of the water on his hands and knees and collapsed on the rock and lay there, coughing up seawater. His right hand was not gold. It was flesh, withered and white and scarred, as if it had been burned a long time ago and never healed.

He lifted his face. It was the same face. Lean and brown and pleasant and clever. But it was not pleasant now. It was a ruined face, a face full of terror.

"Leon," he said. "Leon. Leon."

Stephanos got to his feet. He drew his sword. He stood over the old man with the blade raised. His heart was hammering. The heat was in his chest, the old familiar heat, roaring up like a fire in a chimney.

"Who is Leon?" he said.

"My son." The old man was weeping. "My son. My firstborn. He was seven. I gave him. I gave him to the Dragon, in the pool, at the dark of the moon. So the fog would come. So the ships would pay. He was seven, and he was afraid of the water, and I held his hand and walked him into it. I gave him. I gave him." He put his withered hands over his face. "Thirty years. Every night. I hear him."

The sword was very heavy in Stephanos's hand.

He thought of the courtyard at Kaisareia. He thought of his count's smiling face. He thought of the Goldhand's factor rolling up the banner and nailing it into the chest. He thought of forty-three men chained to presses in the dark, and hundreds more who had drowned there, and would never go home. He thought of a boy of seven, afraid of the water.

He wanted to bring the sword down more than he had wanted anything in his life.

"He's the fog," said Dora behind him.

He did not turn.

"He's all the debts," said Dora. "All the voices in the fog. All the counting. If you kill him now, Stephanos, you're doing the counting too. You're saying he owes it, and he has to pay." She came and stood beside him. She did not touch his arm. "Everybody's forgiven everybody tonight. Everybody but you."

The old man on the rock was looking up at them, at the sword, at the girl. His ruined face was waiting.

Sergios had come to stand on the other side of him. And Amma Thekla, dripping, bent, with Petros holding her elbow. And Kale, and Lazaros, and Theophanes with his purple book. They stood in a circle around the old man on the rock and said nothing.

Stephanos lowered the sword.

He could not say it. He tried. The words would not come; they stuck in his throat like a stone in a sling-pouch. *I forgive you.* He could not make them.

But he could say something else. He had said it at a hundred deathbeds in the east, over wounded men and dying villagers. It was the least a Hornbearer could say. It was all he had.

"Christ have mercy on you," he said. "He has it for you. If you want it."

The old man looked at him. For a long time, he did not move.

Then he bowed his head until it touched the wet rock.

"I want it," said Leon Chrysocheir. "God help me. I want it."

---

They came up out of the Drowned Mint at noon into the Goldhand's hall, through the crypt of the Panagia, where the lamp of Saint Mamas burned in its niche with a steady golden light. Sixty-one men came up with them. The rest of the wights had lain down in the water below, and the water had gone still, and Amma Thekla said they were at rest now and someone should say the prayers for them. Ioannikios's brother Demetrios said he would stay and say them. So did Fotios the captain.

The guards in the hall saw them come up out of the floor, and saw who walked among them: a small naked old man wrapped in an altar cloth, with a withered right hand. The guards looked at their master. Their master looked back at them. Then the guards put down their spears.

By evening, it was all over Paros.

The Goldhand had given everything away. He had stood on the steps of his palace in the altar cloth and told the whole town, in a voice that shook, that he had bowed to the Sea-Dragon and given it his son. He had told them that the fog was his doing, and the tithe, and the Mint. He had said that half of all he owned would go to the poor of the islands, and that anyone he had cheated could come to the palace and be paid back four times over. Then he had sent men up the tower with hammers, and they had knocked the golden serpent off its globe, and it had fallen and struck the terrace below and broken into a thousand pieces.

The people had stood in the square and watched it fall. Nobody cheered. Most of them looked frightened. But when the sun went down, Stephanos, standing on the palace terrace, saw lights coming on in the white town below: small lights, one by one, in windows, in doorways. Lamps. And a woman in the square below was prying the serpent coin off her door with a kitchen knife.

"Wavering," said Theophanes, beside him. He had his book under his arm. He had not let go of it since the Mint. "That's what the Chamber would call it now. The Waters. Not Returned. Not yet. But not Bowed." He looked down at the lamps. "It won't hold unless someone fills the house. The Goldhand's gold will buy bread for a year. After that..."

"After that, they'll need churches," said Stephanos. "And priests. And someone to keep the lamps lit."

"That's what I mean."

Behind them, in the hall, the old man sat on the floor by the dead fire with the altar cloth around his shoulders, and the Goldhand's steward knelt beside him, weeping. The factors were counting out gold on the long table into piles for the poor. The Venetian merchant had gone. So had the bishop with the serpent coin.

At the far end of the hall, rolled up on the marble floor where the servants had dropped it that morning, lay the red eagle of Taron.

The old man had given it to Stephanos an hour ago. He had not said anything. He had pointed at it with his withered hand and then at Stephanos, and then looked away.

Stephanos had not picked it up yet.

He looked at it now across the hall. The gold thread of the eagle caught the lamplight. The torn corner with his own bad stitches lay folded over.

*It's only a flag,* Dora had said.

He went and picked it up. He rolled it carefully, the way it should be rolled, and tied it with a cord, and put it under his arm.

Across the hall, Dora was sitting with Petros and the other Thracian boys by the fire, sharing bread. She looked up and saw what he was carrying. She did not say anything. She looked at him for a moment, and then went back to her bread.

He did not know why that bothered him more than if she had.
