## Chapter 8: The Goldhand's Harbour

*No one can serve two masters. You cannot serve God and money.*

*The Gospel according to Matthew*

---

The Goldhand's harbour on Paros was the most beautiful place Stephanos had ever seen, and he hated it within an hour.

It lay in a deep bay on the island's western side, sheltered by two long arms of rock, and the water inside was so clear that from the deck of Loukas's fishing boat he could see the white sand on the bottom thirty feet down, and the shadows of fish moving over it. The town climbed the hill above the harbour in terraces of white houses with blue doors. Every roof had a garden. Every garden had a fountain. Bougainvillea spilled over the walls in sheets of purple and scarlet, and the air smelled of jasmine and bread and money.

At the top of the hill, on the cliff above the harbour, stood the Goldhand's palace.

It was built of white marble, all of it, walls and columns and roof, and it caught the morning sun and threw it back so fiercely that it hurt to look at. Its gates were bronze plated with gold. On its highest tower, taller than any church tower Stephanos had ever seen, stood a statue of a serpent, coiled around a globe, biting its own tail. The statue was gold too. It shone like a second sun.

The quays below the palace were crowded with ships: round-bellied merchantmen from Egypt and Syria, galleys from Venice and Amalfi, grain ships and wine ships and slave ships. On every mast, Stephanos saw the tokens nailed. On some masts there were dozens.

"He takes a tithe from all of them," said Loukas at the tiller. He had brought them over from Naxos in his own boat, a small, patched fishing caique, with Ioannikios at the sail. "Every one. The whole Aegean pays him. Some of those captains have been paying for thirty years."

"And the people?" asked Kale, looking up at the white town.

"Rich," said Loukas. "Rich and afraid. Nobody on Paros goes hungry. Nobody on Paros sleeps well either." He nodded toward the waterfront. "Look at the doors."

Stephanos looked. On every door of every house along the harbour, beside the blue paint, there was a small coin nailed. On each coin, the serpent.

---

They came ashore at the fishermen's quay, at the poor end of the harbour, where the smell of fish was stronger than the jasmine. Loukas and Ioannikios stayed with the boat. The two knights had been seen on Paros too many times, Loukas said, and the Goldhand's steward knew their faces.

"He'll know mine," said Stephanos.

"He'll know the purple," said Loukas. "Everyone knows the purple. But he's never seen you. That's something."

"Take the purple off," said Theophanes.

They all looked at him. He flushed, but went on.

"Take it off. Cover the shield. Put on a sailor's coat over the lamellar. I've been working on this since Naxos." He took a folded parchment out of his satchel. "It's a writ. From the Chamber of Scribes, under Master Kallinikos's seal, instructing the Goldhand's steward to release the seven Thracian youths delivered on the *Saint Theodore* into the custody of the bearer, a factor of the Concord, for transport east. I know the Master's hand better than my own. I know the steward's name. I know the form of words. They'll take one look at it and hand the boys over."

"And where's the seal?"

Theophanes held up a small lump of carved wax. "I cut it on the boat. From memory." He almost smiled. "I told you I was the best they had."

Stephanos looked at the writ. He looked at it for a long time.

It was a good plan. It was better than anything he had. He had been lying awake on Loukas's boat trying to think of a way into the Goldhand's palace and down into the Mint without fighting through a hundred guards, and he had found none. Theophanes's writ would take them in through the front gate. It might take them straight down to the presses. It might have Petros free by noon, and all seven boys, and nobody would have to die.

He put his hand on his belt.

The bronze plates were cold under his fingers. Colder than the morning air. Colder than they had any right to be, in the sun on a Paros quay.

"No," he said.

Theophanes stared at him. "No? Why not? It'll work. I know it'll work."

"It's a lie."

"It's a writ! It's paper! It's the same thing the Master did to the Eparch and the Patriarch for two years, except this time we're doing it to the Goldhand, who is a slaver and a servant of the Sea-Dragon, to free seven boys from drowning. How is that a lie?"

"It's a forged letter in another man's hand," said Stephanos. "It says I'm a factor of the Concord. I'm not. It says I'm taking the boys east. I'm not." He took his hand off the belt. The cold stayed in his fingers. "I wear this belt, Theophanes. It says Truth on the buckle. The Abba blessed it. If I lie with it on, it goes cold, and it stays cold until I confess. And then I have nothing, in that palace, against a man whose whole power is lies. I don't have much. But I have that."

"So take it off!"

"I swore a vow."

"You swore a vow to obey the Domestikos too!" Theophanes's voice cracked. "And you broke that one easily enough!"

The words hung in the air on the fishermen's quay. Kale looked at the ground. Lazaros looked at Theophanes with his gentle, sad face. Dora, sitting on a bollard with the lamb in her lap, looked from one of them to the other with her head on one side.

Stephanos felt the heat rise in his chest. He held it down.

"Yes," he said. "I did. And I'll answer for it. But I won't add to it."

Theophanes opened his mouth, and shut it, and turned away. He stood with his back to them, looking up at the white palace on the cliff, with the writ crumpled in his fist.

"Then how?" he said. "How do we get in?"

It was Amma Thekla who answered. She had been sitting on an upturned basket by the nets, weaving, all this time, and nobody had noticed her. She did not look up.

"We ask," she said.

---

So they went up to the Goldhand's palace, all seven of them, by the main road, in the full light of the morning, and Stephanos wore the purple on his shield.

People stared at them as they climbed through the white town. Women stopped at their fountains and men stopped in their doorways, and children came running out to follow them, and were called back. Nobody spoke to them. At every door, the serpent coin gleamed.

At the bronze gates, the guards crossed their spears. They were big men in gilded scale armour, Syrians by their faces, with serpent badges on their helms.

"The Tagma of the Horn," said Stephanos, "asks audience of Leon Chrysocheir."

The guards looked at each other. One of them went inside. They waited a long time in the sun. Sergios sat down in the road and began building a tower out of pebbles. Dora sat down beside him to help. Then the gate opened, and a man came out.

He was small and plump and bald, dressed in white silk with a gold chain around his neck, and he smiled at them with every one of his teeth. The Goldhand's steward, Stephanos guessed. He had the soft, pale hands of a man who had never lifted anything heavier than a pen.

"The Tagma of the Horn," the steward said. "What a pleasure. My master has long wished to meet one of your order. He is at his table now, with guests. He bids you join him." The smile grew wider. "All of you. Even the little one with the lamb."

---

The Goldhand's hall was the size of the Great Church, and it had a roof.

That was the first thing Stephanos noticed, and he noticed it with a pang that surprised him. The roof was a dome, like the Great Church's had been, gilded on the inside so that it shone like the inside of a golden bowl. Light came down through windows set around its rim and fell in long bars across the hall, and in the light, everything glittered. The floor was marble inlaid with gold. The columns were marble wrapped with gold. The walls were hung with tapestries shot through with gold thread, and on the tapestries, sea battles and harvests and hunts, all in gold and blue.

In the centre of the hall stood a table, and at the table sat forty people eating.

They were merchants, most of them, in fine silks from every corner of the sea. Stephanos saw a Venetian in black, an Egyptian in white, two Syrians, a Genoese. He saw a bishop, in purple, with a serpent coin hanging on a chain around his neck among his crosses. He saw a Concord Scribe in grey, at the far end of the table, who looked up when they came in and then looked quickly down again.

And at the head of the table, in a chair carved in the shape of a coiled serpent, sat the Goldhand.

Stephanos had pictured him many times. In nine years, he had pictured him a thousand times: a gross, fat, glittering monster, a spider in a web of gold. The man at the head of the table was none of those things. He was about fifty, lean and brown, with close-cut grey hair and a short grey beard and a plain, pleasant, clever face, the face of a successful sea captain. He wore a tunic of dark blue wool with no ornament at all. His hands, resting on the table in front of him, were brown and square and strong.

His right hand was gold.

It was not a glove. It was not gilded. The whole hand, from the wrist to the fingertips, was solid, shining gold, and it moved like flesh. As Stephanos watched, the Goldhand lifted it and picked up a cup of wine with it, and drank, and set the cup down. The golden fingers flexed.

"Stephanos of Taron," said the Goldhand. His voice was warm and courteous, with an island accent. "Or Stephanos of the Horn, as you call yourself now. Welcome. Sit. Eat. You look as if you've come a long way."

Stephanos did not move.

"You know my name," he said.

"I know the name of every man who ever walked away from my gold," said the Goldhand. "There aren't many. You were the only one from Taron. I asked your count about you, at the time. He said you were proud and stubborn and the best lance in the east, and that you'd be back within a month, begging. You never came back." The Goldhand smiled. "I respect that. Sit."

"I'm here for the seven Thracian boys your steward took off the *Saint Theodore* two weeks ago," said Stephanos. "And for the crew of the Eparch's grain ship. And for every man you have chained to your presses in the Mint below this palace."

The table went quiet. Forty faces turned toward him. The bishop with the serpent coin set down his spoon.

The Goldhand did not stop smiling.

"Is that all?" he said. "Well. We'll come to that. First, I want to show you something." He lifted his golden hand and snapped the fingers, and it made a sound like a bell. "Bring it."

Two servants came from the back of the hall carrying a long bundle between them. They unrolled it on the floor in front of the table, in a bar of sunlight, and stood back.

It was a banner. Red silk, faded and stained, with a gold fringe that had lost half its threads, and in the middle of it, worked in gold thread gone dull with age, an eagle with its wings spread. There was a tear across one corner. Stephanos knew that tear. He had been holding the banner when the arrow went through it at the Halys, eleven years ago, and he had sewn it up himself that night by the fire, badly, with a sailmaker's needle, and the stitches were still there.

The red eagle of Taron.

He could not breathe.

"Your count sold it to me," said the Goldhand gently. "With the men and the horses. The men I've used. The horses I've used. The banner I've kept. I never flew it. I don't know why. I suppose I was waiting for you." He leaned forward. "It's yours, Stephanos of Taron. Take it. Take it home to Taron. Raise the men who remember it, and ride under it again, against the giants. I'll pay them. I'll arm them. I'll give you ships to carry them anywhere you like. You'll be what you were born to be, and not a beggar-knight laying stones for a City with no roof."

The whole hall was silent. The bars of light lay across the marble. The eagle lay in the light, its gold threads glittering.

"What do you want for it?" said Stephanos. His voice sounded strange to him, far away.

"Nothing much. Your service. The same as your count gave me. You'd ride for me, and I'd pay you better than any lord on earth." The Goldhand spread his hands, the brown one and the gold one. "And I'd want you to leave the Mint alone. It's a business matter. You wouldn't understand it."

Stephanos looked at the banner.

He thought about his cousin Vahan, holding it up at Dorylaion with an arrow through his throat. He thought about the courtyard at Kaisareia, and the chest, and the nails. He thought about nine years of stones and nine years of silence and nine years of the Question with no answer.

He took a step toward it.

His belt was cold. It was so cold that he could feel it through his tunic and his mail, a ring of ice around his waist. He stopped.

Behind him, Dora said clearly, "It's only a flag."

Everyone turned to look at her. She was standing just inside the door with the lamb in her arms, and her face was red, but she did not look away.

"It's only a flag," she said again. "It's cloth. You told me on the ship. You said your cousin died holding it up. He didn't die for the cloth. He died for the men behind it." She looked at the Goldhand. "You bought the men. You bought the horses. You can't sell the flag back to him and pretend it's the same thing. The things it meant are gone. You spent them."

The Goldhand looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind his pleasant, clever face, something that was not pleasant at all.

"Who is this child?" he said softly.

"My name is Dora," she said. "You bought my brother."

And then everything happened at once.

---

It was the Concord Scribe at the end of the table who moved first. He was on his feet before Dora had finished speaking, pointing at her and shouting, "That's her! That's the sister! Master Kallinikos's letter said... Take her! My lord, take her!"

The Goldhand lifted his golden hand. The guards along the walls came off them in a rush.

Stephanos drew his sword. He did not think. He went backward, three long steps, until he stood in front of Dora with his shield up, and the first guard's spear struck it and skidded off. Kale was beside him with an arrow on the string. Lazaros had the icon out of its case, holding it up in both scarred hands. The merchants were scrambling back from the table, overturning chairs, and the bishop had fallen off his bench.

"Out!" Stephanos shouted. "The door! Go!"

But the door was full of guards.

He counted twenty of them, in gilded scale, with spears and round shields, coming in a wall across the marble floor. Behind him there were more. He heard Theophanes cry out and turned his head and saw a guard's spear-butt catch the scribe across the side of the head, and Theophanes went down.

Then a voice said, quite loud and cheerful: "Oh, look! Look at all this lovely money!"

It was Sergios. He had climbed onto the Goldhand's table, in his rags and his bare filthy feet, and he was walking down it among the dishes and the cups, kicking them aside. He had his arms full of gold. Cups and plates and the Goldhand's golden salt-cellar and a heap of coins from somebody's purse. And as the guards stopped, staring, the fool began to throw it.

He threw it at the guards. Not hard. He lobbed it, underhand, like a man throwing bread to ducks: cups, plates, coins, a golden pepper-pot, a jewelled knife.

"Here!" he shouted. "Have it! Have it all! It's yours! Take it! I don't want it! Nobody wants it! It's only money!"

A coin struck a guard's helm and bounced. Another guard caught a golden cup against his chest, by reflex, and stood looking at it. A third stooped to pick up a coin from the floor, and then a fourth, and then the wall of spears had broken into a crowd of men scrabbling on the marble for the gold that the fool was raining down on them.

"Stop!" the Goldhand was shouting. "Stop it! Leave it! Take them!"

Nobody was listening to him. Sergios had reached the head of the table. He stood over the Goldhand's serpent chair, looking down at the Goldhand with his mad, clear, sad eyes, and the Goldhand looked up at him.

"You poor thing," said Sergios. "You poor, poor thing. You don't have anything at all, do you?"

The Goldhand struck him with the golden hand.

It was a hard blow, full in the chest, and it threw the fool clean off the table. He hit the marble floor on his back and slid, and lay still.

"Sergios!" Dora screamed.

The hall had gone silent again. The guards stood frozen with gold in their hands. The merchants stood against the walls. The Goldhand stood beside his chair, breathing hard, and looked down at his golden hand as if it belonged to someone else.

Then Sergios sat up. He coughed. He rubbed his chest, and looked at the Goldhand, and laughed.

"That hurt," he said. "That's the first honest thing you've done all day."

---

They did not get out through the door. The door was still full of guards, and the guards had put away the gold and remembered their orders.

They got out through the floor.

It was Kale who found it. She had backed against the wall at the side of the hall, behind a column, with her bow drawn and her eyes on the guards, and suddenly she said, "Here. Stephanos. Here. There's a stair under this."

He did not ask how she knew. He put his heel against the marble slab she was standing on, and it moved. It had been cut to move. It slid aside with a grinding sound, and under it was darkness and a smell of salt water and a stair going down.

"The old church," said Amma Thekla. She was beside them. Stephanos had not seen her move. "The Panagia. This was its crypt. The Goldhand built his hall on top of it." She looked down the stair. "It goes to the sea caves. And the sea caves go to the Mint."

"Go," said Stephanos. "All of you. Go!"

They went. Lazaros first, with the icon held up before him like a lamp. Then Amma Thekla. Then Kale, dragging Theophanes, who was on his feet but stumbling, with blood running down the side of his face. Then Sergios, limping, holding his chest and laughing.

Dora stopped at the top of the stair. She looked back across the hall.

"Dora," Stephanos said. "Go."

"The lamb," she said. "I dropped Poimen. He's under the table."

He looked. The lamb was under the Goldhand's table, among the fallen cups and plates, bleating. Between the table and the stair were twenty guards with spears.

"Leave him," Stephanos said.

"He's the youngest," said Dora. "He's always getting lost."

She went past Stephanos before he could catch her. She went across the marble floor of the Goldhand's hall, small and fast and barefoot, straight through the middle of the guards. They were so surprised that not one of them moved. She dived under the table and came out with the lamb in her arms and turned and ran back, and one of the guards came to himself at last and grabbed at her hair and got a handful of red wool ribbon and nothing else.

She reached the stair. Stephanos pushed her down it ahead of him and turned and struck the first guard who came after them, and the guard went back into the others, and Stephanos went down into the dark and dragged the slab across over his head.

It ground shut. Above them, muffled through the marble, he heard shouting, and the Goldhand's voice over it all, high and furious, calling for the steward, calling for the seer, calling for the water to rise.

Then the stair under his feet began to shake.

---

They stood together at the bottom of the stair in pitch darkness, breathing hard, and listened.

Water. The sound of it came from all around them, deep and slow, like breathing. The air was cold and wet and tasted of salt. Somewhere ahead, far off, there was a sound Stephanos could not place at first, a steady, heavy, rhythmic thud, like a giant's heart beating.

Then he knew what it was. He had heard it once in Kaisareia, in the count's mint. It was the sound of a coin press, a great iron weight lifted and dropped, lifted and dropped, striking coins.

Many coin presses. Hundreds of them, beating in time, somewhere below them in the dark.

"Lazaros," said Stephanos. "Light."

There was a scrape and a flicker, and the painter's lamp caught. In its small yellow circle, Stephanos saw their faces. Theophanes, pale, with blood on his cheek. Kale, grim. The Amma, calm. Sergios, grinning, with his hand pressed to his chest. Lazaros, with the icon of the Archistrategos under his arm.

And Dora, holding the lamb, looking up at him.

"That was very stupid," he told her.

"Yes," she said. "Like jumping off a scaffold."

He stared at her. "Who told you about that?"

"Kale." She shifted the lamb in her arms. "She said it was the stupidest thing she'd ever seen, and it saved a boy's life, and that's why she followed you to Thrace." She looked past him, into the dark, toward the sound of the presses. "Petros is down there. I can feel it. He's down there working, and he's angry, and he's frightened, and he's too proud to cry."

The presses thudded in the dark. The water breathed.

Stephanos took the lamp from Lazaros, and held it up, and led them down.
