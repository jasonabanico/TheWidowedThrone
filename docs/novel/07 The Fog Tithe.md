## Chapter 7: The Fog Tithe

*Forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors.*

*The Lord's Prayer*

---

The ship was called the *Saint Phokas*, and her captain was a Lemnian named Zenon who had paid the Goldhand's tithe for eleven years and hated himself a little more each time.

"One nomisma in ten," he told Stephanos on the first night out from Rhaidestos, leaning on the stern rail with a cup of wine. The Marmara was flat and black and full of stars. "Every cargo. Every voyage. You pay at the customs house on Paros, or you pay to one of his factors in any port from Thessalonike to Rhodes, and they give you a little token, a coin with the serpent on it, and you nail it to your mast. And the fog lets you pass." He jerked his chin upward. Stephanos looked. Nailed to the mainmast, at the height of a man's head, were eleven small coins, blackened with salt. On each of them, in low relief, a serpent coiled around itself and bit its own tail.

"And if you don't pay?"

"Then you don't come home." Zenon drank. "The Eparch's grain ship, the *Theotokos*, sailed from Alexandria in the summer with a full hold for the City. Her captain was a pious man. Fotios. I knew him. He said he'd pay no tithe to a serpent. He took down his tokens and threw them in the harbour at Alexandria and sailed north." He was quiet a moment. "She went into the fog off Naxos. Nobody's seen her since. They say the crew turned up on the block at Paros a month later, sold for debts. The ship's debts. The fog's debts." He spat over the rail. "That's the tithe. You pay it in coin, or you pay it in flesh."

"You've paid it."

"Eleven years." Zenon looked at him sidelong. "Do you despise me for it, Hornbearer?"

Stephanos thought about his count, and the courtyard at Kaisareia, and the banner going into the chest. He thought about the eleven coins on the mast.

"No," he said. "I think you have a wife and children on Lemnos."

"Four children," said Zenon. "And a mother-in-law." He finished his wine. "Pray for me, if you pray. I'm told you people do it all the time."

---

They passed out of the Marmara through the Hellespont on the third day, and on the fourth they came into the Aegean, and the sea changed.

It was a small thing at first. The water lost its dark Marmara green and turned a flat grey, like pewter. The wind dropped. Birds that had followed the ship all the way from Rhaidestos turned back one by one and flew north, until the sky was empty. Then Stephanos, leaning over the side to watch the bow wave, saw something on the hull below the waterline, and called Kale over to look.

Coins. They were growing on the planks among the weed and the barnacles, in clusters, like mussels on a rock. Some were no bigger than a fingernail. Some were the size of a man's palm. They were gold and silver and copper, dull under the water, and as the ship rolled, he could see them catching the light. On every one of them, the serpent.

"They weren't there yesterday," said Kale.

"No."

Zenon, when they showed him, only shrugged. "The Blight," he said. "It gets worse every year. My brother-in-law scraped his hull at Mytilene in the spring and got forty nomismata off it. He was pleased for a week. Then his daughter took a fever and died." He turned away. "Leave them be. Don't touch them. You don't want to owe him anything."

---

That night, Stephanos found Dora on the foredeck, practising with her sling.

She had set up a row of empty wine jars along the rail at the bow and was standing at the foot of the mast, twenty paces off, whirling the sling in a lazy loop over her head. The lamb was asleep in a coil of rope beside her. The lantern on the mast threw her shadow across the deck, long and thin, and the sling hissed.

She let fly. The stone cracked into the nearest jar and burst it, and pieces of pottery went over the side and splashed.

"Zenon will want those back," Stephanos said.

"I'll pay him." She fitted another stone. "Petros taught me. When I was six. He said a shepherd who can't use a sling is just a person standing in a field with some sheep."

She whirled and let fly. The second jar burst.

"Show me how you hold it," she said.

"I know how to hold a sling."

"You held it wrong at the ford. I saw. You had the cord wrapped round your middle finger. That makes the release late, so it goes low." She held out her hand. "Show me."

He hesitated. Then, because there was nobody else on the foredeck and because the night was very dark and quiet, he took his sling off his belt and showed her.

She looked at it, and at his hand, and made a face.

"The loop goes on your ring finger," she said. "Not the middle. And you don't grip the other end. You pinch it, like this, between your thumb and the side of your finger. Like you're holding a feather." She took his hand in both of hers and moved his fingers, the way a mother moves a baby's hand to make it wave. Her hands were small and rough, calloused across the palms. "There. Now throw."

He threw. The stone went a little high and a little right and cracked the rail beside the third jar.

"Better," said Dora. "Do it again."

He did it again. It went low and struck the deck.

"You're angry," she said. "You're thinking about it. Stop thinking about it."

"I'm not angry."

"Yes, you are. You're always angry." She sat down on the coil of rope beside the lamb and looked up at him. "Why?"

He did not answer. He fitted another stone to the sling and whirled it and threw, and missed.

"Petros is angry sometimes," Dora said. "When he loses a wrestling match, or when a girl laughs at him. But he gets over it by supper. Yours doesn't go away. It sits inside you all the time, like a stone in a boot." She stroked the lamb's head. "Is it the Goldhand? Kale said something about the Goldhand. About your count."

He was quiet for so long that she stopped waiting and started to hum, a shepherd's tune, over and over.

"I carried a banner," he said at last. "For six years. The red eagle of Taron. My grandfather carried it before me, and his father before him. We rode under it against the Persians and the Arabs and the Gibborim. Men died for it. My cousin died for it at Dorylaion, holding it up with an arrow through his throat." He looked at the sling in his hand. "Nine years ago, my count sold it. He sold it to the Goldhand, and the men who rode under it, and their horses, for twenty thousand nomismata and a palace on Chios. He stood in the courtyard and told us, and he smiled while he said it, as if it were good news. We'd get paid better, he said. The Goldhand paid well."

Dora had stopped humming.

"I laid my colours on the ground and walked away," he said. "Two months to the City, on foot most of the way. I've been in the Tagma ever since. And every day, Dora. Every day I lay stones on the wall or ride the roads or ask the Question at some shrine where nobody answers, I think about that banner rolled up in a chest in the Goldhand's treasury, and I think about my count's face when he said it. That's why."

The girl was quiet a moment. The ship creaked. Somewhere below, one of the sailors laughed at something.

"So you wanted to be bought for more?" she said.

He turned and stared at her.

"I don't mean it badly," said Dora. She looked puzzled. "I only mean, you're angry because your count sold you. But if he'd sold you to someone good, would you still be angry? If he'd sold you to the Eparch, or to your Abba?"

"He had no right to sell us at all."

"No," she agreed. "But you let him sell the banner. You didn't take it and run. You walked away and left it in the chest. And you've been angry at him ever since, but I think you're angry at yourself too, for leaving it." She picked up her sling. "Grandmother said that when you're angry at someone for a long time, you're mostly angry at yourself. She said it's the same stone, only you're throwing it in two directions."

She whirled her sling and let fly. The third jar burst.

Stephanos stood on the foredeck with his sling in his hand and did not say anything. After a while Dora picked up the lamb and went below, and he stayed there alone, looking out at the flat grey sea, until the stars began to fade.

---

The fog came on the fifth morning, off Naxos.

It came up out of the south like a wall. One moment the sea was grey and flat and empty to the horizon, with the dark shapes of the islands floating on it in the distance. The next, a long white bank was rolling toward them across the water, fast, faster than any fog Stephanos had ever seen, higher than the masthead. The sun went pale behind it. Then it was on them.

The world disappeared. Stephanos could not see the bow from the stern. He could not see the sea over the side. He could barely see his own hand. The fog was thick and wet and cold, and it smelled of salt and old metal, like the inside of a moneybox.

"Hold her steady!" Zenon was shouting somewhere forward. "Steady! Nobody touch the sails!"

And then the whispering began.

It came from all around them, from the fog itself. It was soft, like the murmur of a crowd in another room, and at first Stephanos could not make out the words. Then he could.

*You gave your word.*

He turned. There was no one there. The fog pressed against his face.

*You gave your word to the Domestikos. In front of the chapter. Yes, Domestikos, you said. And then you rode away in the night like a thief. You owe him an oath. You owe the Tagma an oath. You owe your count an oath, too; you swore to him before God and you broke it. How many oaths do you owe, Stephanos of Taron? How many debts? Shall I count them for you?*

He drew his sword. It was absurd. There was nothing to cut. The voice went on, patient and reasonable, like a moneylender going over his books.

*You owe the Abba for nine years of bread. You owe Gregoras for the saddle and the oats. You owe the girl's brother, whom you will fail. You owe the children of the Sangarios, whom you left to Anak. You owe your cousin Vahan, who died holding the banner that you let go. Pay, Stephanos. Pay, and the fog will let you pass. Pay, or you will be sold for your debts, like all the others.*

He heard, through the fog, the others. Their voices were very faint and far away, as if they were on another ship.

Theophanes was saying, over and over: "I know. I know. I know I did. I know."

Lazaros was weeping.

Kale's voice, hard and flat: "I couldn't. There were too many. I couldn't save them. I couldn't."

And somewhere below, from the hold, the voices of the sailors, crying out, begging, making promises. One of them was screaming that he would pay, he would pay anything, only let him go.

Stephanos felt something touch his arm. He looked down. A hand had come out of the fog and closed on his wrist: a pale, wet, swollen hand with barnacles on the knuckles and a gold ring sunk deep into the flesh of one finger. It was cold as the sea. It pulled.

He cut at it. His sword went through it as if through water, and the hand did not let go.

*Pay,* whispered the fog.

"I forgive you!"

The shout rang out across the ship, loud and cracked and cheerful. The whispering faltered.

"I forgive you!" It was Sergios, somewhere forward, near the bow. "All of you! Every one! All your debts! Whatever you owe me, I forgive it! I don't want it! Keep it! Here!"

A clatter, as of something thrown onto the deck. Then the fool's voice again, singing now, a psalm, one Stephanos knew well: *Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered.*

The hand on Stephanos's wrist loosened.

He felt it hesitate. He felt it go slack, and slide away, and heard, very faintly, a sound like a sigh. The fog thinned around him for a moment, and he saw the deck at his feet, and the rail, and a dark shape in the water over the side sinking out of sight.

"Say it!" Sergios shouted. "All of you! Forgive them! Out loud! They can't stand it!"

Stephanos opened his mouth. The words stuck in his throat. He thought of his count's smiling face in the courtyard at Kaisareia.

Below him in the hold, a sailor was shouting it now, a rough Lemnian voice: "I forgive you! I forgive you! Christ have mercy, I forgive everyone!" And Zenon from the helm, hoarse: "I forgive my debtors! I forgive the Goldhand! God rot him, I forgive him!" And Dora, small and clear and not afraid, from somewhere amidships: "I forgive the man who took Petros. I forgive the giants. I forgive them. There."

The fog was thinning. Stephanos could see the mast now, and the eleven coins nailed to it, and the sail hanging slack above. He could see Kale at the rail with her bow in her hands and tears running down her face, and Lazaros on his knees by the hatch, and Theophanes beside him with both hands over his eyes. He could see Sergios on the bowsprit, straddling it like a horse, throwing things into the sea. They were coins. The fool had pulled the tithe tokens off the mast and was throwing them, one by one, into the water.

"Don't!" Zenon shouted from the helm. "Those are my tokens! We'll never get past!"

"We're already past!" called the fool. "Look! Look!"

And they were.

The fog rolled back from the ship in all directions, like a curtain drawn aside, and the sun came through. The sea was blue again, a deep, bright, brilliant blue, the blue of the Aegean in all the old songs. A mile off the port bow, green and gold in the morning light, lay the island of Naxos, with its white town climbing the hill above the harbour and a small chapel on a rock at the harbour mouth. On the chapel roof, faded but unmistakable, a purple banner flew, with a horn and a stone on it in white.

Stephanos lowered his sword. His wrist ached where the hand had held it. When he looked down, he saw the marks of the fingers on his skin, white and cold, like frostbite.

He had not said it. He had not said the words. The fog had lifted around him, but he had not said them.

He looked up and saw Dora watching him from amidships, with the lamb in her arms, and he looked away.

---

The Harbour Chapel of the Tagma on Naxos was a single room built onto a rock at the mouth of the harbour, with a bell tower barely taller than a man, a cistern for rainwater, and a stable for two horses. Two Hornbearers kept it. They had kept it for eleven years, watching the Goldhand's fleet come and go, sending reports to the City when they could find a ship that would carry them.

Brother Loukas was a small, quick man with a squint and a laugh like a donkey's bray. Brother Ioannikios was tall and silent and had one eye. They came down to the quay when the *Saint Phokas* tied up, in full armour, with their lances in their hands, and they stopped dead when they saw the purple on Stephanos's shield.

"Nobody comes through the fog," Loukas said. "Nobody. Not without tokens. We've watched it for eleven years."

"We had a fool," said Stephanos.

Loukas looked at Sergios, who was sitting on the quay dangling his feet in the water and trying to catch a fish with his hands. Then he brayed with laughter and embraced Stephanos, armour and all.

That night in the chapel, over bread and olives and a thin island wine, Loukas told them what he knew.

"The Tower's boys came through a fortnight ago," he said. "Seven of them, Thracians, on a Concord ship. We watched them unload them at Paros. They weren't put on the block with the others. The Goldhand's steward took them himself, straight off the ship, and up to the palace." He hesitated. "Then down."

"Down?"

"The Goldhand's palace sits on the harbour at Paros, on the cliff. Under the cliff are the sea caves. Under the sea caves is the Mint." Loukas's squint deepened. "That's where the serpent coins come from. They say he strikes them down there, in the dark, by the thousand. They say the presses are worked by drowned men."

"Drowned men."

"Debtors," said Ioannikios. It was the first thing he had said all evening. His voice was deep and rough. "Men who couldn't pay the tithe. Men who borrowed and couldn't pay back. The Goldhand takes them down to the Mint and chains them to the presses. They work until they drown. Then they keep working." He looked at the fire. "My brother is down there."

Nobody spoke.

"Nine years," said Ioannikios. "He borrowed to buy a boat. The fish didn't come that year. The Goldhand's men came instead." He stood up and went to the door and stood in it, looking out at the dark harbour. "We tried to go down once, Loukas and I. We got as far as the first cave. The water came up. We barely got out."

Stephanos looked at Dora. She was sitting by the fire with the lamb in her lap, very still, listening.

"Is Petros drowned?" she asked quietly.

"Two weeks," said Loukas. "No. Not yet. It takes longer than that. Months, sometimes." He looked at her, and his face softened. "Your brother?"

"Yes."

"Then there's time." Loukas turned to Stephanos. "What do you mean to do?"

"Go to Paros," said Stephanos. "Find the way down. Bring the boys out. And Brother Ioannikios's brother, and every other man on those presses, if we can."

Loukas was quiet a moment. "And the Goldhand?"

Stephanos thought about the banner in the chest. He thought about the hand in the fog with the gold ring sunk into its finger, and the voice counting his debts.

"The Goldhand," he said, "can come and stop me."

At the door, Ioannikios turned around. In the firelight, his one eye was bright.

"There's something else," said Loukas. "An old story. The monks on Paros used to tell it, before the Goldhand threw them out. They said that when the Shepherd was old and Symeon came down from his pillar for the last time, the old saint gave four words to the four themes, for safekeeping. One of them came to Paros. The monks kept it at the church of the Panagia by the harbour. Written in the niche behind a lamp, they said, where you could only read it when the lamp was lit." He shrugged. "The Goldhand looted the church thirty years ago. He took the lamps, the silver, everything. Turned it into a customs house. The niche is still there, I suppose. Under the palace. The church stood where his palace stands now." He looked at Theophanes, who had gone very still. "Is that any use to you?"

"A word of Symeon," said Theophanes slowly. "Behind a lamp. In the Waters." He looked at Stephanos, and for the first time since the Cisterns, there was something in his face besides fear. "The lame lord said four words. Four themes. That's one of them, Stephanos. It has to be."

Stephanos felt the letter against his chest, under the word Justice. He felt his heart beating against it.

"Then we'll find it," he said. "On the way down."

Across the fire, Amma Thekla had stopped weaving. She was looking at him with her black, bright eyes, and there was something in them he could not read.

"On the way down," she said. "Yes. That's where you'll find everything, I think."
