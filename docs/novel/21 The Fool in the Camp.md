# Part Four: The Anointing

## Chapter 21: The Fool in the Camp

*God chose the foolish things of the world to shame the wise; God chose the weak things of the world to shame the strong.*

*Paul, to the church at Corinth*

---

Sergios walked out of the City by the Gate of the Spring, which was shut, and barred, and held by forty men of the Eparch's watch with their spears levelled over the parapet.

He did not go through the gate. He went over the wall beside it, at a place where the old stones had shifted and left gaps a child could climb, and he climbed down the outside of it in the grey light with his rags flapping, singing a psalm. The watch on the tower saw him. One of them lifted a bow. The captain beside him put a hand on the bowman's arm and shook his head.

"It's only the Sparrow," the captain said. "Let him go. God knows where he's off to."

"He's going toward the giants."

"He's a fool," said the captain. "Fools go where they like."

---

The Hunter's camp lay across the plain before the Land Walls like a second city, dark and smoking and vast.

It had come up out of the west in the night. Sergios had watched it come from the Great Church steps, eating his onion. At sunset there had been nothing on the plain but the ruined suburbs and the burned chapel on the hill where Lord Tobias used to sit his horse and mock the builders. At dawn the plain was covered. Tents and wagons and pens of cattle and horses, and the smoke of ten thousand fires, and the great wheeled tower of the Hunter standing in the middle of it all, a mile from the Golden Gate, with the purple-cloaked giant sitting on its top like a spider at the heart of a web.

Sergios walked into it.

He did not hide. He did not creep. He walked down the middle of the widest lane between the tents in the full light of the morning, barefoot, with his beard sticking out in every direction and a string of onions around his neck, and he sang.

He sang a drinking song first, the one about the sailor and the mermaid. Then a psalm. Then a song he made up as he went along, about a giant who was so tall he couldn't see his own feet and kept tripping over sheep.

The Hunter's host looked at him.

Concord soldiers in red cloaks, sitting by their fires polishing their spears, looked up as he passed and stared and then looked at each other and shrugged and went back to their polishing. A Gibbor sitting on an upturned wagon eating a whole goat looked down at him with its small pale eyes, and chewed, and did not stop him. One of the grey long-armed things crouched on top of a tent pole and watched him go by with its head cocked like a dog's, and then yawned.

Nobody stopped him. Nobody asked who he was. He was dirty and ragged and barefoot and singing, and he was nobody, and nobody stops nobody.

He had known that since he was a boy. It was the first thing he had learned when he gave up everything and went into the streets of the City for Christ's sake, twenty years ago. People see a fool, and they look away. The world is built to keep out the strong and the dangerous and the clever. It has no gates for fools. Fools are not worth keeping out.

He sang his way through the whole camp of Nebrodes the Hunter to the foot of the great wheeled tower, and nobody touched him.

---

At the foot of the tower, a seer was waiting.

It was the same one. Sergios knew it at once, though it had a new robe now, black and covered in new writing, tight rows of little characters from the collar to the hem. It stood tall and thin in the shadow of the tower's great wheels with its hood up and its staff in its hand, and when it saw the fool coming, it did not move.

Sergios stopped singing.

"Hello," he said. "You're the one from the Cisterns. With the writing. The one who fell in the water." He looked at the robe. "Did you have to write it all out again? That must have taken ages."

The seer's hood turned toward him. Under it, the long ivory face with its black eyes looked down at him without expression.

"*Fool,*" it said.

"Yes."

"*You cannot pass.*"

"Why not?"

The seer lifted its free hand. The green fire gathered on its fingers, the way it had in the Cisterns, crackling, bright as marsh-light.

Sergios looked at it with interest.

"You know that won't work," he said. "It didn't work last time. You threw it at me and it went out. It always goes out. That's what you don't understand. You can't burn a fool, because a fool hasn't got anything to burn. I haven't got a house. I haven't got a name. I haven't got a single thing in the whole world that I'm afraid to lose." He smiled up at the seer. "Your fire eats what people are holding on to. I'm not holding on to anything. It hasn't got anything to eat."

The seer's hand stayed raised. The fire crackled. It did not throw it.

"I'm going to go past you now," said Sergios. "I'm going to go and see my friend. The little one. With the sheep. You've got her in a tent. I can smell her lamb." He took a step forward. "You can try and stop me if you like. But I don't think you'll like it."

He walked past the seer.

The seer did not stop him. It stood there with its green fire flickering uselessly on its fingers, and it watched him go by, and its beautiful ivory face showed the same look it had shown in the Cisterns: bewilderment. It did not understand him. It had spent a thousand years learning the secrets of the rebel sons of God, the names of the stars and the powers of the deep, and it did not understand one ragged man who wanted nothing.

Sergios did not look back.

---

The tent was at the foot of the tower, in its shadow, guarded by six Concord soldiers in red cloaks and two Gibborim. It was a big tent, square, of good grey canvas, the kind the Chamber of Scribes used for its travelling offices. There was a table outside the door with ink and parchment on it, and a stool.

On the stool, with the purple book in his lap, sat Theophanes.

He looked terrible. His grey robe was stained and torn. His face was the colour of the parchment on the table. He had not slept; there were dark smudges under his eyes like bruises. He was staring at the book in his lap without reading it, and his cut hands, still scarred from the palm weaving at the laura, lay open on the pages.

Sergios walked past the Concord soldiers and the Gibborim, who looked at him and did not stop him, and sat down on the ground beside the stool.

Theophanes looked down.

For a long moment he did not seem to understand what he was seeing. Then his face went white, and then red, and then white again.

"Sergios," he said. "What... how did you..."

"I walked," said Sergios. He took an onion off his string and held it out. "Want one?"

Theophanes stared at the onion. Then he stared at the fool. Then, to Sergios's surprise, he began to laugh. It was a thin, cracked, terrible laugh, the one he had had on the first night, on the floor of the Fool's Tower with the Concord knife-cut in his shoulder.

"You walked," he said. "You walked. Through the Hunter's host. Past the seer. Past the Gibborim." He put his hand over his eyes. "Of course you did."

Sergios waited.

"Did it help?" he asked after a while.

Theophanes took his hand away from his eyes.

"Did it help?" said the fool again. He said it gently, the way he had said it to the blind thing in the Cisterns, and to the coin-wights, and to the Goldhand. "What you did. The letter to the Master. Telling him where she'd be. Did it help? Is she safe now?"

Theophanes did not answer for a long time.

"The Master promised me," he said at last. His voice was a whisper. "He promised she'd be kept safe. A ward of the Chamber. Fed, clothed, taught. He said the giants would take her whatever I did, and the only question was whether it would be the giants or the Concord, and if it was the Concord, she'd live." He swallowed. "He's never lied to me. Never once in eleven years."

"And?"

"And he hasn't lied now." Theophanes looked at the tent behind him. "She's alive. She's fed. Nobody's hurt her. He's been very kind to her." He laughed again, and it broke in the middle. "He's going to take her into the Great Church when the walls fall, Sergios. Tonight. Or tomorrow. The seers say tonight. He's going to take her to the crack in the sanctuary floor, and make her go down, because the least of the Shepherd's house has to go down, and she's the least. And when the Horn comes up in her hands, the Hunter is going to take it from her and pour it on his own head in front of the whole City. In the Great Church. Under the dome. Where the proud one stood."

Sergios said nothing.

"That's the Concord's dream," said Theophanes. "One empire. One tongue. One law. The Tower and the City joined. The Hunter anointed with the Shepherd's oil, and all the old quarrels ended, and peace forever." His voice cracked. "And the Master believes it. He really believes it. He thinks he's saving the world. He thinks he's saving her, too; he'll keep her safe in a library in Nebrodia for the rest of her life, and teach her to read Aramaic, and she'll never be hungry." He put his face in his hands. "And I believed it too. I thought: better a library than a giant's cookpot. I thought I was choosing the lesser evil. I thought I was being wise. I thought..."

"You thought you were saving her," said Sergios.

"Yes."

"You thought you were the only one who could."

Theophanes lifted his head.

"That's what he said to you," said the fool. "The Master. In the letter. *You can save her, Theophanes. You're the only one who can.* Wasn't it?"

Theophanes stared at him. "How do you know that?"

"Because it's what the other one says to everybody," said Sergios. "The one with no shadow. He said it to the Emperor. He said it to Petros. He said it to the Hornbearer, in his way. He says it to everyone. *You're the only one who can. It's all up to you. If you don't do it, nobody will.*" The fool shrugged. "It's his best trick. It works on the good ones better than the bad. The bad ones don't care who saves anybody."

Theophanes was silent.

"You wanted to save her," said Sergios. "That was good. That was the good part. You picked the wrong way, that's all. Like the Hornbearer did. Like the Emperor did." He held out the onion again. "Everybody picks the wrong way sometimes. The trick is to pick another one."

Theophanes took the onion. He held it in his cut hands and looked at it.

"What other way?" he said. "There isn't one. They've got her. They've got her in chains in a tent in the middle of the Hunter's host. There are forty thousand men and three hundred giants and a dozen seers between her and the City. There's no other way."

"There's me," said Sergios.

---

The inside of the tent was lit by a single hanging lamp, and it smelled of ink and wax and sheep.

Dora was sitting on a pallet against the far wall, with a chain around her ankle running to an iron ring driven deep into the ground. She was still wearing the veil of the Myrrh-bearer. The lamb, Poimen, was asleep in her lap. When the tent flap opened and the fool came in, she looked up, and her face did not change for a long moment.

Then she put the lamb down very carefully on the pallet, and held out both her arms, and Sergios went and knelt beside her and she put her arms around his neck and held on to him so tightly that he could not breathe, and she did not make a sound.

"Sparrow," she said into his shoulder at last. "Sparrow. You came."

"I walked," said Sergios.

"How?"

"Nobody stops nobody." He held her away from him and looked at her. She was thin and white and there were marks on her wrists where they had tied her before they chained her, but she was not hurt. Her eyes were clear. "Are you all right?"

"I'm all right. They didn't hurt me. The Master Scribe is very polite." Dora's voice was flat. "He brought me figs. He talked to me for an hour last night about the Concord and how it's going to end all wars. He wanted me to understand. He said he'd teach me to read Aramaic." She paused. "I told him I already read Greek, and the first thing I'd read in Aramaic would be the Gospel, and he looked sad."

"Where's the sling?"

"Here." She pulled up the sleeve of her dress. The sling of Poimen was wound around her wrist under it, the old cream-and-grey wool close against her skin. "They didn't take it. They didn't notice it. The Master Scribe looked right at it and didn't see it. He thought it was a bit of string."

"Of course he did," said Sergios.

"Is Stephanos...?"

"Alive. He came to the Tower in the night. The Abba died in his arms." The fool paused. "And then he went to the Great Church, and he met someone there."

Dora looked at him for a long moment. Something moved in her face, far down.

"The lion?" she whispered.

"The lion."

She closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet.

"Petros," she said. "Sparrow, they've got Petros too. In the next tent. He's hurt. He was at the bridge. The seers wanted to kill him, but the Master said no, he might be useful." Her voice cracked. "He's not himself, Sparrow. He hasn't said a word since they brought him in. He just sits there."

"Then we'll get him too," said Sergios.

"How? There are guards. There are giants. There's the chain." She pulled at it. It was thick iron, the links as big as her fist, and the ring in the ground did not move. "The seer has the key. The tall one, with the writing."

Sergios looked at the chain. He looked at it for a long time with his head on one side.

Then he laughed.

---

Theophanes went out of the tent a quarter of an hour later and walked across the camp to the foot of the Hunter's tower, where the seer was standing in the shadow of the great wheels.

He walked slowly. He was shaking so badly that he had to stop twice and put his hand on a wagon to steady himself. He had the purple book under his arm.

The seer turned its hooded head toward him as he came.

"*Reader,*" it said. Its voice was contemptuous. "*Kallinikos's little scribe. The one who ran away and came crawling back. What do you want?*"

Theophanes stopped in front of it. He did not look at its face. He looked at its robe. At the writing.

The tight rows of little characters ran down the black cloth from the collar to the hem, thousands of them, in a script that was not Greek and not Aramaic and not Syriac and not anything a man had ever written. It was the script of the Nephilim, the stolen writing of the rebel sons of God, the letters they had taught their giant children in the days before the Flood. Theophanes had seen it before, in the Chamber. He had never been allowed to read it. Only the seers read it. The Master said that a man who read it without the seers' training would go mad.

He had been reading it for the last quarter of an hour from inside the tent flap. He was not mad. He was only very frightened.

He had understood something at the laura of Saint Chariton, weaving palm at noon with his hands bleeding. He had understood that the gift the Grammatikoi called Interpretation was not a trick of the mind. It was not cleverness. He had always thought it was cleverness; he had always been clever. But in the salt city, on his knees, with the salt creeping up his legs and the Amma's holy water running down his face, he had understood that it was a gift. It was given. It came from outside, from the same place the Amma's discernment came from, and Kale's knowledge, and the fool's wisdom. And a gift that is given can be given again.

He opened his mouth, and began to read the seer's robe aloud.

"*I am the lord of the eighth star,*" he read. His voice shook. "*I am the son of the son of Shemihazah, who descended on Hermon. I bind the dead with their own pride. I burn the living with their own hunger. My name is...*"

The seer screamed.

It was the same terrible, high, inhuman scream it had given in the Cisterns when the holy water struck it. It staggered back against the great wheel of the tower. Smoke was rising from its robe, from the writing, from every line Theophanes had read aloud. The characters were blackening, curling, burning away like paper in a fire.

"*Stop!*" it shrieked. "*Stop! You have no right! You were not taught! Stop!*"

"*My name is Kokabiel,*" read Theophanes, "*and I am afraid.*"

He had not expected that line. He read it before he understood it. It was written very small, at the hem of the robe, on the inside, where no one would ever see it: a single row of characters, tight and cramped, like a secret.

The seer stopped screaming. It stood against the wheel with the smoke rising from its robe, and looked at him with its black eyes, and for a moment it did not look contemptuous or beautiful or terrible. It looked like a thing that had been caught.

Theophanes lifted his head and met its eyes for the first time.

"You're afraid," he said. His voice was steady now. "All of you. That's what's written at the bottom of everything. I read it. You've been afraid since the Flood."

The seer's hand went to its belt. There was a ring of iron keys hanging there. It did not seem to know it had moved. Its fingers closed on the keys.

And a hand came out from under the great wheel of the tower, behind it, a thin grimy hand with long dirty fingernails, and took the ring of keys from its belt as neatly as a man picks an apple.

The seer did not notice. It was staring at Theophanes.

Under the wheel, Sergios put the keys in his rags and crawled away on his belly through the mud, singing under his breath.

---

It was the strangest escape anyone ever made from the camp of Nebrodes the Hunter, and afterward the Chamber of Scribes could not agree how it had happened, and in the end they wrote it down as a mystery.

Nobody saw the fool unlock the girl's chain. The Concord soldiers outside the tent swore afterward that no one had gone in and no one had come out, except the fool, who had come out once and gone back in with a jar of water. They had not stopped him. Why would they stop a fool?

Nobody saw the fool unlock the chain in the next tent, where the tall young man from Arkadiopolis sat against the pole with his head bowed and his hands open in his lap. Nobody saw the tall young man look up at the fool and at the girl beside him, and his face crumple like a child's. Nobody saw him get to his feet.

Everybody saw what happened next.

A Gibbor had come round the side of the tent with its axe on its shoulder to see why the fool was taking so long with the water. It found the tent flap open, and the chain on the ground, and the girl in the veil standing in the doorway with a lamb in her arms. It opened its mouth to roar.

Petros hit it.

He was unarmed. His gilded armour was gone; he was in a shirt and leggings, filthy, bloodstained, with a bandage around his head. He hit the Gibbor under the jaw with his fist, with everything he had, the way he had hit Stephanos in the Garden Below, and the Gibbor's head snapped back, and it sat down in the mud with a surprised grunt. Petros picked up its axe.

"Run," he said to his sister.

"Petros..."

"Run, Dora! Go with the Sparrow! Run!" He turned, with the axe in his hands, as the Concord soldiers came round the tent, and the second Gibbor behind them. "I'll hold them. Go!"

Dora did not run. She stood in the tent door with the lamb in her arms and looked at her brother's back, broad and bloody, planted in the mud between her and the Hunter's host with a giant's axe in his hands.

Then she put the lamb down, and pulled the sling of Poimen off her wrist, and put the loop on her ring finger.

She picked up a stone from the mud of the camp. She whirled the sling once, fast and short, and let fly.

The stone struck the second Gibbor in the eye. It roared and clapped its hand to its face and staggered sideways into the Concord soldiers, and knocked three of them down. Dora picked up another stone. The stone struck the fourth soldier on the temple, under the rim of his helm, and he dropped.

"Dora!" shouted Petros.

"I'm not leaving you," said Dora. She picked up another stone. "I did the milking for you for six years. I'm not leaving you in the mud now."

---

They came through the Hunter's host in the middle of the morning, the four of them: a tall young man with a giant's axe, a small girl with a sling, a fool with a lamb in his arms, and a scribe with a purple book.

They should not have lived. Every soldier in the camp who saw them afterward agreed on that. There were forty thousand men and three hundred giants and a dozen seers between the tent at the foot of the Hunter's tower and the walls of the City, and the alarm had gone up the moment the first Gibbor went down in the mud. Drums were beating. Horns were blowing. The grey long-armed things were coming out of the tents on all fours, hunting.

But nobody could quite decide who was chasing them.

The fool went first, and wherever the fool went, the host fell into confusion. He ran straight into a pen of the Hunter's cattle and opened the gate, and a hundred panicked oxen came out into the lanes of the camp bellowing, and scattered a company of the Iron Host like chaff. He ran through a Gibbor's cookfire and kicked the pot over, and the Gibbor roared and chased him and ran into another Gibbor, and they fought. He climbed a wagon and stood on top of it and threw onions at a seer, shouting, "It's written on your underwear! It says you're afraid! Everyone! Look! The seer's afraid!" and the seer's green fire went out on its fingers, and the Concord soldiers around it looked at each other and began, uneasily, to laugh.

Petros came behind the fool with the axe. He did not boast. He did not shout. He fought the way a shepherd fights a wolf, grimly, in silence, putting himself between his sister and whatever came at her. He was hurt and he was tired and he had not eaten in two days, and he was still the strongest man Theophanes had ever seen, and nothing that came at Dora got past him.

Dora came behind her brother with the sling of Poimen. She did not miss. Not once. She did not seem to aim. She picked up stones from the mud and the camp's lanes as she ran, and flicked them out of the old wool pouch underhand, without looking, and they flew, and whatever they struck went down. Giants, grey things, soldiers, a seer. A Gibbor twice her height came at her from the side with its axe raised, and she turned and let fly without stopping, and the stone went into its forehead above the eye, exactly where the stone from the ford had gone into the lead giant at Arkadiopolis, and it fell like a tree.

And Theophanes came last, with the purple book clutched to his chest. He had no weapon. He could not fight. But when the seers came, he read their robes aloud to them, one after another, and their fire went out.

They reached the edge of the camp at noon. Beyond the last tents was the ditch, and beyond the ditch the Land Walls, grey and huge and very near, their towers black with the Eparch's archers.

"There!" Theophanes gasped. "The Tower! The Fool's Tower! Look!"

Far up on the wall above the ditch, at the top of the Fool's Tower, a figure was standing on the battlement with a bow. It was a woman. She had short black hair and a scar from her ear to her mouth, and she was lowering a rope.

Behind her, on the battlement, a Hornbearer in lamellar stood with his right hand on the parapet. His hand was not bound. He was looking down at them. Even from the ditch, Dora could see his face.

She stopped.

"Dora!" said Petros. "Come on! The rope!"

She did not move. She stood at the edge of the ditch with the sling of Poimen hanging from her finger, and looked up at Stephanos on the wall. And he looked down at her. And neither of them moved for a long moment, while the Hunter's host roared behind them and the arrows of the Eparch's archers began to fall on the camp's edge like hail.

Then she ran for the rope.

---

He was waiting at the top when Kale and Lazaros hauled her over the battlement.

He did not say anything. He knelt on the stone in front of her, so that his face was level with hers. He knelt on his bad knee, and it hitched, and he did not notice.

She stood in front of him, filthy and scratched and bleeding from a cut on her cheek, with the veil of the Myrrh-bearer torn and muddy on her dark hair and the sling of Poimen wound round her wrist. She looked at him. She looked at his right hand, whole again, resting on his knee. She looked at his face.

"You were wrong," she said.

"I was wrong."

"About Petros."

"About Petros. About the stranger. About the bridge. About you." He did not look away. "About all of it. I was wrong. I'm sorry, Dora. I'm sorry."

She was quiet a moment.

"You're getting better at saying it," she said.

"I've had some practice." He paused. "I was taught."

She looked at him a long moment more, with her dark eyes. Then she stepped forward, and put her arms around his neck, and held on.

Behind her, Petros was coming over the battlement on the rope, with Sergios on his back and the lamb inside his shirt. Behind Petros, Theophanes was clinging to the rope halfway up the wall, with the purple book in his teeth, sobbing and laughing at the same time. Below them, the Hunter's host was pouring out of its camp toward the ditch in a dark roaring tide, and the war-drums were beating so loud that the stones of the Fool's Tower shook, and on his wheeled tower a mile off, Nebrodes the Hunter had risen from his throne and lifted his great black bow.

Stephanos held Dora on the battlement and did not let go.

"I'll go down with you," he said into her hair. "Wherever it is. Whatever's down there. I'll go down with you, if you'll let me."

"I know," said Dora. "That's why I came back."
