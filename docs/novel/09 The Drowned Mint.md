## Chapter 9: The Drowned Mint

*The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it.*

*The Gospel according to John*

---

The stair ended in a chapel.

It was small, no bigger than a village church, cut out of the rock beneath the cliff. The walls had been plastered once and painted, and in the lamplight Stephanos could see the ghosts of the paintings still there under the salt stains: saints in a row with their hands raised, a ship on a blue sea, the Mother of God in the curve of the apse with her arms spread wide. The floor was wet. Water stood in the hollows, black and still, and the air smelled of salt and rot.

"The crypt of the Panagia," said Amma Thekla. "Where the monks were buried. Before."

There were no monks now. The tomb niches along the walls were empty, the bones gone. Someone had taken them out and thrown them somewhere and used the niches to store things. Stephanos lifted the lamp and saw chests, iron-bound, stacked three high. He saw sacks. He saw candlesticks and chalices and censers of silver and gold, heaped on the floor in the corner as carelessly as firewood. He saw a bishop's crozier with the jewels pried out of it. He saw icons, dozens of them, stacked against the wall face inward, like pictures turned to the wall in a house of mourning.

Lazaros made a sound in his throat and went to them. He knelt, and turned one around, and looked at it in the light of his own lamp. It was a Christ, very old, the gold almost gone. Someone had scraped the eyes out with a knife.

"He robbed the churches," said Lazaros. His voice shook. "All of them. All over the islands. This is where he keeps it."

"He doesn't keep it," said Theophanes. He was leaning against the wall with Kale's scarf pressed to the cut on his head. "He melts it down. The gold and silver, anyway. For the coins. The icons he sells to the Bowed, for their rites." He looked at the scraped-out eyes, and looked away. "The Chamber has a price list. I copied it once."

Stephanos had stopped listening. He had found the niche.

It was in the east wall of the chapel, behind where the altar had stood: a round-topped recess in the rock, about the height of a man's chest, with a stone shelf at the bottom of it. The shelf was bare. Above it, from an iron hook in the roof of the niche, hung a short length of chain with nothing on the end of it. The plaster at the back of the niche was smooth and white, whiter than the rest of the walls, and clean. No salt had touched it.

There was nothing written on it. Not a letter. Not a scratch.

"Here," he said. "This is where it was. The lamp. The word."

They gathered round. Theophanes came and ran his fingers over the plaster at the back of the niche and held the lamp close and squinted.

"Nothing," he said. "Not even the marks of a chisel. If there was ever writing here, somebody plastered over it very carefully."

"Loukas said you could only read it when the lamp was lit," said Kale.

"There's no lamp."

"Then we need one."

Lazaros had risen from beside the icons. He was looking at the heap of church silver in the corner, and his face had changed. He went to it and began to move things, carefully, lifting aside a censer, a candlestick, a paten, the way a man looks for something he has lost in a drawer. Near the bottom of the heap he stopped.

He drew out a lamp.

It was clay, the plainest clay, the kind of lamp a poor man buys in the market for a copper: a little round body with a hole in the top for the oil and a spout at the front for the wick, and a loop at the back to hold it by. It was very old. The clay had gone dark and smooth with handling. On its round belly, pressed into the clay before it was fired, was a tiny picture of a man with a crook, sitting among sheep.

"Saint Mamas," said Lazaros. "The shepherd saint. He read his psalms by lamplight in the hills of Cappadocia, and the wild beasts came and lay down to listen." He turned it over in his scarred hands, gently. "This is his lamp. There's a story about it. It was kept in a church on Naxos, and the oil never ran out. The Goldhand's men took it when they took everything else. It's worth nothing. It's clay. They must have thrown it in with the rest by mistake."

He shook it. Something sloshed inside.

"There's oil in it," he said. "After thirty years."

Nobody said anything. Stephanos found that the hair on his arms was standing up again, the way it had in the crypt of the emperors.

Lazaros carried the lamp to the niche. He hung it by its loop on the iron hook at the end of the chain. He took a twist of wick from the pouch at his belt, and fed it into the spout, and waited while it drank the oil. Then he took his own lamp and held its flame to the wick.

It caught.

It was a small flame, no bigger than a fingertip, yellow and steady. It gave very little light. But the moment it caught, the white plaster at the back of the niche changed.

Letters rose on it. They came up out of the plaster the way a bruise comes up under skin, slowly, darkening, until they were clear and black and sharp, in the old square script of the Shepherd's time. There were not many. One line.

Theophanes read it aloud. His voice was hushed.

"*Where the proud one stood, the Horn lay down.*"

They all stood looking at it. The little flame burned. The letters stood black on the white plaster.

"That's all?" said Kale.

"That's all." Theophanes read it again, silently, his lips moving. "Where the proud one stood. The proud one. That could be anyone. Nebrodes is proud. The Goldhand is proud. Every emperor of the Shepherd's house after the third was proud, if you believe the chronicles. It could be anywhere."

"It's one of four," said Amma Thekla. "One word is never enough. That's why he split it."

"Then we need the other three."

"Yes," said the old woman. "And we need to keep this one lit."

Stephanos turned to her. "Keep it lit?"

"Look at the water."

He looked down. The black water on the chapel floor, which had lain perfectly still when they came in, was moving. It was rising. It had crept up over the lowest step of the stair while they stood reading, and now, as he watched, it lapped at the next one. And in it, far below the surface, something clinked.

It was a small sound. Metal on metal, like coins in a purse.

Then another. Then many, all over the chapel, under the water, coming closer.

"The Blight doesn't like lamps," said Thekla. "It never has. A lamp lit in a place like this is a door. It lets the light in. The Goldhand's master will want it out before morning. If it burns till morning, it stays lit." She took her half-finished basket out of her rope belt and sat down on a chest, in front of the niche, facing the water. "So. We keep it lit."

"Till morning?" said Theophanes. "We don't have till morning. Petros is down there. The boys are down there. The Goldhand's calling the sea up on top of us."

"The sea is coming up," said the old woman. "It will go down again at dawn. It always does. The tide doesn't belong to the Goldhand, whatever he thinks." She began to weave. "We couldn't get down to the presses now if we wanted to. So we might as well do something useful while we wait."

The clinking grew louder. The water rose over another step.

Out in the middle of the chapel, the surface broke, and something stood up.

---

It had been a man.

It was a man still, mostly. It was thin and white and swollen, the way the drowned are, and water ran off it in streams. It wore the rags of a fisherman's tunic. Its hair hung in wet ropes over its face. And it was wrapped in chains, dozens of them, fine gold chains like the ones rich women wear around their necks, wound around and around its body from its ankles to its shoulders, and hanging from every link of every chain was a coin.

The coins clinked as it moved. It came toward them through the water with its white arms reaching, and its mouth open, and a sound came out of its mouth like water gurgling in a drain.

Behind it, all over the chapel, others were rising.

"Coin-wights," Kale breathed. "I've heard of them. Never seen one."

Stephanos drew his sword and stepped in front of the niche. "What kills them?"

"I don't know."

"I do," said Sergios.

The fool was standing at the edge of the water with his rags hitched up around his knees. He had stopped laughing. He was looking at the first wight with his clear, sad eyes, the way he had looked at the blind thing in the Cisterns.

"Remember the fog," he said. "Remember what you heard in the fog. They're the same. They're the ones in the fog. They're the debtors." He took a step into the water toward the wight. "Hello. What's your name? Do you remember your name? What do you owe? Tell me."

The wight stopped. Its drowned face turned toward him. Its mouth worked. Water ran out of it, and with the water, a word, choked and bubbling.

"*...boat...*"

"A boat," said Sergios. "You borrowed for a boat. And the fish didn't come."

"*...fish... didn't... come...*"

"No. They didn't. That wasn't your fault. The sea is the sea." The fool held out his hand, palm up. "I forgive you. Whatever you owe, I forgive it. It's gone. I tore it up."

The wight stood still in the water. Its arms, which had been reaching, dropped a little.

"Everyone," said Sergios, without turning around. "Say it. Say it to them. Out loud, the way we did on the ship. Not to the Goldhand. To them. They owe him, but he's not the only one they owe. They owe the whole world. Everyone's been counting their debts at them for years. Forgive them."

Stephanos looked at the wights rising all over the chapel, white and swollen and clinking. There were twenty of them. There were thirty. More were coming up out of the water every moment. He looked at his sword.

He thought about the fog, and the hand on his wrist, and the words he had not said.

"I forgive you," said Dora. She was standing beside the niche with the lamb in her arms, and her voice was very clear. "I forgive you. All of you. I don't know what you owe, but I forgive it."

"I forgive you," said Lazaros.

"I forgive you," said Kale, fiercely, as though she were loosing an arrow.

Theophanes said it in a whisper, and then again, louder. Amma Thekla said it without looking up from her basket, and went on weaving.

And the wights stopped.

All over the chapel, one after another, they stopped in the water and stood still, and their arms came down, and their drowned faces turned toward the little flame in the niche. The coins on their chains had stopped clinking.

The first one, the one who had borrowed for a boat, was standing in front of Sergios. As Stephanos watched, one of the gold chains around its body slipped. It slid down the white arm and fell into the water with a small splash, and was gone. Then another. Then another.

"There," said Sergios softly. "There you go. That's better, isn't it?"

The chains were falling. All of them. They slid off the wight's body one by one and sank. And as they fell, the white swollen flesh underneath them was changing, darkening, shrinking. The drowned face was becoming a face, a man's face, thin and bearded and worn, with the dazed, blinking look of someone woken suddenly from a long sleep.

The last chain fell. The man stood in the water up to his waist, shivering, in the rags of his tunic. He looked down at his own hands. Then he looked up at the fool.

"Where am I?" he said. His voice was a croak. "What... is this Paros? I was on the presses. I was on the presses, and the water came, and..."

"You're in a church," said Sergios. "You've been asleep. What's your name?"

"Demetrios," the man said. "Demetrios of Naxos." He began to cry. "My brother. I have a brother. He's a knight. He'll be looking for me."

---

They held the chapel through the night.

Not every wight woke. Some of them heard the forgiveness and stopped, and stood swaying in the water, and did not wake; their chains stayed on. Some of them did not stop at all. Those came on, reaching, clinking, and Stephanos and Kale fought them at the edge of the water with sword and arrow, and they were strong, stronger than living men, and heavy with gold. But they fell. And when they fell, they sank, and did not rise again.

Twice in the night something bigger came. It came up out of the flooded passage at the far side of the chapel, a long pale shape like the blind thing in the Cisterns but with more teeth, and it went for the niche. The first time, Kale put three arrows into it and Stephanos cut it across the snout and it went back into the dark. The second time, it came so close that its breath blew the little flame in the niche sideways, and the flame guttered, and nearly went out. Lazaros threw himself in front of the niche with the icon of the Archistrategos held up in both hands, and the thing stopped a yard from him and looked at the painted face of the angel, and turned away.

By the middle of the night, eleven men had woken. They sat together on the chests at the back of the chapel, shivering in the damp, wrapped in whatever cloth the band could find, and they talked in low voices about Naxos and Syros and Mykonos, about wives and boats and fish, and some of them wept and some of them prayed. Demetrios of Naxos sat among them with a church banner around his shoulders and stared at the lamp.

Stephanos did not sit. He stood at the edge of the water with his sword in his hand and watched the dark.

After a while, Dora came and stood beside him.

"You didn't say it," she said. "On the ship, in the fog. And again, just now. Everyone else said it. You didn't."

"I was holding a sword."

"You can say things while you're holding a sword. Kale did." She looked up at him. "Is it your count? Is that who you can't forgive?"

He did not answer. The water lapped at the step.

"It's all right," said Dora after a moment. "I couldn't forgive Petros once, for a whole year. He broke my grandmother's lamp. The one she'd had since she was a girl. He was showing off, throwing a ball in the house, and it fell and broke, and he laughed. He said it was only a lamp. I didn't speak to him for a year. Grandmother said I was being a fool, and I said I didn't care." She paused. "Then one day he brought me a new one. He'd made it himself, out of clay from the river. It was ugly and lopsided and it leaked. He'd been trying for months." She shrugged. "I still didn't forgive him. But I stopped being angry. I think that's how it starts."

He looked down at her. She was looking at the water, not at him.

"Your brother made you a lamp," he said.

"It leaked," said Dora. "It was terrible. I still have it."

---

The flame burned till morning.

Stephanos knew it was morning because the water went down. It went down all at once, with a long sucking sound, like a bath being emptied. The flooded steps came up out of it one by one, streaming. The floor of the chapel came up, wet and gleaming, strewn with gold chains and coins and weed. The wights that had not woken and had not fallen stood on it in the grey light that came down the stair from somewhere above, swaying, and then, one by one, they lay down on the wet floor and were still.

And the flame in the niche changed.

It did not grow bigger. It grew brighter. Its small yellow light turned white and then gold, and it spread, not like the light of a lamp but like the light of a window when the sun comes out from behind a cloud. It filled the niche. It spilled out of the niche and across the chapel floor. It touched the faded paintings on the walls, and the saints in their row seemed to lift their heads. It touched the Mother of God in the apse, and her spread arms seemed to open wider.

It touched the stacked icons against the wall, and the Christ with the scraped-out eyes, and in that light, for a moment, Stephanos could have sworn the eyes were there.

The words on the back of the niche burned in the light, black on gold.

*Where the proud one stood, the Horn lay down.*

"It's lit," said Amma Thekla. She put down her basket. It was finished. "It'll stay lit now. The Blight can't come in here anymore. Not in this chapel." She stood up, slowly, stiffly, with a hand on the chest. "Now we can go down."

---

The way to the presses went down from the far side of the chapel, where the pale thing had come from: a tunnel cut in the rock, sloping steeply, running with water from the ebbing tide. The sound of the presses came up it, louder with every step. Thud. Thud. Thud. The rock shook with it.

Stephanos went first, with the lamp. Kale came behind him. The others followed, and behind them, because they would not stay behind, came the eleven men who had woken in the night. Demetrios of Naxos walked at their head with a broken candlestick in his hand for a club. None of them had armour. Most of them could barely walk. They came anyway.

The tunnel opened out.

Stephanos stopped at the mouth of it, on a ledge of wet rock, and looked out at the Drowned Mint.

It was a sea cave, vast, bigger than the Cisterns of the City. Its roof was lost in darkness. Its floor was a series of great shelves of rock, stepping down and down toward a black pool at the bottom where the sea came in through some hidden channel and breathed in and out. On every shelf stood presses. They were iron, tall as a house, each one a great iron weight hung from a beam, lifted by a chain that ran over a wheel. And at every wheel, chained to it, working it, was a man.

Most of them were not men anymore. Most of them were wights, white and swollen and wound in gold, turning the wheels in the dark with their drowned arms, over and over, forever. The weights rose, and fell, and struck the dies beneath them, and at every stroke a coin sprang out and fell into a chute, and the chutes ran down and down to the black pool at the bottom, where the coins went into the water with a hiss like rain.

Thud. Thud. Thud.

On the highest shelf, near the ledge where Stephanos stood, the presses were smaller, and the men at the wheels were still men. They were thin and filthy and chained at the ankle, and they were soaked to the skin, and they worked with their heads down and did not look up. The water on this shelf was only ankle-deep. Further down, the shelves were deeper in it.

At the nearest press, working the wheel with a savage, furious strength, as if he meant to tear it off its axle, was a young man taller than any of the others. He had broad shoulders and long arms and a mass of dark curly hair plastered to his skull by the damp, and his face, in the grey light, was handsome and hard and set like stone.

"Petros!" cried Dora.

The young man's head came up.

He saw her. For a moment his face did not change. Then it cracked open, like ice breaking on a river, and Stephanos saw a boy's face under the man's, astonished and frightened and fierce all at once.

"Dora?" he said. "Dora? What... what are you doing here? You little idiot! Get out! Get out, they'll see you!"

"We came to get you," said Dora. She was already running down the ledge toward him, splashing through the water. "We came all the way from Arkadiopolis. There's a knight. He killed a giant with a sling. And a fool, and an Amma, and..."

Petros looked past her at Stephanos. His eyes went to the purple on the shield, and the lamellar, and the sword.

He grinned.

It was a wide, white, wolfish grin, and it lit up his whole face. He took hold of the chain that ran from his ankle to the press, with both hands, and set his feet, and pulled. The muscles stood out on his arms and neck like ropes. His face went dark red. For a long moment nothing happened.

Then the iron ring that held the chain to the base of the press burst out of the rock with a crack like a whip, and Petros staggered backward and nearly fell, and stood there in the water, laughing, with six feet of chain hanging from his ankle.

"A knight!" he shouted. His voice rang off the roof of the cave. "A Hornbearer! Grandmother always said you'd come! She said the Tagma would come for the lame lord's house one day!" He swung the loose chain around his head and let it crash down on the next press, and the iron weight jumped on its beam and stuck. "Come on, then, Hornbearer! Come on! Let's break it all!"

Every man on the upper shelf had stopped working. Every head had come up.

And far below, on the lowest shelf, beside the black breathing pool, in the deepest dark of the Drowned Mint, something very large began to move.

Stephanos did not see it. He was looking at Petros, standing in the water with the broken chain in his fists and his head thrown back, laughing, tall and strong and fearless, with the Shepherd's blood in his veins.

He thought: *This one. Surely this one.*

Beside him, very quietly, Amma Thekla began to pray.
