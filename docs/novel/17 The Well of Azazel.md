## Chapter 17: The Well of Azazel

*I will make rivers flow on barren heights, and springs within the valleys. I will turn the desert into pools of water, and the parched ground into springs.*

*The prophet Isaiah*

---

The Well of Azazel was a hole in the desert, and nothing grew within a hundred paces of it.

They came to it at dawn, walking, because the horses would not go near it. It lay in a shallow bowl of white sand a mile south of the laura, where the gorge opened out onto the flat. The sand around it was not like the sand elsewhere. It was finer and whiter, like flour, and when Stephanos knelt and touched it, it stung his cut fingers. Salt.

The well itself was round and wide, twenty paces across, and its rim was built of great blocks of pale stone, worn smooth, with no mortar between them. Giant's work, Kale said. There was no windlass. There was no rope. There was no bucket. There was a hole, going down into the dark, and a smell coming up out of it, dry and bitter, like the inside of a salt cellar.

Stephanos lay on his belly on the rim and looked down. He could see nothing. The morning light went down the shaft a little way and stopped, as if the dark swallowed it.

"How deep?" he said.

"Deep," said Thekla. She was sitting on the rim, weaving. She had been weaving since she woke, without a pause. "The old men said three hundred cubits to the water, when there was water. Then the city under the water. Then the city under the city."

They had brought rope from the laura, all the rope Iakovos could find, knotted together into one long line. Kale tied it to the biggest block on the rim and dropped the coil down into the dark. They listened. They did not hear it land.

"I'll go first," said Stephanos.

"With one arm?" said Kale.

"With one arm and two legs." He tested the splint on his right wrist. It would hold the rope, if not much else. "Lazaros, give me the lamp."

"Stephanos," said Thekla.

He looked at her.

"When you get to the bottom," the old woman said, "don't look back up. Whatever you hear. Whatever you think you see. Don't look back at the way you came." She did not stop weaving. "That's how they turn to salt."

---

He went down into the dark with the lamp tied to his belt and the rope between his knees and his good hand.

The walls of the shaft were smooth stone at first, pale and dry, and then, after a long way, they changed. They became rough and white and glittering, and when he put his hand to them to steady himself, they crumbled a little under his fingers. Salt. The whole shaft was cut through salt, a great pale seam of it, like the inside of a cave of ice.

He went down a long time. His arms burned. His broken wrist throbbed in its splint. The circle of daylight above grew smaller and smaller, and then he could not see it at all without looking up, and he did not look up.

He heard things.

At first it was only the sound of his own breathing, loud in the narrow shaft, and the creak of the rope, and the whisper of salt trickling down the walls where his boots brushed them. Then, far below, very faint, he heard singing. It was a hymn. He knew it. It was the hymn the monks sang at the laura on Sunday morning, at the rising of the sun: *Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace, good will toward men.* Men's voices, many of them, singing together, the way monks sing.

Then it changed. The voices were still singing, but the words were different. He could not make them out. They were slow and heavy and sad, and every line ended lower than it began, like someone sinking.

And then, from above him, from the top of the shaft, a voice called his name.

"Stephanos!"

It was the Abba.

He knew it at once. He would have known it anywhere. The old dry papery voice of Pelagios the Fool, calling down the shaft from the daylight above.

"Stephanos! Come back! Come back up! They need you in the City! The Hunter's at the walls! The Domestikos is dead! Come back, my son! Come back!"

He stopped on the rope. He hung there in the dark with his heart pounding. The voice was right above him. It was so close. It sounded so frightened.

*Look up,* something said in his mind. *Look up and see. It's him. He's come all this way to find you. Look up.*

He did not look up.

He hung there with his eyes shut and his face pressed against the cold salt of the wall, and he said, out loud, in a whisper: "*Lord Jesus Christ. Son of God. Have mercy on me.*"

The voice above him stopped.

He opened his eyes and went on down.

---

At the bottom of the shaft was a city made of salt.

He stood on a floor of packed white crystal in the pool of his lamplight and looked around, and did not believe it for a long time.

The well had come down through the roof of a cavern, vast and domed, like the Cisterns of the City but bigger, and in the cavern, stretching away into the dark on every side as far as his light could reach, there were buildings. Houses, with doorways and windows and flat roofs. Streets between them, paved. Columns. Porticoes. A market square, with stalls. A fountain, dry, in the middle of it. Stairs going up to terraces. Towers. And all of it, every stone and every step and every column, was salt. White, glittering, crystalline salt, as if the whole city had been carved out of a single block of it.

There were people in the streets.

They were salt too. They stood in the doorways and on the steps and in the market square, frozen in the middle of whatever they had been doing. A woman bending over a stall. A man with a basket on his shoulder. Two children running. An old man sitting on a bench. And every one of them, every single one, had their head turned, looking back over their shoulder, toward the north.

He stood very still. He did not look back.

The others came down the rope one by one. Kale first, fast and light. Then Lazaros, with the icon on his back. Then Sergios, who slid down the whole last hundred cubits without using his hands and landed laughing. Then Dora, with the veil over her face and the sling of Poimen around her wrist. Then Theophanes, grey and trembling, with the purple book in his satchel. Last came the Amma, lowered on a loop of rope by the others with her basket still in her lap, weaving all the way down.

None of them looked back.

"The giants' city," said Thekla, when she stood on the salt floor. She looked around her at the white streets and the frozen people. "Before the Flood. The rebel sons of God built it for their children, and God burned it with fire from heaven, and the Dead Sea came up and covered it, and when the sea went down again, this was left." She began to walk. "The hermitage is this way. By the old spring. Stay together. Don't touch the people."

"Are they alive?" asked Dora.

"They were never people," said Thekla. "Not the way we mean. They're what the Wilderness makes out of despair. Salt is what's left when all the water's gone." She did not stop walking. "Don't touch them."

---

The hermitage was in the middle of the salt city, beside the dry fountain in the market square.

It had been a spring once. They could see where the water had come up, out of a crack in the rock at the foot of a low cliff, and where it had run down in a stone channel to the fountain. The channel was dry and white now, crusted with salt. And beside the crack, cut into the cliff face by human hands, was a cell: a small square room, big enough for one man to lie down in, with a stone bench and a stone shelf and, in the back wall, a niche.

The niche was empty. The hook in the roof of it was bare. The plaster at the back was white and clean, cleaner than the salt around it.

Lazaros filled Kale's bronze lamp with the last of the Göreme oil. He hung it on the hook, and lit it.

The words came up out of the plaster, black and sharp.

*When the least of the Shepherd's house goes down, the Horn will come up.*

For a long moment nobody spoke. Then Theophanes took the purple book out of his satchel, and opened it to the back page where the other three were written, and copied the fourth underneath them with his burnt twig. His hand was shaking so badly that the letters came out crooked. When he had finished, he read all four aloud, in a low voice, one after another.

"*Where the proud one stood, the Horn lay down. It went down with the lamps, and it keeps the door. The strong will seek it, and it will not rise for them. When the least of the Shepherd's house goes down, the Horn will come up.*"

He stopped. He looked at Stephanos.

"Where the proud one stood," Theophanes said. "That's the Great Church. It has to be. Andronikos set his statue in the Great Church, under the dome. The Horn lay down there. That night. The deacon Isaakios saw it; the older chronicle says so; there's a whole page. It went down. With the lamps. The seven lamps above the altar, the ones that went dark." He was speaking faster now. "It went down through the floor of the sanctuary. Where the crack is. Where the Patriarch sealed it with the icon of Michael. It's down there. Under the Great Church. In the Undercroft, at the bottom, where the crypts go down past the cisterns. And it keeps the door. What door? There's only one door at the bottom of the Undercroft. Everyone in the City knows the story. The Gate of Sheol."

"The strong will seek it," said Kale, "and it will not rise for them."

"And when the least of the Shepherd's house goes down," said Theophanes, "the Horn will come up."

He looked at Stephanos again. They were all looking at Stephanos. Kale, and Lazaros, and Sergios, and the Amma with her hands still on her basket.

Not Dora. Dora was looking at the niche.

"Who is the least of the Shepherd's house, Stephanos?" said Theophanes quietly.

Stephanos had known it was coming. He had known since the mountain. He had known since Tarsus, lying awake. He had his answer ready.

"The one who's been brought lowest," he said. "Petros went up the mountain as the proudest man in Göreme and came down on a litter. He was humbled in front of the whole valley. He's been fasting every Friday since. He hasn't touched wine. He's the lame lord's great-grandson, and he's been brought as low as a man can go." He heard his own voice, steady and reasonable. "The least doesn't mean the smallest. It means the humblest. It means the one who knows he's nothing."

"And the stranger with no shadow?" said Kale.

"If he was an angel..."

"He wasn't," said Kale.

"You didn't see anything," said Stephanos. "You said so. You said it was like looking at the sun."

"I said I couldn't see anything," said Kale. "That's what frightened me." She did not raise her voice. "Stephanos. Look at the words. *The least of the Shepherd's house.* Not the humblest. The least. The youngest. Like the Shepherd himself. Theophanes read it to us at the Gates. The youngest of eight sons, out with the sheep, and his brothers were angry with him when he came." She pointed, without looking. "There's only one person in this cell who fits that."

He did not look at Dora.

"The Horn anoints emperors," he said.

"There was an Empress Irene," said Theophanes quietly. "Two hundred years before the Shepherd. She ruled alone for five years. The Eparch is named for her."

"She blinded her own son to do it." Stephanos heard his voice rising and could not stop it. "And the Horn wasn't there. It didn't anoint her. It's never anointed a woman. It's never anointed a child. She's thirteen, Theophanes. She keeps sheep. She can't read. She..."

"I can read," said Dora.

She had turned around from the niche. She was standing by the wall of the little cell with the lamplight on her face, and the veil of the Myrrh-bearer pushed back, and the sling of Poimen around her wrist.

"Grandmother taught me," she said. "From the lame lord's letters. I can read. I read all of them." She looked at Stephanos steadily. "And I'm not asking you to say it's me. I don't know if it's me. I don't think it matters very much who it is. Grandmother always said the Horn would find who it wanted, and it wouldn't be the one anybody picked, and that was the whole point." She paused. "But you're not reading the words, Stephanos. You're reading Petros into them. You've been doing it since the Mint. You'd have read him into them if they'd said the Horn will rise for a one-legged Egyptian with a squint."

Sergios laughed, loud and sudden, and then clapped his hand over his mouth.

"It doesn't say *the least is the heir*," said Dora. "It says when the least goes down, the Horn will come up. It doesn't say anything about who's anointed. It's about who goes down." She looked back at the niche. "Somebody's got to go down."

Nobody said anything.

Then Amma Thekla said, very quietly: "Listen."

They listened.

The singing had come back. The slow, sad, sinking hymn Stephanos had heard in the shaft, with the words he could not make out. It was coming from the far side of the market square. From a building there, bigger than the others, with a portico of salt columns and a wide stair going up to a door.

It was coming closer.

And the lamplight in the cell had stopped flickering. The flame on the wick stood perfectly still, like a flame painted on a wall.

"He knows we're here," said Thekla. She stood up. She put her basket under her arm and went on weaving. "Take out your palm. All of you. Now."

---

The Noonday King came down the stair of the salt palace into the market square, and time stopped.

Stephanos felt it happen. He had felt it on the church porch at the laura, the sun not moving, the shadow not moving, the day stretching out forever. This was worse. This was the thing itself, not its shadow. It came over the salt city like a great wave of stillness, and everything in it stopped. The lamp flame stood still. The dust in the air stood still, glittering, each mote hanging where it was. His own breath seemed to stop in his chest halfway between in and out.

And into the stillness came the King.

He was tall and thin, so thin that he seemed to be made of sticks, and he wore the ragged black habit of a desert monk, belted with rope, the same as Thekla's. His skin was white. Not pale; white, glittering, crystalline, like the salt of the city. His hair and beard were long and white and stiff as frost. His face was the face of an old man, noble and lined and very tired, with deep-set eyes that were entirely black. And on his head he wore a crown. It was a circle of white light, blinding, a ring of fire like the sun at noon, and it burned without heat and without flicker, perfectly still.

He stopped at the foot of the stair and looked at them.

"*Thekla,*" he said.

His voice was soft and dry and very gentle. It was the most tired voice Stephanos had ever heard.

"*You came back. I knew you would. You always did. Even when you were twenty, and I sent you away because you talked too much, you always came back.*" The black eyes moved over the rest of them, slowly, one by one. "*And you've brought friends. How kind. They look tired, Thekla. Are they tired?*"

"Abba," said the old woman.

"*They are tired,*" said the Noonday King. "*I can see it. The knight is tired of waiting. The archer is tired of losing. The painter is tired of pain. The scribe is tired of not knowing what's true. The fool...*" His eyes rested on Sergios, and slid away. "*The girl.*" He looked at Dora for a long moment. "*The girl is tired of not being seen.*"

Stephanos felt it come into him.

It came softly, like the heat of noon coming through a closed door. The thought. The reasonable, tired, true thought. *What is the point. What is the point.* Only it was so much stronger now, so much heavier, that it was like a great weight settling on his shoulders, pressing him down toward the salt floor.

*You'll never get out,* said the thought. *You're three hundred cubits under the desert in a city of salt. The rope's a long way up. You're tired. Your arm is broken. Your knee is ruined. And even if you got out, what then? The Hunter is coming. The City will fall. Petros is lost. The girl hates you. It's over. Lie down. Rest. You've done enough.*

His knees began to bend.

"Over," said Thekla's voice beside him, cracked and fierce. "Under. *Lord Jesus Christ.*"

His hands moved. He did not tell them to. They were holding the half-woven basket from the laura, and they moved on it, over and under, the way she had taught him. His lips moved. *Son of God.*

The weight on his shoulders did not lift. But his knees stopped bending.

All around him, the others were weaving. Kale with her jaw clenched and tears running down her face. Lazaros with his eyes shut. Sergios cross-legged on the salt floor, braiding his reeds and humming. Dora with her own small basket in her rough brown hands, over and under, fast, her lips moving.

Theophanes was not weaving.

He was standing still in the middle of the cell with the purple book clutched against his chest in both arms, and his head was turning. Slowly. Over his shoulder. Toward the north.

"Theophanes!" Stephanos shouted. "Don't!"

It was too late. The scribe's head had turned. He was looking back. Back over his shoulder at the dark way they had come, toward the shaft, toward the desert, toward the north and the City and everything he had left there. And his face had changed. It had the look the salt pilgrims had on the road: longing and horror at once, frozen.

The salt began at his feet.

Stephanos saw it creep up out of the floor, white and glittering, over the toes of his sandals, over his ankles. It climbed his legs like ivy up a wall. Theophanes did not move. He did not seem to notice. He stood looking back over his shoulder, and his lips were moving, and Stephanos heard what he was saying.

"*Master. Master. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'll come home. I'll bring them. I'll bring you everything. I'm sorry.*"

Amma Thekla dropped her basket.

She went to Theophanes in two quick steps and took the clay flask from her belt, the last of the holy water from the Jordan, the flask she had carried from the City and used on the seer in the Cisterns and on the salt child on the road. She pulled out the stopper with her teeth. And she poured all of it, every drop, over the scribe's head.

The salt stopped.

It ran. It ran down off Theophanes's legs like snow off a roof, and pooled around his feet, and soaked away into the floor. His head jerked round. He stared at the old woman, and at the empty flask in her hand, and his face crumpled, and he fell to his knees on the salt with the purple book still clutched against his chest and began to sob.

"*That was the last of it,*" said the Noonday King softly. "*Wasn't it, Thekla? The last holy water. You were saving it for me.*"

"I was," said Thekla.

"*And now you've spent it on a scribe who was going to betray you all. You can see it in his face. You've always been able to see it. I taught you.*" The black eyes were sad. "*It was always like this, Thekla. You were always wasting yourself on the wrong ones. Pouring out your water on salt. What is the point?*"

The old woman did not pick up her basket. She stood in front of the Noonday King with her empty hands at her sides, small and bent and brown, in her black habit, and looked up at him.

"You taught me that too," she said. "Forty-two years ago. In your cell. You said: *Thekla, the point is not whether the water saves them. The point is that you pour it.*"

The Noonday King was silent.

"You taught me the prayer," said Thekla. "And the palm. And how to sit in the cell at noon when the sun won't move and the thoughts come. You taught me that the demon of acedia is a liar, even when it tells the truth, because it only tells you half of it." She took a step toward him. "It says, *there is no point*. And that's true. There is no point in us. There never was. We're dust. We're salt. We're nothing." Another step. "But it doesn't tell you the other half. It doesn't tell you that he came down into the dust with us. It doesn't tell you he was nothing too, on the cross, and in the tomb, and that he went down into Sheol and came up again with the keys in his hand." Her voice cracked. "You taught me that, Abba. You taught me all of it. And then you forgot."

The white crown on the King's head flickered.

It was a small thing. Stephanos almost missed it. The ring of still white fire wavered, for the first time, like a candle flame in a draught.

The King lifted his hand. And from all over the salt city, the salt people began to move.

---

They came out of the doorways and down the steps and across the market square, hundreds of them, walking stiffly, like puppets. Their heads were still turned back over their shoulders. They could not see where they were going. They walked sideways, crabwise, with their faces toward the north, and their white hands reached out in front of them, groping.

Kale shot the first one. The arrow went into its salt chest and it cracked, and fell, and broke into pieces on the floor. But there were so many. Stephanos stood in the door of the cell with his sword in his left hand, because his right was useless, and cut at them as they came, and they broke, and more came. Their hands closed on him. They were cold and dry and rough and they pulled, and where they touched his skin he felt the salt begin to spread.

"Don't let them turn your head!" Thekla shouted. "Whatever you do!"

He did not let them turn his head. He cut. He broke them. The salt dust rose in clouds around him, glittering, hanging in the still air.

Beside him, Dora was using the sling of Poimen.

She was using it the way he had never seen anyone use a sling. She did not whirl it over her head. There was no room. She flicked it from her wrist, short and sharp, underhand, and the stones went out of the pouch like arrows. She had filled her apron pockets with salt crystals from the floor, sharp and heavy, and she threw them one after another, fast as breathing, and every one struck a salt pilgrim in the face, and the pilgrim cracked and fell.

"Stephanos," said Sergios behind him. The fool's voice was calm. "The water."

"What water?"

"The spring. It's under us. Can't you hear it?"

He could not hear anything but the crack and crash of breaking salt and the slow sad hymn of the King. But Sergios was kneeling on the floor of the cell beside the dry crack in the rock where the spring had once come up, with his ear pressed to the stone.

"It's there," the fool said. "It's still there. It's been there the whole time. It's only asleep." He looked up at Thekla. "Amma. Tell him. Tell the King. Tell him about the water."

---

The old woman had not moved. She stood in the middle of the market square in front of the Noonday King, and the salt pilgrims went around her on both sides like a river round a rock. None of them touched her.

"Makarios," she said.

The King looked down at her. His black eyes were full of something that might have been pain.

"*Don't,*" he said. "*Don't, Thekla. It's too late. I gave it away. I gave him my hope. There's nothing left in me but this.*" He touched the white crown. "*The noon. The stillness. The salt. I can't give it back. I don't have anything to give it back with.*"

"You don't have to give it back," said Thekla. "You only have to ask."

"*Ask?*"

"The prayer. The one you taught me. The one you said a million times in your cell." Her voice was very gentle. "You don't have to believe it. You don't have to feel it. You never did, you said; you said most days you felt nothing at all. You only have to say it. With your mouth. Once." She held out her hand to him, the brown, wrinkled, palm-scarred hand. "Say it with me, Abba. *Lord Jesus Christ.*"

The Noonday King stood very still.

All around the market square, the salt pilgrims had stopped. They stood where they were, frozen again, with their heads turned north.

"*I can't,*" whispered the King.

"*Lord Jesus Christ,*" said Thekla.

His lips moved. No sound came out.

"*Son of God,*" said Thekla.

"*...Son...*"

The white crown flickered. It flickered again. It wavered and bent like a candle flame in a wind, and the stillness around it wavered too. Stephanos felt it. He felt the air move on his face for the first time since they had come down the shaft. He saw the dust in the air begin to drift.

"*Have mercy on me,*" said Thekla.

The Noonday King fell to his knees.

"*Have mercy on me,*" he said. His voice was a dry rasp, like wind over sand. "*Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me. Have mercy on me. Have mercy on me, a sinner.*"

The crown went out.

It did not fade. It went out all at once, like a lamp blown out, and the market square was dark except for the small flame of Kale's lamp in the niche and Lazaros's lamp in his hand. In the sudden dark, Stephanos heard a sound he did not understand at first. It came from under the floor. A deep, low, rushing sound, like wind in a chimney, or like a crowd of people all breathing out at once.

Water.

It came up out of the crack in the rock beside the hermitage cell. It came up in a gush, black and cold and clear, and splashed over the dry salt channel and ran down it toward the fountain, and the salt of the channel dissolved in front of it like sugar in wine. It reached the fountain. The fountain filled. It overflowed. The water ran out across the market square in a sheet, spreading, and everywhere it touched, the salt ran.

The salt pilgrims were melting.

They stood in the rising water and it ran up around their ankles and their knees, and they dissolved, slowly, gently, from the feet up, like snowmen in the spring. Their heads were still turned back toward the north. But as the water rose over their chests and their shoulders, Stephanos saw them turn. One after another. Their salt faces turned slowly around, away from the north, toward the fountain, toward the water. And the longing and the horror went out of them, and they were only faces, calm and still. And then the water covered them, and they were gone.

In the middle of the square, the Noonday King knelt in the water with the old woman kneeling in front of him, holding both his hands.

He was dissolving too. His white glittering salt skin was running in the water like wax. But his face was not the face of the King anymore. It was the face of an old man, noble and lined and very tired, with brown eyes, not black. He was looking at Thekla.

"*Thekla,*" he said. "*I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I left you.*"

"I know, Abba."

"*I was so tired.*"

"I know."

"*Will you pray for me?*"

"Every day," said Thekla. "At noon."

The old man smiled at her. Then the water rose over his shoulders, and over his face, and his hands dissolved out of hers, and she knelt alone in the rising water with her empty hands held out in front of her, and her head bowed, and her shoulders shaking.

---

They climbed out of the Well of Azazel with the water rising behind them.

It rose fast. By the time the last of them, the Amma, was hauled up out of the shaft on the loop of rope by Kale and Stephanos together, the water was a hundred cubits from the top and still climbing, black and clear and cold, with the reflection of the sky in it. It rose until it reached the old high-water mark on the giants' stones of the rim, a dark line that had been dry for three hundred years. And there it stopped, and lay still, and a little wind came across the desert and ruffled its surface, and it was a well again.

Around the rim, in the white salt sand, something was coming up. It was green. Tiny shoots, thin as hairs, pushing up through the salt, a ring of them, all around the well.

Thekla sat on the rim and looked at them for a long time. She did not weave. Her hands lay open in her lap.

"Twenty years," she said. "He was down there twenty years. With the water under him all the time. Right under him. All he had to do was ask." She shook her head. "All he had to do was ask."

Nobody said anything. After a while, Dora came and sat beside her, and put her arm around the old woman's thin shoulders, and the Amma leaned against her and closed her eyes.

Kale was standing a little way off from the well, on the edge of the bowl of white sand, looking north. She had been standing there since she came up the rope. She had not moved.

"Kale?" said Lazaros.

"He's crossed," said Kale.

They all looked at her.

"The Hunter." Her voice was flat. Her eyes were fixed on the northern horizon, where there was nothing to see but brown hills and white sky. "I can see it. I can see him. He's crossed the Bosporus. He crossed at Chrysopolis, three days ago, on a bridge of ships the Concord built for him. His host is in Thrace. They're burning the villages." She swallowed. "The ground shakes where he walks. There are so many of them, Stephanos. So many. Giants and men and things I don't have names for. They cover the land like locusts. They're a week from the Land Walls." She turned her head, finally, and looked at him. Her face was grey. "Maybe less."

Stephanos stood by the well with the rope still coiled over his shoulder and the salt still crusted on his boots, and he felt the four Words of Symeon in the purple book in Theophanes's satchel like a weight on his own chest.

"The coast," he said. "Gaza. Or Caesarea. We find a ship. We sail tonight."

---

That night, in the laura of Saint Chariton, while the others slept, Theophanes sat alone in his cave with a candle and a sheet of clean parchment from the back of the purple book.

He wrote the four Words of Symeon on it, carefully, in his best hand. Under them, he wrote: *The Horn is beneath the sanctuary of the Great Church, at the Gate of Sheol, at the bottom of the Undercroft. It will rise when the least of the Shepherd's house goes down. The least is the girl.*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he wrote one more line underneath: *She will be at the Fool's Tower on the Land Walls, with the blind Abba, when the Hunter comes. The Hornbearer will leave her there. He will ride out with the Emperor. I know him.*

He folded the parchment very small and sealed it with a drop of candle wax, and put it inside the back cover of the purple book, beside Kallinikos's letter.

He did not sleep. He sat until dawn with the book in his lap and his cut hands lying on it, not weaving, not praying, only looking at the wall of the cave, where the candle had burned out.
