## Chapter 19: The Field of Melantias

*How the mighty have fallen in the midst of the battle!*

*David's lament for Saul and Jonathan*

---

The Hunter was waiting for them on the far side of the river.

Stephanos saw him from two miles off, as the army came over the last low ridge before the plain of Melantias and the whole valley of the Athyras opened out below. He had been braced for it since dawn. He had fought giants for fifteen years. He had seen Anak son of Gathos stand four metres tall in his grandfather's barrow. He thought he was ready.

He was not ready.

The plain on the far side of the river was black with men. That was the first thing. They covered it from the riverbank to the hills behind, a mile deep and three miles wide, a dark mass that moved and glittered in the morning sun like the scales of a snake. Banners stood up out of it, hundreds of them, in colours he did not know. Smoke rose from its campfires. The sound of it came across the valley on the wind: a vast low murmur, like the sea heard from a long way off, made of drums and horns and voices and the tramp of feet and the creak of wagons and the clash of iron, all together.

Among the men, standing up out of them like trees out of long grass, were the giants.

There were hundreds of them. Gibborim in their rusted mail, twice the height of the men around them, moving through the host in knots of ten and twenty. Anakim, taller still, like Anak had been, crowned with iron, each with a war-host of lesser giants around him. And things that were not quite giants and not quite men: grey shapes, hunched and long-armed, that moved on all fours; figures in black robes covered with writing, standing still in the midst of the host, with green fire playing around their hands.

And in the middle of it all, on a low hill a mile back from the river, stood a tower.

It was not a building. It was a platform, a great square scaffold of timber and iron, eighty feet high, built on wheels as big as houses, and drawn by what looked like a hundred oxen. On top of it was a throne. And on the throne sat a figure that Stephanos could see clearly even from two miles away, because it was the height of the tower itself.

Nebrodes the Hunter.

He was a giant among giants. Sitting, he was taller than any of the Anakim standing around the foot of his tower. He wore purple: the imperial purple of the Caesars, a cloak of it that fell from his shoulders to the platform and spilled over the edge like a waterfall, and Stephanos knew, with a cold certainty, whose cloak it was. It was the cloak Andronikos the Proud had worn on the night of the statue. Nebrodes had taken it from the palace when he broke the walls, and he had worn it for a hundred years.

Across his knees lay a bow. It was as long as a ship's mast, black, and even from here Stephanos could see the arrows in the great quiver on the platform beside the throne: each one as long as a lance, with a head of black iron.

"He's not moving," said the Emperor.

Bardas had reined in beside him on the ridge. His face under his gilded helm was calm, but Stephanos saw his hand tighten on the reins.

"He's waiting for us to cross," said the Domestikos, on Bardas's other side.

"Then we won't cross," said Bardas. "We'll hold the bridge. That's what we came for. If he wants the City, he has to come over the Athyras, and there's only one bridge for twenty miles." He pointed with his sword. "We hold the near end. Cataphracts in front, the levies behind, archers on the banks. Let him come to us."

The Athyras bridge was old, built in the days of the first emperors: a long causeway of stone on seven arches, wide enough for six horsemen abreast, running a quarter of a mile across the river and the marshes on either side of it. The near end was held by a stone gatehouse. The far end was held by the Hunter.

"Yes, Majesty," said Nikephoros.

They went down the ridge to the bridge in good order, the way the Domestikos had drilled them, eleven thousand men moving like one. The cataphracts drew up in a wedge across the near end of the causeway with their lances levelled, Hornbearers in the front rank. The levies of the City formed up behind them in a great square of spears. The archers spread out along the riverbank on either side, among the reeds.

Stephanos took his place at the point of the wedge, at the Emperor's right hand, with the purple on his lance and the red eagle of Taron snapping on its pole behind him. Petros was on the Emperor's left. And a little way back, sitting his white horse in the middle of the cataphracts, the stranger in the white cloak watched the far bank with his calm and beautiful face.

The morning wore on.

Nothing happened. The host on the far bank did not move. The drums went on beating. The Hunter sat on his throne on his tower, and did not move either. The sun climbed. The men in the wedge sat their horses in their armour and sweated and waited.

At noon, the stranger rode forward.

He came up through the ranks of the cataphracts on his white horse, unhurried, and men moved aside for him without being asked. He reined in beside the Emperor.

"Majesty," he said.

His voice was low and clear and very kind. Stephanos had not heard it before. It was a voice that made you want to lean closer.

"Majesty, he's afraid."

Bardas turned his head. "Afraid?"

"Look at him. He sits on his tower and waits. He doesn't come. A hundred years ago he broke the Land Walls in three places in a single day. Now he sits on the far side of a river and waits for eleven thousand men to come to him." The stranger's beautiful face was grave. "He's afraid, Majesty. He's heard the Words of Symeon. He knows the Horn is waking. He knows the anointed of God is coming. And he's afraid to face you."

"Then let him wait," said the Domestikos. "Let him wait till winter. We hold the bridge."

"And the City?" said the stranger gently. "Every day he waits, his seers are at work. Every day, the Concord's agents are inside the walls. Every day, the Hunter's host grows. Giant kings are coming from Armenia even now; they'll be here in a week. And when they come, the bridge won't hold." He looked at Bardas. "Now is the moment, Majesty. Now, while he's afraid. Now, while his host is spread along the riverbank and not formed for battle. Cross the bridge. Charge him. Break him on the far bank before he knows what's happening. This is the day. This is the hour. God has put him in your hand."

Bardas was silent.

Stephanos looked at the far end of the bridge. He looked at the host on the far bank. It was not spread out. It was not unformed. It was waiting. He could see the Gibborim standing in their knots of twenty along the riverbank, with their axes grounded. He could see the black-robed seers.

He could see, if he looked, that the Hunter on his tower had turned his great head, and was watching the Emperor.

"Majesty," said Stephanos. "Don't."

Bardas turned to look at him.

"It's a trap," said Stephanos. He heard his own voice as if from a distance. "He's waiting for us. He's been waiting all morning. He wants us on the bridge."

The stranger looked at him too. His blue eyes were full of sorrow. "Hornbearer," he said. "You've done great things. God has used you. But you've been in the Wilderness a long time, and the Wilderness breeds fear." He turned back to Bardas. "The strong will seek the Horn, and it will not rise for them. You know the Word, Majesty. You're not seeking the Horn. You're seeking the Hunter. God has put him in your hand. Will you close your hand, or open it?"

Bardas looked at the far bank for a long moment.

Then he lifted his sword.

"Cross the bridge," said the Emperor.

---

They crossed the bridge.

Stephanos would remember it for the rest of his life: the thunder of the hooves on the old stone, the wind in his face, the purple on his lance-point streaming, the red eagle of Taron cracking on its pole behind him. The Emperor was beside him, and Petros on the Emperor's other side, and a thousand cataphracts behind them in a wedge of steel, and the far bank was coming up fast, and the Gibborim along it were raising their axes.

For a moment, for one long glorious moment, it was the best thing he had ever done.

They struck the far bank like a hammer. The first rank of Gibborim went down under the lances. Stephanos felt his lance go into the chest of a giant, and break, and he dropped it and drew his sword and cut down at another giant's face as Dove carried him past. The wedge went into the host like a knife into bread. Men were screaming. Giants were roaring. He saw Petros on the Emperor's left swinging the sword with the red stone in its hilt, and a Gibbor's arm flew off at the elbow, and Petros was laughing, the wide white wolfish grin from the Mint. He saw the Emperor cut a path through a knot of Concord soldiers in red cloaks. He saw the Domestikos's big grey horse rear and strike with its forefeet. They were going through. They were going to break them.

Then the bridge fell.

He heard it behind him. A crack like thunder, splitting the sky. He turned in the saddle and saw it: the old stone causeway of the Athyras, seven arches long, breaking. It broke in the middle, at the fourth arch, where the black-robed seers on the far bank had turned their green fire on it. The arch cracked and fell into the river. Then the third arch. Then the fifth. The stone went down into the brown water in a roar of spray, and the cataphracts still on the bridge went down with it, horses and men together, screaming.

On the near bank, the City's levies stood in their great square of spears and stared across the river at the gap where the bridge had been.

On the far bank, a thousand cataphracts and their Emperor were alone.

And the Hunter's host closed around them like a fist.

---

It came from every side at once.

The Gibborim came in from the left and the right, where they had stood waiting along the riverbank. The Concord's Iron Host came from the front, in ranks of red cloaks and levelled spears, disciplined, steady, marching in step. The long-armed grey things came from everywhere, scuttling on all fours through the legs of the giants, leaping up onto the horses' backs and tearing at the riders. And the seers stood in the midst of the host and lifted their hands, and green fire came down on the cataphracts like rain.

Stephanos fought.

He fought the way he had been trained to fight for fifteen years, the way he had fought at the Halys and at Dorylaion and on a hundred nameless fields in the east. He did not think. He cut and parried and turned Dove with his knees and cut again. He killed a Concord soldier and a grey thing and wounded a Gibbor. His splinted wrist was agony. He did not notice. He was aware of the Emperor beside him, fighting, and of Petros, and of the red eagle of Taron still flying on its pole somewhere behind him.

He was aware of the Domestikos, off to his right, shouting orders in a voice like a trumpet, trying to form the cataphracts into a ring.

He was not aware of the stranger. He looked for him once, in a moment between blows, and did not see him. The white horse was there, a little way back, among the fighting. It was standing still with its reins hanging loose and its saddle empty, and it was looking around, calmly, as if it had never had a rider at all.

Then the Hunter stood up.

He rose from his throne on the tower a mile back, slowly, and the whole host fell silent around him. Stephanos felt the silence go out across the field like a wave. The drums stopped. The giants stopped roaring. Even the grey things stopped, and crouched, and turned their heads toward the tower.

Nebrodes stood on his tower in the imperial purple and lifted his great black bow, and set an arrow to the string, and drew it back to his ear.

He was a mile away. No bow on earth could shoot a mile.

The arrow came down out of the sky like a thunderbolt. It went through the Domestikos's horse from withers to belly and pinned it to the ground, and Nikephoros Skleros was thrown clear, and rolled, and came up with his sword still in his hand, and the grey things were on him before he could stand.

The second arrow went through the Armenian cataphract who carried the red eagle of Taron, and through his horse, and the pole went down, and the banner with it, and the Gibborim trampled it into the mud.

The third arrow went through the Emperor's shield and his arm and his side, and Bardas Argyros fell from his horse.

---

Stephanos was off Dove before he knew he had moved.

The Emperor was lying on his back in the trampled grass with a black arrow the length of a lance through his left side. His gilded helm had come off. His wheat-coloured hair was spread out on the ground around his head. His eyes were open. He was looking up at the sky.

Stephanos knelt beside him. Around them, the fight roared on. He did not hear it.

"Majesty."

Bardas turned his head. His face was very white.

"He was an angel," said the Emperor. His voice was a whisper. "He knew everything. The Sangarios. The fords. The Hippodrome. He knew." He coughed, and blood came out of his mouth. "Where is he?"

Stephanos did not answer.

"He's gone," said Bardas. He was still looking up at the sky. "Isn't he. He's gone. He was never there." He shut his eyes. "I said I didn't want it. The crown. I said it in the Magnaura. I said I'd give it back." He smiled, a strange, broken smile. "I lied. I wanted it more than anything. I wanted it since I was a boy. And he knew. He always knew. That's why he came."

"Majesty..."

"Don't call me that." Bardas opened his eyes. "Stephanos. The Horn. Was it ever me?"

Stephanos looked down at him. He wanted to say yes. He wanted to lie. He felt the belt of Truth around his waist, and it was cold, it had been cold since last night, since the armoury, since the Abba's room, since before, since Tarsus, since the wall. It was cold as a ring of ice.

"No," he said.

Bardas nodded slowly. He did not seem surprised.

"Good," he whispered. "Good. I'm glad. Tell whoever it is..." He coughed again. "Tell them I'm sorry. Tell them I..."

He did not finish. His eyes were still open, looking up at the sky over Melantias, but there was no one in them anymore.

Stephanos knelt beside him. He did not know for how long.

Then a hand closed on his shoulder and hauled him up, and it was Petros.

Petros's gilded armour was hacked and dented and splashed with blood. His helm was gone. His face was grey and his eyes were wide and wild, and the sword with the red stone in its pommel was broken off a foot from the hilt. He was shouting something. Stephanos could not hear it.

"...back! We have to get back! The river! Stephanos, the river!"

He looked round.

The cataphracts were gone. He did not understand it at first. There had been a thousand of them. There had been a ring of steel, and the Domestikos shouting orders. Now there were bodies, horses and men, on every side, heaped in the trampled grass, and the Gibborim were walking among them with their axes, and the grey things were crouched on them, feeding. He saw a few knots of riders still fighting, far off, surrounded. He saw a purple lance-pennant go down. He saw no one standing near him except Petros, and a dozen Concord soldiers in red cloaks coming toward them across the field at a steady walk, with spears levelled, and behind them, huge, three Gibborim.

He saw Dove.

The grey mare was lying on her side a few yards away with a Gibbor's axe in her neck. Her eyes were open. Her legs were moving, slowly, as if she were galloping in a dream. Then they stopped.

"The river!" Petros shouted. He was pulling at Stephanos's arm. "We can swim it! Come on!"

Stephanos went with him. He did not think. He ran, stumbling, with Petros dragging him, across the field toward the river and the broken bridge. The Concord soldiers came after them. An arrow went past his head. The riverbank was a hundred paces away, then fifty, then thirty. He could see the brown water, fast and deep, and on the far side, the City's levies, still standing in their square of spears, watching.

Then something hit him in the back like a hammer, and the ground came up, and there was nothing.

---

He woke under the dead.

It was dark. That was the first thing. There was a great weight on top of him, and the smell of blood and horse and opened bowels, and he could not breathe. He lay very still for a long time, trying to understand. Then he understood. He was lying on the riverbank of the Athyras, face down in the mud, and on top of him was a dead horse, and on top of the horse, men. He could feel an arm across the back of his neck. It was cold.

He did not know how long he had been there. It was night. He could hear, far off, singing: not hymns, but the deep rhythmic chant of the giants, and drums, and laughter. The Hunter's host was camped on the field. They were celebrating.

He began to dig himself out.

It took a long time. He could not use his right arm at all now. The splint had broken and the wrist was swollen to twice its size, and when he moved it, the pain went up to his shoulder like fire. He used his left arm and his legs. He pushed and wriggled and crawled, an inch at a time, out from under the horse and the dead men, until he lay in the mud at the edge of the river, gasping, under the stars.

His back hurt. He put his hand behind him and felt the lamellar there, and found a great dent in it, and a crack, and the broken shaft of an arrow sticking out. It had gone through the lamellar and the mail and stopped. He could feel the head of it, a cold hard point against his spine. Not in it. Against it.

He lay there a while longer.

Then he looked around for Petros. He knew before he looked that Petros would not be there. He looked anyway. He crawled along the riverbank in the dark among the dead, turning over the ones he could turn, looking at their faces in the starlight. Cataphracts. Hornbearers. Concord soldiers. A Gibbor with an arrow in his eye. He found Brother Gregoras, with his three-fingered hand still gripping his mace, and his eyes open, and his white beard black with blood. He closed the old man's eyes, and kissed his forehead, and crawled on.

He did not find Petros.

He found the red eagle of Taron. It was lying in the mud by the broken end of the bridge where the Gibborim had trampled it. It was torn in half. Someone had used it to wipe blood off an axe.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he left it there.

---

He swam the Athyras in the dark with one arm.

He did not remember much of it. He remembered the cold, and the current pulling at him, and his armour dragging him down, and going under once, twice, and coming up choking. He remembered thinking, quite clearly, *I should take off the lamellar*, and not being able to with one hand, and deciding it did not matter. He remembered his feet touching mud, and crawling up a bank through reeds.

The levies were gone from the near bank. The City's army had fallen back to the walls in the afternoon, when the bridge broke, and taken with them what they could. There were a few horses wandering loose in the dark, their saddles empty. He caught one. It took him a long time. He got into the saddle somehow, and lay along the horse's neck, and let it take him east along the road toward the City.

He rode all night. He did not know how. Behind him, the sky over Melantias was red with the Hunter's fires. Ahead of him, the sky over the City was dark.

The Golden Gate was shut when he reached it, an hour before dawn. Its towers were thick with men. Someone on the walls shouted at him to stop. He did not stop. He rode north along the outside of the Land Walls, past Charisios, past the new stone where he had laid courses on the morning Niketas nearly died, to the Fool's Tower.

The postern was open.

It stood open in the dark with no one guarding it, and the lane beyond it was empty. He slid off the horse. He fell. He got up, and went through the postern, and into the Tower.

---

There was blood on the stair.

He saw it in the grey light that was coming now through the arrow slits: a long smear of it, dark red, going up the steps. There was a Concord soldier lying dead on the landing below the armoury, in his red cloak, with a Hornbearer's mace buried in his helm. There was another on the next landing. There was a Hornbearer he knew, Brother Ioannes, sitting against the wall with his hands pressed over a wound in his belly, dead. There was a smell in the air he knew from the Cisterns, from the island in the black water: a smell of scorched cloth and green fire.

He went up the stair. He did not run. He could not.

At the top, in the doorway of the Abba's room, Amma Thekla was sitting on the floor.

She was holding the Abba in her lap.

The old man lay across her knees with his white head on her arm. His grey habit was burned across the chest, a great black scorch mark the shape of an open hand, and the skin underneath was blistered and black. His blind eyes were open. His chest was moving. Barely.

Kale was kneeling beside them. Her face was streaked with blood and soot. Her bow lay broken on the floor beside her.

She looked up at Stephanos in the doorway, and her face did not change.

"They came at noon," she said. Her voice was flat, like a stone. "While we were all on the walls. While you were at the bridge. A seer, and twenty Concord soldiers. Master Ioannes the silk merchant brought them in through the postern. He had a key." She stopped. "The Abba was in his room with Dora and the Amma. The brothers held the stair. Most of them died on it."

"Dora?" said Stephanos.

"Gone," said Kale. "They took her."

He stood in the doorway. The grey light came in through the window, the west window, the one that looked out toward Thrace. The sky was red there. It was always red now.

"The Abba stood in the door," said Amma Thekla. She did not look up. Her voice was very quiet. "When the seer came up the stair. He stood in the door with his arms out, the way the fool did in the Cisterns. He said: *Not this child. Not while I stand in the door.* He's seventy-four years old and blind, and he stood in the door." She stroked the old man's white hair. "The seer put his hand on him. And the Abba fell, and they went past him, and took her."

"Kallinikos was with them," said Kale. "The Master Scribe. In grey. He stayed at the bottom of the stair. He didn't come up." She looked at Stephanos. "Theophanes was with him."

He did not understand.

"Theophanes," said Kale. "He was standing beside Kallinikos in the lane, when they brought her down. With the purple book in his arms. I saw him from the wall. I was too far off to shoot." Her voice cracked for the first time. "He let them take her, Stephanos. He brought them. He told them where she'd be. And then he went with them."

Stephanos did not move.

He thought of the scribe falling through the postern on the first night, with the Concord knives behind him. He thought of Theophanes on the Paros quay with his forged writ. He thought of Theophanes in the Garden Below, fasting, grumbling. He thought of the scribe on his knees in the salt city, with the salt creeping up his legs, weeping, *Master, I'm sorry, I'll come home, I'll bring you everything.*

*She will be at the Fool's Tower on the Land Walls, with the blind Abba, when the Hunter comes. The Hornbearer will leave her there. He will ride out with the Emperor. I know him.*

He had not read those words. He did not need to. He knew them now as if he had written them himself.

"And Petros?" said Kale.

"Taken," said Stephanos. His voice sounded strange. "At the river. Or dead. I don't know."

"And the Emperor?"

"Dead."

"And the Domestikos?"

"I don't know."

"And the red eagle?"

He did not answer.

Kale looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked down at the Abba in the Amma's lap, and did not look up again.

---

Stephanos knelt in the doorway of the room at the top of the Tower.

He knelt on the stone beside the Amma, and took the Abba's thin dry hand in his left hand, and held it. It was cold. It was lighter than paper.

The old man's blind eyes moved. They did not find him. They moved in his direction, the way they always had.

"Stephanos," said the Abba.

"Abba."

"You came back." The voice was a thread, a breath. "Good. I asked you to come back."

"I came back."

"The girl," said the Abba. "They took the girl."

"Yes."

"I stood in the door. I thought I could stop them." The ghost of a smile on the old man's lips. "I was always a fool. It's the only thing I was ever good at."

"Abba." Stephanos's voice broke. "Abba, I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I left her. I rode out with the Emperor and I left her here, because I thought it was safe. Because I thought I knew. I told you I knew. I told you it was Petros. I told you..."

"Hush."

"The belt's cold, Abba. It's been cold since Tarsus. I saw the wall and I didn't believe it. She told me and I didn't believe her. She asked me to go down with her and I said no. I said she was a child. I said..." He could not go on. He bent his head over the old man's hand and wept.

The Abba's other hand came up, slowly, trembling, and found his head. It rested there, very light.

"Stephanos," said Pelagios the Fool. "My son. Listen."

He listened.

The old man's breath was very slow now. Very quiet. The grey light from the window lay on his white hair and his burned chest and his open, clouded eyes.

"Whom," said the Abba, "will the Horn anoint?"

Stephanos opened his mouth.

He did not know. He did not know. He had thought he knew, at the Mint, on Paros, in the Garden, on the mountain, in the salt city, on the Thracian road with the sun at his back and the stranger's white horse behind him. He had been sure. He had been so sure. And the Emperor was dead in the grass at Melantias, and the red eagle was in the mud, and Petros was gone, and Dora was gone, and the belt of Truth was a ring of ice around his waist, and he did not know anything at all.

He said nothing.

The Abba waited. He waited a long time. The breath went in and out of him, slower, slower, like a tide going out.

Then the old man smiled.

It was the same smile Stephanos had seen on his face a thousand times, in the room at the top of the Tower, by the window. It was the smile of someone who had asked a question and not been answered, and did not mind, because the question was the right one.

The hand on Stephanos's head grew heavier, and then it did not move.

The breath went out, and did not come back in.

Outside the window, far off to the west, a horn sounded. Then another. Then many, all along the Land Walls, from the Golden Gate to the Blachernae, one after another, the long low note of alarm. And under them, faint and deep and terrible, rising out of the west like thunder, the war-drums of Nebrodes the Hunter, coming on toward the City.

Amma Thekla closed the Abba's eyes with two fingers.

Kale bowed her head.

And Stephanos of the Horn knelt in the doorway with the old man's hand in his, and the belt of Truth cold around his waist, and the Question the old man had asked every night for thirty years hanging in the air of the little room, unanswered, as it had always been.
