## Chapter 12: The Garden Below

*Their god is their belly, and their glory is in their shame.*

*Paul, to the church at Philippi*

---

Stephanos went after him.

It was like wading against a river. The crowd on the track had closed in behind the children, dancing, singing, swaying, and every one of them had eaten the Queen's cakes, and every one of them was turned toward the golden stair with the same soft, happy, empty face. They did not see him. They did not move for him. He pushed through them with his shoulder and his elbows, and they gave way like tall grass and closed again behind him.

Petros was twenty paces ahead. Then thirty. He was taller than anyone in the crowd, and his gilded lion mask caught the torchlight, and Stephanos could see him walking steadily toward the spina with his arms loose at his sides.

"Petros!"

He did not turn.

Something caught at Stephanos's cloak. He looked down. A woman in a mask of green leaves had hold of it with both hands. She was laughing. Behind the mask, where her eyes should have been, there was nothing but more leaves, wet and green and curling, growing out of the holes.

He pulled free. She laughed louder. And all around him, suddenly, the laughter was coming from everywhere, a beat before the dancers moved, high and wild and hungry, and the masked revellers were turning toward him.

Their masks were not masks. He saw it now, in the torchlight. They had been masks once. They had been painted leather and feathers and silk, tied on with ribbons. But they had grown. The ribbons had sunk into the skin behind the ears, and the leather had fused to the faces underneath, and the painted eyes had opened, and the painted mouths had grown teeth. A man in a mask of a laughing sun reached for him with fingers that ended in thorns. A girl in a mask of a white dove danced toward him with her head on one side and a beak where her mouth should be.

"Stephanos!" It was Kale, somewhere behind him. "Their faces! Tear off the masks! It frees them!"

He did not know how she knew. He did not stop to ask. He grabbed the laughing sun by the edge of its mask and pulled.

It came away with a wet, tearing sound, like bark stripped from a green branch. Under it was a man's face, pale and sweating and bewildered, with red marks all around the edges where the mask had grown into him. The man looked at Stephanos. Then he looked down at the thorny mask in Stephanos's hand, and screamed, and sat down on the flowers of the track with his hands over his face.

The dove came at him. He tore off her mask too. Under it was a girl of fifteen, weeping.

"Keep going!" Kale shouted. She was beside him now, with her bow slung and her knife in her hand, cutting the ribbons of the masks where they had not grown in yet. "Keep going! Get him!"

He kept going.

Behind him he heard the others. He heard Sergios laughing and shouting, "Take it off! Take it off! You look ridiculous!" He heard Theophanes cry out. He heard Dora calling Petros's name, over and over, high and desperate. He heard Mother Xene praying. And somewhere under all of it, low and steady, he heard Amma Thekla singing a psalm.

The spina rose in front of him, a long wall of old stone overgrown with roses. The statue of the Queen towered over it, forty feet high, with her arms spread wide against the torchlit sky. At her feet the bronze door stood open, and the golden light came up out of the stair, and the last of the children in white were going down into it, two by two.

Petros was at the top of the stair.

The Lady Eudokia was holding his face in her hands. She was smiling up at him. She was so tall that she did not have to smile up very far. She kissed his forehead through the lion mask, gently, the way she had kissed the children.

Then she turned him around and put her hand on his back, and he went down the golden stair.

She looked up and saw Stephanos. She saw the sword in his hand and the purple on the shield under his cloak. She did not stop smiling.

"*Hornbearer,*" she said, in the voice that was inside his head. "*You're too thin. You haven't eaten all day. Come down. There's a feast.*"

Then she went down the stair too, and the bronze door began to swing shut.

He reached it with a hand's breadth to spare. He got his shield into the gap and heaved, and the door stopped, and ground, and did not close. He heard running feet behind him. Kale. Dora. Lazaros with the icon. Theophanes with blood on his face again. Sergios, with a feather mask stuck to his head. Amma Thekla, last, walking, still singing.

They went through the gap one by one. Stephanos went last. When he pulled his shield out, the door shut behind him with a boom that shook the stair.

---

The Garden Below was the most beautiful place he had ever seen, and it was the worst.

The stair went down a long way, much further than the Mint, and at the bottom it opened into a cavern so vast that he could not see its walls. There was a sun in it. That was the first thing he saw. High up in the darkness of the cavern roof, a great globe of golden light hung burning, like the sun at noon on a summer day. It filled the whole cavern with warm, heavy, honey-coloured light. It was hot. He felt it on his face like the heat of an oven.

Under it lay a garden.

Paradise, the old painters would have called it. Orchards, rank on rank, heavy with fruit. Meadows of flowers so thick and tall they came up to a man's waist. Streams of clear water winding between banks of moss. Fountains. Arbours. Little temples with white columns, half-hidden among the trees. Birds, bright as jewels, flashing between the branches. Everything was growing. Everything was blooming. Everything was ripe.

And everything was too much.

The fruit on the trees was so heavy that the branches had split under it, and the fruit went on swelling on the broken limbs, glossy and huge, and when he looked closer, he saw that it was pulsing, very slightly, like something breathing. The flowers had too many petals, and some of them had teeth, and some of them had eyes. The streams were not water. They were honey, thick and slow, and things were caught in them, small things, birds and mice and insects, struggling, drowning in sweetness. The smell was so heavy he could taste it. It was the smell of fruit gone past ripeness, the smell of a cellar full of apples in March, sweet and rotten at the same time.

"Don't eat anything," said Amma Thekla behind him. "Don't drink anything. Don't touch the flowers."

"Where are the children?" whispered Mother Xene.

Dora pointed.

In the middle of the garden, a long way off, stood a great tree. It was taller than any of the others, taller than any tree Stephanos had ever seen, with a trunk as wide as a house and branches that spread out over half the cavern. Its leaves were silver-green. Its flowers were white. And hanging from its branches, in their hundreds, were pods.

They were like the pods of a bean, or a pea, but huge, long enough to hold a child. They were pale green and translucent, and they glowed a little in the honey light. Inside each one, curled up asleep with a white garland on its head, was a child in white linen.

The children from the procession were climbing the tree. Stephanos could see them now, far off, in their white tunics, climbing up into the branches with calm, sleepy faces. When each one reached a branch, it lay down along it, and a pod opened, and closed around it like a hand.

And at the foot of the tree, on a throne of woven vines, sat the Lady Eudokia. Petros knelt at her feet with his lion mask off and his head in her lap, and she was stroking his hair.

"Oh God," said Mother Xene. "Oh God, oh God."

"They're alive," said Kale. Her voice was tight. "Most of them. The new ones. I can see... I can feel them. They're alive. They're sleeping." She stopped. "Not the ones at the top. Not the oldest pods. Those are..."

She did not finish. Stephanos looked up at the top of the great tree, where the oldest pods hung, high and dark and shrivelled like dead leaves, and he did not ask her to finish.

---

They were halfway to the tree when the Lady Eudokia lifted her head and looked at them.

She did not get up. She did not stop stroking Petros's hair. She only looked across the garden at them, with her perfect pale face and her pearl-woven hair and her gentle eyes, and smiled.

"*You came,*" she said. "*I'm so glad. You must be starving.*"

And the garden came for them.

The flowers moved first. The tall flowers on either side of the path turned their heads, all at once, the way the lily on the quay had turned to follow him, and the ones with teeth snapped. Stephanos felt one close on his leg, just above the knee, and bite through his leggings to the skin. He cut it off at the stem. It went on biting.

Then the vines. They came up out of the meadow like snakes, green and thick as a man's arm, and they wound around his legs and his waist and his sword arm, and they pulled. He fell. He hit the ground with the vines already crawling over his chest, over his shoulders, reaching for his throat. They were warm and wet and they smelled of honey.

He heard Kale shout, and heard her knife, chopping. He heard Theophanes screaming. He heard Mother Xene praying. He heard Sergios laughing and saying, "Tickles! That tickles!" He could not see any of them. The vines were over his face.

Then a light came.

It came through the vines like a sword through cloth. It was not the warm heavy honey-light of the false sun. It was clear and bright and cold, like the light of a winter morning on snow. And where it touched the vines, they shrivelled.

He pulled his face free.

Lazaros was standing in the middle of the path with the icon of the Archistrategos held up over his head in both scarred hands. The angel's painted face was blazing. It was not a trick of the light. The gold leaf of the halo was burning with a white fire, and the scarlet of the angel's cloak glowed like a coal, and the great calm eyes looked out over the garden, and every vine and every flower that the light touched was withering, curling, turning brown and falling away.

The painter's face was wet with tears. His arms were shaking. But he held it up.

"Go," he said. "Stephanos. Go. I'll hold it."

Stephanos went.

---

The Lady Eudokia rose from her throne as he came.

She was taller than he had thought. Standing, she was taller than Petros, taller than any woman or man he had ever seen. Her green gown rippled around her like leaves in a wind. She held out her arms to him, and smiled, and the voice in his head was so warm and so kind that he almost stopped.

"*Poor man,*" she said. "*Poor, poor man. Always hungry. Hungry for a banner. Hungry for a king. Hungry for an answer. I can give you all of it. Sit down, Stephanos of Taron. Sit down and eat, and be filled.*"

He was hungry. He had not eaten since yesterday. He felt it, a hollow ache under his ribs, a lightness in his head. He felt it pull at him toward her, toward the throne, toward the great tree with its sleeping children.

He said no to it. He had been saying no to it all day. It was easier than he had expected.

He raised his sword.

And Petros stood up in front of him.

Petros had risen from the Lady's feet. He stood between her and Stephanos with his arms spread, the way the Lady's were spread, and his face was calm and happy and empty, and his eyes were glass.

"No," Petros said. "You won't hurt her."

"Petros. Move."

"She loves us. She'll feed us. She said so." Petros stepped forward. "You won't hurt her. I won't let you."

He hit Stephanos in the face.

It was like being kicked by a horse. Stephanos went over backward onto the flowers with his helm ringing and blood in his mouth. Petros came after him. He was unarmed, but he did not need to be armed. He fell on Stephanos with his knees on his chest and his hands on his throat, and his face above Stephanos's was smiling.

Stephanos could have killed him. He had the sword still in his hand. He could have put it into Petros's side, under the ribs, and ended it. He knew exactly where. He had done it to men before.

He dropped the sword. He grabbed Petros's wrists and tried to break his grip. He could not. Petros was stronger than he was. Much stronger. The hands on his throat closed tighter. The honey light of the false sun began to go dark at the edges of his sight.

"*There,*" said the Lady Eudokia softly, somewhere above them. "*There. That's right. Hold him, my love. Hold him till he's quiet.*"

"Petros," said Dora.

She was standing over them. Stephanos saw her through a darkening red haze. She had torn her way through the flowers to reach them, and her arms and legs were bleeding where the teeth had caught her, and her dress was in rags. She was carrying something in her hands. A clay pot. Small and ugly and lopsided.

It was a lamp. A clay lamp, badly made, with a crooked spout and a crack down one side.

"Petros," she said again. "Look. Look what I brought. I brought it all the way from Arkadiopolis. I carried it all the way to Paros and back. Look."

Petros's hands on Stephanos's throat loosened, very slightly. His glassy eyes turned toward the lamp.

"You made it," said Dora. "Remember? When I was nine. After you broke Grandmother's. You went down to the river every day for months and dug the clay and made it yourself. It came out terrible. It leaks. I told you it was the ugliest lamp I'd ever seen." Her voice cracked. "I didn't tell you I sleep with it by my bed. I didn't tell you I brought it with me when they took you. I didn't tell you I've lit it every single night since you've been gone, and prayed."

Petros stared at the lamp.

"You're not hungry, Petros," said Dora. "You never were. You're just afraid of being nobody. That's what she's feeding. That's all." She held out the lamp to him. "You're not nobody. You're my brother. You made me a lamp."

Petros's hands fell away from Stephanos's throat.

He knelt there on the flowers, looking at the ugly clay lamp in his sister's hands, and the glass went out of his eyes. It went out slowly, like frost melting off a window. And underneath it, Stephanos saw the boy's face again, the one he had seen in the Mint, astonished and frightened and fierce. And then ashamed.

"Dora," Petros said. "Dora. What... what did I..."

"*No,*" said the Lady Eudokia.

It was not the warm voice. It was the other voice, the one underneath, and it was not warm at all.

---

The Lady Eudokia was changing.

She grew. She rose up from the flowers like a tree growing in a single breath, taller and taller, until she stood higher than the great tree's lowest branches. Her green gown became leaves, and the leaves became vines, and the vines poured down from her in a green torrent and spread out across the garden in every direction. Her pearl-woven hair became a mass of blossoms, white and red and gold. Her arms, spread wide, became branches, and fruit swelled on them, huge and glossy and pulsing. Masks hung from the branches too, hundreds of them, laughing and crying and screaming, the faces of every reveller who had ever danced in the hippodrome.

Her face stayed. It hung in the middle of all that growth, pale and perfect, smiling. But it was not the face of a woman anymore. It was the face of the statue on the spina above them. The Queen of Heaven. The Mother of Festivals.

And her mouth was opening.

It opened wider than any mouth should open. It opened until the perfect face split around it, and there was nothing left but the mouth, a vast dark hole ringed with white teeth like the teeth of the lilies. From somewhere deep inside it came a sound, low and long and endless. It was the sound of hunger. Pure hunger, with nothing else in it at all.

And from the great tree, the children began to fade.

Stephanos saw it happen. The pale green pods on the branches began to glow brighter, and the children inside them grew paler, and thinner, and the light flowed out of the pods along the branches and down the trunk and across the ground and into that vast open mouth.

"She's eating them!" Mother Xene screamed. "She's eating them!"

Kale shot her. Three arrows, one after another, straight into the mouth. The mouth swallowed them. Stephanos picked up his sword and ran at her and hacked at the vines that held her up, and they were thick as tree trunks and the sword bit into them and they bled honey and closed up again. He hacked again. The vines closed again.

The children were fading.

"The fast," gasped Amma Thekla. She had come up beside him, somehow, through the garden, with her hands empty and her face grey. "She's hunger. That's all she is now. Nothing else left. She can only eat what's offered. What's hungry." The old woman looked at him with her fierce black eyes. "We have fasted. We are not offering. The children are asleep. They can't say no. Wake them up."

"How?"

Thekla looked at Lazaros.

The painter was standing at the edge of the meadow with the blazing icon over his head. He had followed Stephanos in, step by step, burning a path through the vines. His arms were shaking so badly that the icon trembled. His face was white. His scarred hands were blistering again, Stephanos saw, where they touched the frame, as if the icon were hot.

Lazaros looked at the Mother of Festivals. He looked at the great open mouth, and the pale perfect face split around it that had once been the face of the Lady Eudokia, who had held his icon over a brazier and made him save it with his hands.

He walked toward her.

He walked straight toward her, through the vines, holding the icon up, and the vines burned away in front of him. He walked into the reach of her branches, and they did not touch him. He walked right up to the edge of that vast mouth, and stood there, small and thin and shaking, with the angel blazing over his head.

Then he turned around and held the icon up toward the tree. Toward the children.

The light of it poured into the branches. It poured over the pods, clear and cold and bright as a winter morning, and it went through them, and touched the sleeping children inside.

And the children woke.

Stephanos saw their eyes open. All at once, all over the great tree, hundreds of them. He saw them look around at the pods that held them, and at the branches, and at the terrible false sun above, and he heard the first one scream. Then another. Then all of them, all over the tree, screaming and crying and tearing at the pods with their small hands, calling for their mothers, calling for Mother Xene, calling for God.

They were not asleep anymore. They were not offering. They were terrified, and they were fighting, and they were saying no.

The flow of light from the pods to the mouth stopped.

The Mother of Festivals shrieked. It was a sound like a tree splitting in a storm. Her branches thrashed. Her fruit burst, and rotten juice rained down over the garden. The masks on her branches were howling. And the pods on the great tree began to split open, one after another, and the children tumbled out and fell into the soft flowers below, and got up, and ran.

The vines holding the Mother of Festivals up began to wither.

Stephanos cut them. They did not close again. He cut through one and then another, and Kale was beside him with her knife, and Petros was on the other side, tearing at them with his bare hands. The great green body sagged. The branches drooped. The fruit fell and rotted where it lay, and the smell was terrible.

The false sun overhead flickered, and dimmed, and went out.

---

In the darkness, when Lazaros's icon was the only light left in the Garden Below, they found the Lady Eudokia.

She was lying in the middle of the rotting heap of vines and fruit and masks that had been the Mother of Festivals. She was very small. Smaller than Dora. She was old, ancient, older than Amma Thekla, with a face like a dried apple and thin white hair and hands like claws. She was naked, and her body was so thin that every bone showed through the skin. She was clawing weakly at the rotten fruit around her, trying to bring it to her mouth.

"Hungry," she whispered. "So hungry. So hungry."

Lazaros knelt beside her.

He had put the icon down. He knelt in the rot with his blistered hands on his knees and looked at her. She did not seem to know him. She did not seem to see him at all.

"Lady Eudokia," he said gently. "It's Lazaros. The painter. From Panselinos's workshop."

The old woman's eyes flickered toward him, and away.

"Hungry," she said.

"I know." Lazaros's voice was very quiet. "Christ has mercy for you. Even now. Even for this. He fed five thousand once on the hillside with five loaves. He said he was the bread of life. He said whoever comes to him will never be hungry." He held out his hand to her, the scarred hand. "Do you want it? Do you want to be filled?"

The old woman looked at his hand.

Then she looked at the rotten fruit in her claws. She looked at it for a long time.

"No," she said at last. "No. I want this."

She put the rotten fruit in her mouth, and chewed, and swallowed. Her eyes closed. And she did not open them again.

Lazaros knelt beside her for a long time with his hand still held out. Then he lowered it. He was crying, silently. Kale came and knelt beside him and put her arm around his shoulders, and he let her.

---

The chapel was under the great tree.

Kale found it, as she found everything. There had been a church here once, a small one, when the hippodrome above was new; the Lady Eudokia had buried it and grown her tree through its roof. Its walls were split by roots. Its floor was broken. But the apse still stood, and in the apse, behind where the altar had been, was a niche.

Mother Xene carried the orphans' candles in her pockets. She always did, she said. She lit one and gave it to Lazaros, and Lazaros set it in the niche, and the letters came up out of the white plaster the way they had on Paros, black and sharp and old.

*It went down with the lamps, and it keeps the door.*

Theophanes wrote it down beside the first, on the back page of the purple book, with a burnt twig for a pen. His hands were shaking. He had fasted too, all day, grumbling; he had not eaten a cake. Stephanos had not noticed until now.

"Where the proud one stood, the Horn lay down," Theophanes read aloud. "It went down with the lamps, and it keeps the door." He looked up. "It went down. It went down, Stephanos. The Horn didn't go somewhere. It went down. Into the ground. With the lamps."

"What door?"

"I don't know." Theophanes stared at the words. "The Tagma always said it vanished on the night of the statue. From the sanctuary of the Great Church. What if it didn't vanish? What if it went down? Under the church?"

Stephanos thought of the crack in the sanctuary floor, sealed with the icon of Michael, and the backward singing that Lazaros had heard through it at night. He thought of the empty tomb of Andronikos the Proud, full of forged letters.

*Where the proud one stood.*

"Two words," said Amma Thekla. She was sitting on a broken pillar in the ruined chapel, and for the first time since Stephanos had known her, she was not weaving. Her hands lay still in her lap. She looked very old and very tired. "Two words aren't enough. You'll want to go back to the City now and start digging. Don't. Two words will take you to the door. They won't open it."

---

They climbed out of the Garden Below at dawn, with three hundred and eleven children.

Mother Xene counted them. She stood at the top of the golden stair in the pale grey light, in the ruin of the hippodrome, and counted them as they came up past her one by one, white-faced and weeping and alive, and she touched each one on the head as it passed. Three hundred and eleven. From this year and last year and the year before. Not from the years before that. She did not say anything about that, and neither did anyone else.

The statue of the Queen of Heaven lay broken on the spina. Nobody had broken it. It had cracked from the base up, in the night, as the Mother of Festivals died below, and fallen, and its face lay on the track in the flowers.

The flowers were dying. All over the hippodrome, all over the city as far as Stephanos could see, the too-ripe fruit was falling at last from the trees and rotting on the ground, and the flowers with teeth were wilting, and the vines on the walls were turning brown. It smelled terrible. It would smell terrible for weeks.

The people of Thessalonike stood in the streets and in the ruins of the hippodrome and watched their children come up out of the ground.

Nobody cheered. Most of them were crying.

Petros came up last. He had carried the smallest children up the stair two at a time, over and over, until there were none left. He stood at the top now with his hands hanging at his sides, and he did not look at anyone. His face was grey. There were bruises on his knuckles from Stephanos's face.

Stephanos went to him.

"It wasn't your fault," he said.

Petros did not look at him.

"It was the cake," said Stephanos. "The Queen's charm. You hadn't fasted. You didn't know. A man who's never fasted can't be expected to..."

"I almost killed you," said Petros. "I had my hands on your throat. I wanted to." He looked at his hands. "Dora had to save me. My little sister had to bring me a lamp. Like I was a baby."

"You carried the children up. All of them."

"That doesn't..." Petros stopped. He looked at Stephanos, and for a moment his face was naked. "Why are you being kind to me? I should have fasted. The Amma told me to. I thought I was too big for it. I thought I was..." He shut his mouth.

"You're the lame lord's great-grandson," said Stephanos. "The Shepherd's blood is in you. The Tower hunted you first. You're going to be a great man, Petros. One night doesn't change that."

Petros stared at him. Then he turned and walked away down the ruined track of the hippodrome, among the dying flowers, alone.

Stephanos watched him go. When he turned around, Amma Thekla was standing behind him, leaning on Kale's arm. The old woman was looking at him with an expression he could not read.

"You're protecting the wrong one," she said.

"Amma?"

But she had already turned away, and Kale was helping her down the steps of the spina toward the orphanage, and she did not look back.

---

Among the rot at the foot of the dead tree, before they left, Lazaros had found a shrine. It was a small one, a niche in a root, where the Lady Eudokia had kept her treasures. Most of them were rubbish: gold rings, silver cups, perfume jars, the kind of thing any rich woman keeps. But at the back of the niche, folded in a silk bag, was a veil.

It was plain linen, very old, yellowed and soft as a cobweb. It smelled of myrrh.

"The veil of the Myrrh-bearer," said Amma Thekla, when Lazaros showed it to her. She touched it with one finger, and closed her eyes. "One of the women who went to the tomb on the first day of the week. With spices, for a corpse. She went expecting death, and came away with news for the whole world." She opened her eyes. "The Lady Eudokia stole it from the church of Saint Demetrios. It will have galled her to keep it. No charm can hold the one who wears it."

"Then Petros should wear it," said Stephanos.

The old woman looked at him. Then she took the veil from Lazaros, and turned, and put it over Dora's head.

It settled on the girl's dark curls as if it had been made for her. Dora put up a hand and touched it, surprised.

"And the dead speak true to her," said Amma Thekla. "That's the other thing it does. You'll need that, I think, before the end." She turned back to Stephanos. "Your heir has a lamp to remind him. That's enough for him."

Stephanos did not argue. But that night, lying awake in the empty dormitory of Saint Anysia with three hundred children breathing in the rooms around him, he thought about it for a long time, and he did not understand it, and it made him angry, and he did not know why.
