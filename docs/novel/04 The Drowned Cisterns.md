## Chapter 4: The Drowned Cisterns

*Scribes lie. Parchment remembers.*

*A saying of the Grammatikoi*

---

Amma Thekla stayed in the crypt with the bound dead, sitting on the floor with her back against the Shepherd's tomb and her basket in her lap. She gave Stephanos a lamp and a small clay flask stoppered with wax.

"Holy water," she said. "From the Jordan, the Patriarch says, though I think he says that about all of it. Use it on the seer, not on the scribes. The scribes are only men."

"And if the seer comes back this way?"

"Then he'll find an old woman praying." Her fingers wove the palm leaf, over, under, over. "Seers don't like old women praying. They never have."

So there were five of them on the stair down to the cisterns. Theophanes led, with a lamp. Stephanos came behind him, then Lazaros, then Kale with an arrow on the string, and Sergios last of all, humming to himself and trailing one hand along the wall as if he were stroking a cat.

The stair was very old. The steps were cut in the rock and polished smooth by water, and they went down in a long spiral, around and around, until Stephanos lost count of the turns. The chanting grew louder as they went. The air grew colder and wetter. Moisture beaded on the rock and ran down it in threads and dripped from the ceiling onto their heads.

"Why backward?" Lazaros asked quietly.

"It's a binding," Theophanes said, without turning. "The seer taught it to the Master. The liturgy is the strongest prayer the Church has. Sung forward, it opens a door to heaven. Sung backward, it opens a door to... somewhere else." He hesitated. "It lets the seer draw power from the dead in the crypts. And from the water. There's something in the water."

"What kind of something?"

"I never saw it. I heard it. At night, when we were copying. It moves between the columns." Theophanes's lamp shook a little. "The Master said it was a guardian. He said it was harmless as long as we kept the lamps lit on the island."

"And you believed him?"

"I believed everything he said for eleven years," said Theophanes. "It's a habit. I'm trying to break it."

---

The stair came out onto a ledge of rock, and they stopped, and for a long moment none of them said anything.

The Drowned Cisterns of the City had been built by the emperors of old to hold water for a siege: a whole underground lake, roofed with brick vaults and held up by columns. Stephanos had heard the number of the columns once. He had not believed it. Now, standing on the ledge with his lamp raised, he believed it.

They went away in every direction, row after row after row, as far as the light could reach and further. Each one was as thick as a man's body and three times his height, and they stood in black water that lay perfectly still, so still that each column had a twin beneath it, hanging upside down in the dark. The capitals were carved with acanthus leaves, and some with faces. The vaults between them were lost in shadow. The sound of dripping came from everywhere at once, a slow, endless music, like a clock with a thousand hands.

Far off in the forest of columns, a light burned. It was yellow and steady, a cluster of lamps, and it lay low on the water as if it were floating. The backward chanting came from it.

"The island," said Theophanes. "There's a causeway. It's under the water, about knee deep. You have to know where it runs, or you'll step off and drown."

"Do you know where it runs?"

"I've walked it every day for two years." He looked at the black water and swallowed. "In the light. With the lamps lit along it."

"The lamps aren't lit."

"No," said Theophanes. "They're not."

Stephanos looked out across the water. He could see where the causeway must be: a line of short stone posts sticking up from the water between the columns, each with an iron cup at the top where a lamp should be. The cups were dark. The line of posts ran away from the ledge and curved out of sight between the columns toward the island.

"They knew we were coming," said Kale.

"Or they always put them out when the seer is working," said Theophanes. "I don't know. I never stayed to watch."

Stephanos made up his mind.

"We go along the causeway," he said. "Theophanes leads. I follow him, with a hand on his shoulder. Lazaros holds on to me. Kale, you hold on to Lazaros, and you keep an arrow ready. Sergios..."

He turned. The fool was not on the ledge.

"Sergios?"

A splash, far out in the water. They all turned toward it. Out between the columns, a ragged shape was wading along the causeway, knee deep in the black water, with his arms spread wide for balance and his head thrown back. He was singing. It took Stephanos a moment to recognise the tune. It was a drinking song from the Harbour taverns, about a sailor who fell in love with a mermaid and drowned.

"Sergios!" he hissed.

The fool waved without turning around, and went on singing.

"Well," said Kale. "I suppose we know where the causeway goes."

---

They waded out after him.

The water was so cold it hurt. It closed around Stephanos's legs like iron bands and soaked through his boots and his leggings in a heartbeat, and within a dozen steps he could not feel his feet. The causeway underneath was slick with weed. Twice he felt his boot slide toward the edge, and twice Theophanes's shoulder under his hand held him steady. The lamps made shivering gold roads across the water. The columns went by on either side, huge and silent, and their reflections went by beneath.

Halfway to the island, Kale said, "Stop."

They stopped.

"Something's under us," she said. "Something big. It's moving."

Stephanos looked down. The black water around his knees was opaque as ink. He could see nothing in it. But he felt it now, a pressure, a slow push of water against his legs, as if something very large had passed close by beneath the surface and was turning around.

"Keep walking," he said. "Slowly."

They walked. Ahead of them, the fool had stopped singing. He was standing in the water, very still, looking down.

The water beside the causeway bulged, and broke.

It came up between two columns in a long, smooth rush, water pouring off it in sheets: a head as big as a cart, pale and blind, with no eyes at all, only smooth white skin stretched over bone, and a mouth that opened and opened and went on opening. Its teeth were long and thin and translucent as glass. It had no face, only that mouth, and it swung toward Sergios on a neck as thick as a column.

"Get down!" Stephanos shouted.

The fool did not get down. He looked up at the thing and laughed out loud.

"Oh, you poor thing!" he said. "You poor ugly thing! Nobody's fed you, have they? Nobody's fed you in a hundred years!"

He reached into his rags and pulled out something, and threw it. It struck the creature on its white snout and fell into the water. Stephanos saw what it was as it fell. It was the heel of a loaf of bread.

The creature stopped.

It swung its blind head down toward the water where the bread had fallen, and snuffled. Its great mouth closed. Somewhere below the surface, Stephanos heard a sound like a millstone grinding. Then the head came up again and turned toward the fool, and it was not hunting now. It was waiting.

"More?" said Sergios. "I don't have more. I'm a poor man. But they have lots of bread on the island. Lots. They've been eating fresh bread every day and giving you none, haven't they? And singing their nasty songs at you." He pointed toward the island, toward the lights and the backward chanting. "Go and get it. Go on."

The creature stayed where it was a moment longer, water pouring from its jaws. Then it turned and slid away beneath the surface, and a long ripple ran away between the columns toward the island, and was gone.

Stephanos found he had been holding his breath. He let it out.

"How did you know it would do that?" he asked.

The fool turned and looked at him. For a moment his eyes were very clear, clear and sad and entirely sane.

"It's only a hungry thing someone chained in the dark," Sergios said. "They're all like that, mostly. Even the big ones." Then he grinned and hopped on one foot in the water and splashed Lazaros. "Come on! Come on! There'll be fighting now! I love fighting!"

---

The island was a raised platform of stone in the middle of the cistern, where six columns stood closer together than the rest. Someone had roofed the space between them with planks and hung lamps from the capitals. Under the roof stood long tables covered with parchment, and jars of ink, and racks of quills, and wax tablets, and seals. There were stools along the tables. There were cots against the columns. There was a brazier with a kettle on it, and a sack of bread, and a string of onions hanging from a nail.

It looked like a schoolroom. Stephanos had expected something else, and it took him a moment to understand what he was seeing.

Then the screaming started.

The creature had come up out of the water at the far side of the island, where the planks hung low over the edge, and it had its mouth around the bread sack. Men in grey robes were running in every direction, knocking over stools, scattering parchment. Two of them had fallen into the water. One of them was trying to climb a column. The backward chanting had stopped.

In the middle of the island, beside the brazier, a figure stood still.

It was taller than a man, and thin, and it wore a long robe of dull black stuff covered in writing, row after row of small, tight characters in a script Stephanos did not know. Its face was hidden under a hood. It held a staff of dark wood in one hand, and the other hand was raised toward the creature in the water, fingers spread, and a greenish light was gathering around the fingers like marsh fire.

"The seer," said Theophanes. His voice cracked.

The Nephilim seer turned its head toward them.

Stephanos saw its face under the hood: long and narrow and beautiful in a way that made his stomach turn, with skin the colour of old ivory and eyes that were entirely black, no white in them at all. It smiled at him. Its teeth were very small and very even.

"Hornbearer," it said. Its voice was the voice that had spoken out of the dead emperor's mouth. "And Theophanes. Little Theophanes. The Master will be so disappointed."

It flicked its fingers at them.

The green light leaped across the island like a thrown spear. Stephanos threw up his shield, and the light struck it, and the shield went cold in his hand, so cold that his arm was numb to the shoulder. He heard Lazaros cry out behind him. He smelled burning.

"Kale!"

Two arrows went past him. The seer moved its staff, and both arrows broke in the air a yard from its face, as if they had struck a wall of glass, and fell into the water in pieces.

It laughed.

Stephanos ran at it. He did not think. He had the holy water in his left hand and his sword in his right, and he ran across the island between the tables with parchment flying up around his feet, and the seer turned its black eyes on him and lifted its hand again. He saw the green fire gathering. He knew he would not reach it in time.

Sergios stepped in front of him.

The fool did not do anything. He stood there, in his rags, with his arms at his sides, between Stephanos and the seer, and looked up at the tall figure in its robe of writing, and the green fire struck him full in the chest.

And went out.

It did not bounce off. It did not burn. It went out, the way a candle goes out when you close your fingers over the flame, and the fool stood there unharmed and blinked.

"What is that?" said Sergios, peering at the seer's robe. "Is that writing? Is it all over you? What does it say?" He leaned closer. "Does it say, I am very clever and nobody loves me? It looks like it says that."

The seer stared at him. For the first time its beautiful face showed something other than contempt. It looked bewildered.

That was enough. Stephanos went past the fool and threw the flask.

It broke on the seer's chest. The holy water splashed over the black robe and the rows of tiny writing, and where it touched them, the writing smoked. The seer screamed. It was a terrible sound, high and thin and inhuman, and it went on and on as the smoke rose, and the seer tore at its robe with both hands. Its staff fell. Its hood fell back. Stephanos saw that its head was bald and its skull was long and ridged, and that its ears were pointed like a bat's.

He hit it with the flat of his sword across the side of the head, as hard as he could.

It went down. It lay on the planks among the scattered parchment, twitching, smoke curling up from its robe. Then it rolled, faster than he could follow, and was over the edge of the island and into the black water, and gone.

A long ripple ran away between the columns. Stephanos stood on the edge of the island with his sword in his hand and his arm numb, breathing hard, watching the ripple fade. Far off, in the dark, the blind creature lifted its head from the water and looked in the same direction. Then it sank again.

"He'll be back," Kale said beside him. She had another arrow on the string. "Things like that always come back."

"I know."

---

They rounded up the scribes. There were nine of them, shivering and wet, in grey robes. Two of them had fallen into the water and been pulled out by Lazaros, whose scarred hands were blistered now where the seer's fire had struck him. None of them fought. They stood in a huddle by the brazier and looked at Stephanos with frightened eyes, and one of them, a boy of fifteen or so, cried silently the whole time with his hands over his face.

"Where's Kallinikos?" Theophanes demanded. "Where's the Master?"

Nobody answered. Theophanes grabbed the nearest scribe by the front of his robe and shook him. "Where is he, Basil? You know. You always know."

"Gone," said the scribe called Basil. He was older, forty perhaps, with a round, anxious face. "Gone, Theophanes. An hour ago. The seer told him you were coming. He took the boat and the east passage. He's out of the City by now."

"What did he take?"

"His books. The Chamber's seals. The ledger." Basil hesitated. "And the letter."

"What letter?"

Basil licked his lips. "The one from Thrace. The one he was so excited about. He had us copy it six times. He sent two copies east by the Tower courier last night." His eyes went to the tables. "The original's there. In the green box. He said to burn it if anyone came. We didn't have time."

Theophanes let go of him and went to the tables. He pushed aside parchment and ink jars until he found a small box of green-painted wood, and he opened it, and stood looking at what was inside.

Then he turned around. His face had gone completely white.

"Stephanos," he said. "You need to see this."

---

It was an old letter, folded in three, the parchment yellow and soft at the creases. The seal had been broken and carefully reattached, and the wax was dark red, stamped with a device Stephanos did not recognise at first: a shepherd's crook crossed with a sceptre, and above them both, very small, a horn.

He unfolded it. The writing inside was cramped and shaky, the hand of an old man or a sick one, and the ink had faded to brown.

*To the Knights of the Horn, at the Tower on the Land Walls.*

*The fool who asked me the question will know this hand. Tell him that the lame man of Arkadiopolis sends him greeting from the far side of death, and thanks him again for asking.*

*I have given this letter to a trader who travels the Thracian roads. He will carry it for twenty years without remembering it, and on the day he remembers, he will bring it to you. I do not know how I know this. I know it the way I knew the fool would come back.*

*Symeon's last word to the Shepherd was split in four, for safekeeping, and hidden in the four themes that would fall. When the four are found and read together, they will tell where the Horn lies waiting.*

*But the Horn does not want a place. It wants a person. Come to the ford below the ruined church at Arkadiopolis, at the first frost after this letter reaches you, an hour before dawn. Someone of my house will be there. Come quickly. Others will be looking.*

*Ask the question.*

The letter was not signed. At the bottom, in the same shaking hand, someone had drawn a small picture: a sheep, and beside it, a sling.

Stephanos read it three times. His hands were shaking, and he could not make them stop.

"Is it real?" he said. He did not look up. "Theophanes. You forge letters. You know hands. Is this real?"

Theophanes took it from him. He held it to the lamp and turned it and tilted it. He smelled the ink. He ran his thumb over the seal, and held the parchment up to the light to see the grain, and looked for a long time at the shaky brown letters.

"The parchment is Thracian," he said at last. "Sheepskin, badly scraped, the kind they make in the hill monasteries. It's at least twenty years old; the ink has sunk into the grain. The seal is old wax and the device is carved by hand, not cast. The hand..." He hesitated. "The hand is a man who learned his letters from a monk and wrote very little afterward. The strokes are unsteady, but they're not forced. A forger's hand shakes when he's copying. This hand shakes because it's old." He lowered the letter. "I can't be certain. Nobody could be certain. But if this is a forgery, it's better than anything I've ever made, and I'm the best there is."

"And Kallinikos has read it," said Kale.

"Kallinikos has read it and copied it and sent it east," said Theophanes. "To the Chamber of Seers. To Nebrodes." He laughed, and this time it was not thin or cracked. It was hollow. "Twenty years, the lame man says. Twenty years this letter waited to reach the Tagma, and when it finally came, it came through the City post. And the Master opened it. Because he opens everything."

Stephanos took the letter back and folded it and put it inside his breastplate, against the word Justice, where he could feel it.

"When is the first frost in Thrace?" he asked.

Kale answered him. "Soon," she said. "It's late in the year. A week. Perhaps two."

"And how far is Arkadiopolis?"

"Four days' hard riding from the Golden Gate. Five, if the roads are bad." She met his eyes. "The Tower courier left last night. With a good horse and a change at every post station, a courier could be in the east in a week. The seers would have to send word back west to their own people. And then their people would have to ride to Thrace."

"So we have time."

"We might have time," said Kale. "Or they might have people in Thrace already. The Gibborim are on the western roads. That's why I came."

Stephanos stood on the island in the middle of the Drowned Cisterns with nine wet scribes shivering behind him and the letter against his heart, and he felt something he had not felt in nine years of vows and stone-laying and asking the Question at every shrine on every road. It was so strong and so sudden that for a moment he could not breathe.

It was hope. And close behind it, like a hound behind a hare, came fear that someone else would get there first.

"We go up," he said. "Now. We take the scribes to the Eparch and the letters to the council. And then I'm going to Thrace."

He turned and looked out across the black water. Far off between the columns, something pale moved and sank, and a ripple spread out across the cistern toward them, and broke against the island with a small, lapping sound, like a tongue.

"If the Domestikos lets you," said Theophanes quietly.

Stephanos did not answer.
